The note leaves a lot of freedom: what the regulariser is, how small "too small" is, what a cluster is, what counts as recovering a covariance. Every chapter had to fix these somehow. This chapter lists what I chose, what else I could have chosen, and how much it would matter, so you can tell which conclusions are about your idea and which are about my engineering.

Decisions, defaults and alternatives

Defaults throughout unless a chapter says otherwise: $\lambda = \eta = 1$, $\gamma = 1$, $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$, $W_{IB} \sim \mathrm{Exp}(0.2)$, $W_{II} \sim U(0, 0.03)$, mean-field, Euler steps of $dt = 0.05$ (in units of $1/\lambda$), run until the residual $\max|dw/dt| < 10^{-5}$. The live views use the same $dt$ as the sweeps. Their own defaults: Three inputs $N = 100$, $\theta = 0.4$ (its tours: $N = 90$, lateral weights starting at the floor); Many inputs 24 inputs in 4 blocks, $N = 150$, $\theta = 0.3$; Homeostasis $N = 150$, $\theta = 0.4$, the floor set as $\omega = N w_{\min} = 0.02$, lateral weights starting at the floor; the ring in Rectified neurons $N = 96$, $w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$, lateral weights at the floor. The one exception to $dt = 0.05$ is the precomputed MNIST runs: minibatches of 256, $dt = 0.1$, 3,000 steps.

What recovering $\Sigma_g$ says about $p(\text{stimulus})$

The note argues that for a rich enough lift $g = \phi(x)$ the covariance of $g$ determines $p(x)$. For uncentred second moments and a characteristic kernel that is right, for a stronger reason: the mean embedding $\mathbb E\,\phi(x)$ already determines $p$, and second moments are the mean embedding under the product kernel $k^2$, which is again characteristic for a Gaussian $k$. With random Fourier features this becomes concrete: since $\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) + \cos(a+b)]$, the second moments of features at frequencies $\omega_i, \omega_j$ are the characteristic function of $p$ sampled at $\omega_i \pm \omega_j$.

Hebb, though, sees the centred covariance, and that does not determine $p$, even if it is recovered perfectly. Take a mixture of two atoms, $x = a$ with probability $p$ and $x = b$ otherwise. For any feature map, $\mathrm{Cov}\,\phi(x) = p(1-p)\,dd^\top$ with $d = \phi(a) - \phi(b)$. So mixtures with weights $p$ and $1 - p$ have the same covariance, any two weights give covariances that differ only by the scalar $p(1-p)$, and without the mean $\mathbb E\,\phi(x)$ the atoms' locations are lost; only their difference survives. Recovering the covariance and recovering the distribution are separate problems. The second needs the mean, which no synapse stores, and further assumptions.

Three more caveats keep "we recovered $\Sigma_g$" from meaning "we recovered $p$". First, with $D$ random features nothing is exactly characteristic; you get finitely many Fourier samples. Second, equilibrium weights store only a parts-sketch of $\Sigma_g$, so even a perfect lift would be read through a bottleneck of rank at most $K + 1$. Third, turning second moments into samples needs an explicit generative assumption (for instance, Gaussian in feature space followed by a pre-image step), and the samples inherit that assumption. With the pixel lift (a linear kernel), $\Sigma_g$ is the pixel covariance and determines $p$ only if $p$ is Gaussian.

Where the note and this page part ways

  • The sign. The note's displayed rule has a minus sign in front of $\langle h_i, h_j\rangle_{\Sigma_g}$, which would be anti-Hebbian; the sentence after it ("we cut the connection between two neurons to the extent that their harmonic embeddings misalign") wants a plus, and that is what I use.
  • The regulariser's constant must be negative. With $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$ and $w_0 \ge 0$, any $\Sigma_g$ without negative entries gives one cluster. Clusters need $w_0 = -\eta\theta < 0$ (or anticorrelated inputs, which is the same thing), and "stops weights getting too small" becomes a separate hard floor.
  • Clusters are parts, not eigenmodes. Non-negative Perron vectors of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a support; eigenvectors only when the top eigenvector happens to be non-negative.
  • The knobs are $\theta$ and $\gamma$, not "modes" and "functions per mode". $\theta$ sets which parts exist, lateral plasticity sets how many are resolved, $\lambda$ only sets the scale, and nothing sets the number of functions per mode.
  • No spectral filter at equilibrium. Any allocation among compatible parts is a fixed point (zero-floor limit; a finite floor adds a weak $O(\omega/c)$ dependence); the filter $n_k \propto \lambda_k^\beta$ comes from the transient, and on the tested families it is steep, not proportional.
  • Noise does not rescue it. Private noise makes the allocation a law of the parameters, but in the tested families that law is nearly winner-take-all wherever parts stay clean; a graded filter needs many neurons in transit between parts. Multiplicative noise with a small floor never lets a pruned synapse regrow.
  • No reservoir. With dense wiring, cluster members ended as exact clones in every condition tested; more neurons give more copies, not more surface area. Private noise adds jitter, not diversity.
  • The readout is a least-squares problem on active edges, not $C = H^+RH^{+\top}$; it recovers only the populated parts, up to scale and a $\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ shift.
  • Not only second moments. If plasticity sees rectified rates rather than voltages, the rule reads moments beyond the second without any lift: on digits it finds a heavy-tailed part that a Gaussian control does not. It still builds one cluster of clones there, so it is not a better code.
  • Covariance is not the distribution. Even a perfect readout of the centred covariance leaves $p(\text{stimulus})$ undetermined without the mean (above).

Nonlinearity, three ways

"Add a nonlinearity" can mean three different things here, and only one of them keeps the note's geometry.

  1. Rectify the voltages of a circuit with non-negative inputs. This does nothing. If $g \ge 0$ (firing rates), every $u_i = h_i^\top g$ is a convex combination of non-negative numbers, so $\mathrm{relu}(u) = u$ by the maximum principle. A rectifier only bites with a threshold.
  2. Put the nonlinearity inside the circuit (nonlinear conductances or nodes). Then $u$ is no longer $Hg$ for a fixed $H$: each neuron's "embedding" depends on the stimulus, and the whole construction (embeddings as functions of the weights alone, the closed ODE, the readout) is gone. That is a different project.
  3. Keep the circuit linear and rectify the output that plasticity sees. Voltages are still $u = Hg$ and the embeddings are unchanged; the rule sees rates $r_i = \mathrm{relu}(u_i - \text{threshold})$. For Gaussian inputs, $\mathrm{Cov}(r_i, r_j)$ is a dual activation (the arc-cosine kernel) of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$ and the two variances, so the inner product of embeddings is replaced by a nonlinear function of it. That is the Rectified neurons chapter. For non-Gaussian inputs the rates carry moments beyond the second, and on digits that changes the part the rule finds (the Gaussian control).

Caveats, and what was not done

  • A tiny residual does not certify stability. An exactly symmetric state can sit on an unstable fixed point with residual near zero (the hierarchical "half" part persisted well past where it is unstable). Private multiplicative jitter of about $10^{-3}$ per step exposes it; with jitter, the $\gamma = 0$ hysteresis loops nearly close, while the $\gamma = 1$ memory survives.
  • The dependence of the extinction threshold on $\omega = Nw_{\min}$ is empirical; I have no derivation.
  • The coarse-to-fine gating rule $\theta_c = \gamma\kappa_0$ is an init-dependent heuristic crossover (the expected initial lateral drift), not a derived phase boundary. It predicts every tested transition on block and hierarchical covariances within 0.03–0.08, but assumes independent, exchangeable starting embeddings and was not tested quantitatively on power-law spectra. The packing inequality holds when the edges between clusters are clipped.
  • Proved versus observed. The part equation, the shift identity and the stability test (sufficient, for the nondegenerate case) are derived. That cluster members become clones, the power-law and Boltzmann allocation fits, and $\theta$ dropping out under input noise are empirical, on the tested families and three thresholds. The lone neuron's Rayleigh quotient is a Lyapunov function only at zero floor.
  • Private noise covers few families: a diagonal 6-input covariance, the planted 4 × 3 blocks, and two 4-block covariances at $N = 120$ (2 seeds). There is no derivation of $\beta(\sigma)$, and no private-noise model was found that keeps parts clean (under 5% in transit) and grades the allocation; none was ruled out. Multiplicative noise with a large floor was tried only without lateral synapses.
  • Rectified runs: the fast-homeostasis threshold uses the exact population moments of $u_i$ (a running estimate was not studied), and the MNIST runs sit at the minibatch noise floor (residual 0.07–0.34), not the mean-field $10^{-5}$. On MNIST the $\theta$ sweep never spans "one cluster → many"; a different initial weighting or a smaller leak might, and was not run.
  • The exact non-equilibrium readout (using the known $dw/dt$) is in the engine but was not evaluated in the sweeps.
  • Stacked layers were not run. A purely linear stack cannot add anything: $u_2 = H_2u_1 = H_2H_1g$, so a second-layer neuron's embedding over the inputs is a convex combination of first-layer embeddings, and a clustered first layer hands the second a $K$-dimensional input. New features need a nonlinearity between layers. With rectified rates, raw MNIST gives one cluster of clones (one effective function), but on a synthetic ring rectification keeps 7–9 parts populated where the linear rule keeps 1, so whether depth helps is still open.
  • Everything outside MNIST uses synthetic covariances (blocks, hierarchies, rings, power laws); CIFAR was not tried.
