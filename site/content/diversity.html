Appendix. The details behind the claim in Nonlinear neurons that members of a cluster differ little as functions: the one-mode experiment and what the population modes are.

The note pictured each cluster as a reservoir: many neurons tuned to one eigenmode of the input, each computing a different random function of it, so that a downstream reader can find the feature it needs. It also took clusters and eigenmodes to be the same objects. This chapter tests both pictures. The first part asks whether the members of a cluster compute different functions now that every neuron has an on-site potential of its own. With an experiment built so that nothing else can masquerade as diversity, the answer is: barely, and the reason is structural. The second part asks what the population's own modes are, and when they coincide with clusters.

Do the members of a cluster compute different functions?

The experiment

To ask whether a cluster holds many functions of one mode, give the network exactly one mode. Every input carries the same scalar,

$$g = z\,v, \qquad z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),$$

with $v$ a fixed positive vector (12 entries evenly spaced from 0.5 to 1.5, rescaled to mean square 1). Neuron $i$ then receives the input current $z\,(a_i\cdot v)$, and because the equilibrium is unique, its response is a function of $z$ alone, $F_i = u_i(z)$. Whatever the members of a cluster differ in, they differ as functions of one variable: precisely the note's "several features of one mode".

Three arms share the attachments $a_{ib}$ (each neuron on $s = 3$ random inputs, total conductance 1), both input banks and the learning rule. They differ only in the on-site potential $V_i(u) = \tfrac{k_{lo}}{2}(u - t_i)_-^2 + \tfrac{k_{hi}}{2}(u - t_i)_+^2$:

  • heterogeneous: a leaky rectifier, $k_{lo} = 3$, $k_{hi} = 0$, with thresholds $t_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_t^2)$, $\sigma_t = 0.7$. Below its threshold a neuron's gain is $d_i/(d_i + 3)$ times its gain above, with $d_i = 1 + \sum_j w_{ij}$: a quarter in the uncoupled state, and closer to one as fusion raises $d_i$;
  • homogeneous: the same potential with every $t_i = 0$;
  • linear: $V_i = 0$.

Two quantities are kept apart, because a neuron can be strongly nonlinear while its cluster still holds only one function. Both are held-out relative errors: the mean squared residual of a least-squares fit, divided by the variance of the response, fitted on one half of an independent evaluation bank and scored on the other.

  • Nonlinearity of a neuron, $\varepsilon_{\rm aff}$: the error of the best affine fit of $F_i$ from the full input $g$.With $g = z\,v$ every input is the same scalar times a constant, so an affine fit from $g$ is an affine fit from $z$; the measure is defined from the full input so that it means the same thing for multi-mode inputs. Zero means the neuron is a linear readout of its inputs.
  • Diversity within a cluster, $\varepsilon_{\rm proto}$: the error of the fit $F_i \approx a_i + c_i\,p$, where the prototype $p$ is the mean response of $i$'s connected component in the live graph. Zero means the member is its prototype up to gain and offset, the same feature. Averaged over the members of components with at least two neurons, weighted by size.

The effective dimension $d_{\rm eff} = (\operatorname{Tr} C)^2/\operatorname{Tr}(C^2)$ of the members' covariance $C$ and its numerical rank are reported as description only: $d_{\rm eff}$ is 1.0 both for exact copies and for members that differ by a few percent, and the rank counts differences of any size.

The linear arm is the control: every response is exactly $c_i z$ with $c_i = h_i\cdot v$, so both errors sit at rounding level and the toggle lands every member on the prototype. The homogeneous arm has nonlinear neurons whose members are nonetheless near-exact copies (see $\varepsilon_{\rm proto}$ in the table). That is forced. With $t_i = 0$ the potential is positively homogeneous, $V_i'(\lambda u) = \lambda V_i'(u)$ for $\lambda > 0$, and so are the node equations: if $u$ is the equilibrium for $z$, then $\lambda u$ is the equilibrium for $\lambda z$. Every response is therefore $u_i(z) = \beta_i^+ z_+ - \beta_i^- z_-$, two numbers per neuron, and all of them lie in the two-dimensional span of $z_+$ and $z_-$. At $W = 0$ every neuron is even an exact multiple of one shared kinked function, $(a_i\cdot v)\,\phi(z)$, so $\varepsilon_{\rm proto} = 0$; coupling only perturbs the ratios $\beta_i^+/\beta_i^-$ slightly.

The heterogeneous arm is the real test. Thresholds put each neuron's kink at a different $z$, so before learning these are genuinely different functions. After learning, toggle the map: the members collapse onto the prototype to within a few percent. Coupling also shares the kinks: a fused member's response bends wherever any neighbour crosses its threshold, so members of one component acquire the same set of kinks.

Verdict on the reservoir

The basic model does not deliver "many random functions of each mode". With one shared mode, members of a cluster are nonlinear but are affine copies of their prototype to within a few percent of variance, and stronger fusion makes them closer copies.

Why: fusing and being different are the same quantity

This is not an accident of the potential. At a stationary point of the rule every pair of neurons is either connected, with $\alpha w_{ij} = b - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2 > 0$, or not, with $D_{ij}^2 \ge 2b$. So

$$w_{ij} > 0 \iff D_{ij}^2 < 2b, \qquad D_{ij}^2 = 2\,(b - \alpha w_{ij}) \ \text{ on every live edge.}$$

Two directly connected neurons differ, in mean square, by less than the fusion scale $2b$: by construction, since that is the condition for the edge to exist. And the stronger the edge, the smaller the difference it certifies; the edge in turn pulls the two responses together (for two neurons, $D^2 = D_0^2/(1 + 2w)^2$). The rule has one measure of difference, $D^2$. It builds a synapse exactly where $D^2$ is small and makes it stronger the smaller $D^2$ is, so fusing and being different are the same quantity pulling in opposite directions. Every edge of a cluster joins two neurons that differ by less than the fusion scale.

The bound carries over to the prototype error. Fitting $a_i = 0$, $c_i = 1$ is one admissible affine map, so $\varepsilon_{\rm proto, i} \le D^2(F_i, p)/\operatorname{Var} F_i$ (in sample). If neuron $i$ is connected to every other member, the prototype, being their mean, lies within $\max_j D_{ij}$ of it, and for such a member

$$\varepsilon_{\rm proto, i} \;\le\; \frac{D^2(F_i, p)}{\operatorname{Var} F_i} \;<\; \frac{2b}{\operatorname{Var} F_i}.$$

Two further effects push the real number far below this bound. First, $D^2$ counts every difference, including a different gain or offset, and most of the room a cluster has is spent on those; the affine map removes them. Second, stiffness: at small $\alpha$ the edges are strong and the differences they certify are a tiny fraction of $2b$. The figure measures both, on the heterogeneous arm at the settings chosen above, for three values of $\alpha$.

Every curve in (a) ends below 1, as it must. At $\alpha = 0.1$ the typical live edge certifies a difference several times smaller than the cap, and at larger $b$ (try the strong-fusion tour above) orders of magnitude smaller, while at $\alpha = 1$ and 10 the curves nearly coincide: there the edges are too weak to move the responses much, and the cap is little more than a selection rule. Panel (b) shows the other half of the story: members can sit further from their prototype than one edge's reach (bars above the dashed line), because a component is connected, not complete. Its members are joined through chains, and two members $k$ hops apart may differ by up to $k\sqrt{2b}$. That room is real, but in the scalar-drive experiment it is spent almost entirely on gain: the shape part, the only part that would count as a different feature, is a small fraction of the distance.

So diversity inside a component can come from only two places. One is chains. With several input modes, a chain can join neurons that mix the modes in different proportions, and then members differ substantially, but as different mixtures of modes, not as different functions of one mode. The sweep's multi-mode runs show this (four input blocks, each driven by its own scalar; neurons attached either to three random inputs or to three inputs of one block). With random attachments, neurons mix blocks from the start (fit error …) and fusion mixes them further (… at the largest $b$). The large $d_{\rm eff}$ at intermediate $b$ lives in chained components, in the linear arm as much as in the nonlinear ones. The other place diversity can come from is a stage outside the energy: something that makes members differ in a way the rule cannot see, and therefore cannot remove.

Diversity put in by hand

To make the second point concrete, take a tight cluster from the live experiment and pass each member through an output stage that is not part of the model: $r_i = \max(0,\ a_i u_i + c_i)$, with a random gain $a_i$ and a random offset $c_i$ per neuron. Learning still acts on $u$ and never sees $r$. The rates are now diverse functions of the mode, rectified copies of one prototype cut at different levels, much like random features. But every bit of that diversity was put in by the random draws of $a_i$ and $c_i$.

Key point

The rule fuses neurons because their responses are close, and the closer they are the harder it fuses them. Within a component, differences are capped at the fusion scale edge by edge and shrink far below it as fusion strengthens. A reservoir of different features per mode needs an ingredient the basic model lacks: a stage outside the conductance energy that the rule does not see (as above), or a term in the objective that rewards differences within a cluster rather than only penalising them. Neither was tested here as a learned mechanism.

Clusters versus eigenmodes

The note's second picture identifies each cluster with an eigenmode of the input. Two different objects deserve that name. One is an eigenmode of the input covariance, $\Sigma_g = \sum_b \lambda_b v_b v_b^\top$, which as a function is $e_b(g) = v_b\cdot g$. The other is an eigenfunction of the population itself. With centred responses $\tilde F_i$, the population response operator is

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal S f = \sum_i \tilde F_i\,\langle \tilde F_i, f\rangle_p, \\ \psi_a = q_a^{-1/2} \sum_i (\beta_a)_i\,\tilde F_i,\end{gathered}$$

where $\beta_a$ is the $a$-th unit eigenvector of the neurons' Gram matrix $C_{ij} = \langle \tilde F_i, \tilde F_j\rangle_p$ and $q_a$ its eigenvalue, so that $\mathcal S\psi_a = q_a\psi_a$. The $\psi_a$ are the independent patterns of variation across the population, and $q_a$ is the variance each carries, summed over neurons. The question is how the $\psi_a$ relate to the cluster prototypes and to the $e_b$.

Linear neurons, exactly. For $F_i = h_i\cdot g$ the answer is a commutator.

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal S \;\cong\; \Sigma_g^{1/2} K\, \Sigma_g^{1/2}, \qquad K = H^\top H, \\ \text{for } \Sigma_g \succ 0: \ \psi_a = e_a \text{ for all } a \iff [K, \Sigma_g] = 0, \\ \text{and then } q_a = \lambda_a \textstyle\sum_i (h_i\cdot v_a)^2 .\end{gathered}$$
The derivation

On linear functionals $f(g) = \theta\cdot g$ the inner product is $\langle f, f'\rangle_p = \theta^\top\Sigma_g\theta'$, and $\mathcal S f = \sum_i h_i\cdot g\ (h_i^\top\Sigma_g\theta)$, so $\mathcal S$ acts as $\theta \mapsto K\Sigma_g\theta$. In whitened coordinates $\phi = \Sigma_g^{1/2}\theta$, where the inner product is Euclidean, this is the symmetric matrix $\Sigma_g^{1/2}K\Sigma_g^{1/2}$, whose nonzero spectrum is that of the neurons' Gram matrix $H\Sigma_g H^\top$. A symmetric matrix shares its eigenvectors with $\Sigma_g$ exactly when it commutes with $\Sigma_g$, which, since $\Sigma_g^{1/2}$ is a function of $\Sigma_g$, holds iff $[K, \Sigma_g] = 0$, provided $\Sigma_g \succ 0$ (eigenfunctions are meant up to normalisation, and inside a degenerate eigenspace of $\Sigma_g$, "its eigenvectors" means a suitable basis of it). Then $q_a = \lambda_a\, v_a^\top K v_a$. If $\Sigma_g$ is singular, as for the rank-one inputs of the first half, the part of $K$ outside its range never reaches a response: commuting is then sufficient but not necessary, and the criterion becomes $[\Pi K \Pi, \Sigma_g] = 0$, with $\Pi$ the projector on the range of $\Sigma_g$. (One neuron with $h = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and $\Sigma_g = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has a single eigenfunction, necessarily the single input mode, while the commutator $[K, \Sigma_g]$ is not zero.) The live figures below check the spectral identity and report the commutator.

Clusters of identical members. If cluster $k$ has $n_k$ members that all respond as $\sqrt{\nu_k}\,\hat p_k$ (unit-norm prototype $\hat p_k$, member variance $\nu_k$), then $\mathcal S = \sum_k n_k\nu_k\,\hat p_k\langle\hat p_k, \cdot\rangle$, and its nonzero eigenvalues are those of the cluster Gram matrix

$$(\nu n)^{1/2}\, P^\top P\, (\nu n)^{1/2}, \qquad (P^\top P)_{kl} = \langle \hat p_k, \hat p_l\rangle_p .$$

For clusters that are tight but not identical this is an approximation, and for wide components it can fail badly; in general $q$ is the spectrum of the full centred Gram matrix $C_{ij}$. Only when the prototypes are orthogonal is $\psi_k = \hat p_k$ with $q_k = \nu_k n_k$, which is the note's picture. Otherwise the $\psi_a$ mix prototypes: for two clusters whose prototypes have cosine $\rho$ and equal $\nu$,

$$q_\pm = \tfrac{\nu}{2}\Big(n_A + n_B \pm \sqrt{(n_A - n_B)^2 + 4 n_A n_B\rho^2}\Big).$$

A shared component and a contrast

The cleanest case has two inputs, $g_1 = s + c$ and $g_2 = s - c$, with $\operatorname{var} s = 1$ and $\operatorname{var} c = \sigma_c^2$ independent. Here $s$ and $c$ are exactly $\Sigma_g$'s eigenmodes. Put $n_A$ linear neurons on $g_1$ and $n_B$ on $g_2$: two clusters with prototypes $s \pm c$ and cosine $\rho = (1 - \sigma_c^2)/(1 + \sigma_c^2)$. Every response is a function $x\,s + y\,c$, so the whole population lives in a plane, drawn below with the geometry of the function space.

With equal clusters and $\sigma_c^2 < 1$ the symmetry decides: $\psi_1 \propto p_A + p_B \propto s$ and $\psi_2 \propto c$, with eigenvalues in the ratio $1 : \sigma_c^2$. At $\sigma_c^2 = 1$ with equal clusters the two eigenvalues coincide, so symmetry fixes only the eigenspace, the whole plane: any orthonormal pair of functions in it is an eigenbasis, and $\psi_1$, $\psi_2$ are not defined one by one (the figure then draws none). Either way the population's modes are $\Sigma_g$'s modes, and neither is a cluster; in the sweep the best match between an eigenfunction and a prototype is only … at $\sigma_c^2 = 0.1$. In the terms of (5), $K = \operatorname{diag}(n_A, n_B)$ commutes with $\Sigma_g$ exactly when $n_A = n_B$ (or when $\sigma_c^2 = 1$, where $\Sigma_g$ is a multiple of the identity). Make the clusters unequal and $\psi_1$ swings from $s$ towards the larger cluster's prototype (in the sweep, $|\cos(\psi_1, s)|$ = … at 20/4 and $\sigma_c^2 = 0.1$, and … at $\sigma_c^2 = 0.5$). Only when the prototypes are orthogonal, at $\sigma_c^2 = 1$, are the eigenfunctions the prototypes, with $q = \nu n$ exactly (… in the sweep). There $\Sigma_g$ is a multiple of the identity, so every direction is one of its modes. A leak in the attachments makes members differ slightly; the exact spectrum then departs from the identical-member formula, which the bars show.

Trained networks

In a trained network the clusters come from the attachments and the fusion scale. Twelve inputs in four blocks of three (variances 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.25, within-block correlation 0.8), and 48 neurons, twelve per block, each attached to the three inputs of its block. Two presets differ only in the correlation between blocks.

With uncorrelated blocks and linear neurons, four tight clusters form over a wide range of $b$, their prototypes are orthogonal, and the eigenfunctions are close to being at once the prototypes and $\Sigma_g$'s top four modes, with $q \approx \nu n$: the note's picture, approximately. Two things are at work, and only one is exact. Responses supported on different, uncorrelated blocks are exactly orthogonal in the population inner product, so the population splits block by block. Within a block nothing makes the prototype, a random attachment-weighted mix of the block's three inputs, equal to the block's top covariance mode (its uniform direction). It comes close because that mode dominates the block's variance at within-block correlation 0.8, and fusion's finite strength and the evaluation bank's sampling add small errors of their own; the table below gives the commutator and the cosines, which stop short of 1. And nothing was learned that the attachments had not already built in: each block's neurons see only that block's inputs, and uncorrelated blocks give orthogonal prototypes. Make the blocks correlated (cross-block correlation 0.4) and the clusters are the same four groups of neurons, but now their prototypes overlap and the eigenfunctions are a shared component plus contrasts across clusters, matching $\Sigma_g$'s modes and not the prototypes. Then $q \approx \nu n$ fails, and the cluster-Gram prediction (6) still holds approximately (the members are close, not identical). Even with linear neurons, $K$ never commutes with $\Sigma_g$, in either preset, so the match with $\Sigma_g$'s modes is close but not exact. The sweep, over three seeds:

With random attachments, at intermediate $b$, the eigenfunctions match neither the prototypes nor $\Sigma_g$'s modes well, and near percolation even the cluster-Gram prediction fails, because the components are wide chains rather than tight clusters. With the rectifying potential the top eigenfunctions are partly nonlinear (last column), and the cluster counts shift.

Clusters are not eigenmodes

Clusters are groups of similar neurons; eigenmodes are the independent patterns of variation across those groups. They coincide only when the clusters' prototypes are orthogonal, which in these networks happens when clusters draw on uncorrelated groups of inputs. Otherwise the population's modes are a shared component and contrasts between clusters, and $q$ is the spectrum of the cluster Gram matrix (6), not $\nu n$: exactly for clusters of identical members, approximately for tight ones. For wide components $q$ comes only from the full centred Gram matrix of the neurons.
