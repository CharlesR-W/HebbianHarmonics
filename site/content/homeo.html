The note ends on a conjecture: the homeostatic parameters are two knobs, resolution and expressivity, that together set a spectral filter $n(\text{energy})$. In this model the resolution knob is real ($\theta$); a filter exists, but the start of the run sets it, or private noise does, and then it is winner-take-all; the expressivity knob does not exist.

Explore it

How the parts that "exist" are counted: exact enumeration, a sufficient stability test

A part counts as stable here (findings F1) when $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}$, its eigenvector is positive, and $(\Sigma_g h)_b < \theta$ strictly for every input off the support. This test is sufficient (the nondegenerate case), not necessary: with a zero margin or gap a part can still be stable, e.g. the dominant input of $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1)$ at $\theta = 0$. For single inputs the exact statement is simple: input $b$ is a part iff $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every $a \ne b$. For the synthetic covariances hebb/homeo_cov.py enumerates all $2^B - 1$ supports at every $\theta$ on a grid of step 0.01 and stores the stable ones in data/homeo/covs.json; where a grid value sits exactly on a correlation level some parts are marginal, and the step line has a gap. For MNIST ($B = 49$) the script runs the lone-neuron flow from 849 starts and checks every distinct end point with the exact test: a lower bound in principle, exact for every part it lists. None of this involves $N$, $\gamma$, $\omega$ or the initial weights.

Neurons per mode: a filter, set by the start

How does the number of neurons on a mode depend on its energy? On the families tested (planted blocks, graded variances, chapter 2's power-law covariance):

  • A steep filter: fitted $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$ ($\lambda_k$ the part's top eigenvalue), $\beta$ from about 7.6 at $\theta = 0.02$ to 1.5 at $\theta = 0.45$; nothing for parts with $c \le 0$. Empirical, not a law.
  • Set in the transient: at $\theta = 0.2$ a narrow start (log-sd 0.5) gives $\beta \approx$ 5.9, a wide one (log-sd 3) 0.76. Any allocation of compatible, disjoint parts is a fixed point in the zero-floor limit (a finite floor adds a weak coupling). With $\gamma = 1$ below the heuristic crossover, all go to the loudest part.
  • With private noise, a law, but winner-take-all wherever parts stay intact (below).
Why the start decides: $\beta$ vs the initial spread, power iteration, no $n_k$ at the fixed point, what changes $\beta$

Planted blocks: at $\theta = 0.25$ without lateral plasticity $\beta \approx$ 2.9, at $\theta = 0.05$ 6.8; with $\gamma = 1$ above the crossover about the same (3.1). The share follows the block's top eigenvalue: a bigger block at fixed top eigenvalue gets slightly fewer neurons, not more.

A neuron's log-weights move as $\tfrac{d}{dt}\log h_b \supset v_b/s - \theta/w_b$. The first term rewards energy, so as $\theta \to 0$ the loudest input wins everything; the second rewards whichever input the neuron happened to start strongest on, so at higher $\theta$ the initial weights decide more. More precisely (findings F12), write a lone neuron's input weights as $w = s\,h$ with $s = \mathbf 1^\top w$: in the rescaled time $d\tau = dt/s$, $dh/d\tau = Ah - h(\mathbf 1^\top A h)$ with $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, continuous power iteration on $A$ clipped to non-negative $h$. Without a floor the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ never decreases (with a positive floor it can dip slightly) and its strict local maxima are the stable parts, so a neuron ends where power iteration from its initial weights ends.

Any allocation is a fixed point (F4), under two hypotheses: the parts are mutually compatible and disjoint, and the floor tends to zero. A cluster's fixed-point equation, $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}h_S = c\,h_S$, does not contain its number of members. With a finite floor $n_k$ enters through $h_k(|w_{kB}| + w_{\min}(N - n_k)) = w_{kB} + w_{\min}\sum_{l\ne k} n_l h_l$, a pull of relative size $\omega/c$ towards the population mean, so the equilibria depend weakly on the allocation. The same holds for any weight-only regulariser ($w = f(\mathrm{Cov})$ gives $h \propto f(\Sigma_g h)$) and for per-neuron synaptic scaling (which $h$ ignores). Converged runs that differ only in the seed sit at different allocations (three corners in chapter 1: 34/30/26, 34/32/24, 35/28/27). Try changing the seed in the explorer.

Private noise: a law, but winner-take-all

If the transient decides, the obvious fix is noise that keeps neurons hopping until they forget the start. Shared minibatch noise cannot (chapter 1); private noise, independent per synapse, can (findings F11–F17). Three Itô models, reflected at the floor: input (each synapse estimates its covariance from its own $1/\sigma^2$ samples per unit time), additive ($\sigma\,dW$), multiplicative ($\sigma w\,dW$). The first two forget the start. Multiplicative noise at the default floor never does: a pruned synapse leaves the floor at rate $\sim e^{-x}$, $x = 2\theta/(\sigma^2 w_{\min})$, a mean escape time of $e^{112}$ to $e^{16\,000}$.

Wherever parts stay intact (at most about 5–9% in transit), at least 99% of neurons sit on the loudest part; a graded allocation needs a sizeable fraction of neurons between parts. Under input noise $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta(\sigma)}$, $\beta \approx$ 6.9–7.2 at $\sigma = 0.25$ (11–25% in transit) to 1.9–2.0 at $\sigma = 0.8$ (76–83%), the same for the three $\theta$ tested apart from which parts exist; still 1.16 and falling at $\sigma = 5$, so the large-noise limit is open. Additive noise: Boltzmann-like, $n_k \propto e^{\lambda_k/T}$. Best trade-off found: multiplicative noise with a large floor, 13% off the loudest part at 8% in transit, but that floor ($\omega \ge 1.2$ at $N = 120$) is lethal for dense lateral networks and was tested on lone neurons only. With lateral plasticity, noise gives one bound cluster on the loudest part from any start, even above the heuristic crossover (try it).

The laws per model, three starts over time (multiplicative never mixes), the integrator

Scope and integration. Lone neurons on $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(0.5, \dots, 3)$ and a planted 12-input family; networks of $N = 120$ on two 4-block covariances. Input and additive noise give total variation $\le 0.004$ between the extreme starts, for all but the smallest $\sigma$. With $h$ frozen over a step, a synapse drifts towards $c$ at rate $\lambda$. Additive and input noise use a Strang splitting: an exact half-step of the drift that sticks at the floor, the noise increment reflected at $w_{\min}$, another half-step; it reproduces the analytic floor mean (0.0207 vs 0.0210). Multiplicative noise uses the same splitting in $\log w$, validated against exact escape rates. An earlier version of this section said multiplicative noise never hops at all: that came from an integrator that multiplied after the drift step and then clipped, so a floored synapse could not leave the floor; it is retracted. The explorer uses the same schemes.

How many clusters?

$\theta$ fixes the menu ((b) in the explorer); lateral synapses decide early which parts get neurons. Two starting neurons covary by about $\kappa_0$, so their lateral drift is $\eta(\gamma\kappa_0 - \theta)$: below this heuristic crossover (not a derived boundary) the population binds and lands on the loudest part; above it neurons choose independently. Applied coarse to fine it predicted every tested transition within 0.03–0.08 (F3); packing: Many inputs.

The floor: above $\omega \approx 0.02$ a leak that kills the network at high $\theta$

Every synapse at the floor still conducts $w_{\min}$, so each neuron is tied to the whole population by about $N w_{\min}$. When $\theta$ exceeds every initial drive, input synapses decay, and a neuron survives only if its embedding concentrates before its input conductance falls to about $\omega$. Across network sizes the extinction threshold roughly collapses on $\omega$ (F5; empirical, no derivation): about 0.8 at $\omega = 0.024$, falling to about 0.35 at $\omega = 1.2$. Below extinction a large floor can also hold the network in states that are not stable parts ($w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$, $\theta = 0.45$ sits on single inputs that are unstable as $w_{\min} \to 0$). Keep $\omega \le 0.02$, the explorer's default.

History: a bound population never splits as $\theta$ rises (hysteresis loops)

Sweeping $\theta$ up and back down with the state carried over, the clean network keeps merged clusters merged and symmetric states symmetric, even unstable ones: a near-zero residual does not certify stability (F8). A little private jitter breaks the symmetry. With $\gamma = 0$ and jitter, $K$ then tracks the number of stable parts both ways within a grid step or two. With $\gamma = 1$ the memory is real: $K = 1$ along the entire loop for both covariances and both noise levels, while a fresh start at the same $\theta$ gives 3 or 12 clusters. (This jitter, $w \leftarrow w\,e^{\sigma\xi}$ after each clipped Euler step, is not one of the noise models below; it serves only to break symmetries.)

Many representatives per mode?

The expressivity knob asks for many different random features per mode. In the dense model members end as clones in every condition tested (spread below $10^{-5}$; empirical: symmetry only keeps identical neurons identical). Private noise adds jitter, not diversity: deviations do not persist (correlation between quarters −0.02 to 0.04; voltages within a cluster correlated ≥ 0.9989 at $\gamma = 1$; F16). Only sparse wiring without lateral plasticity gave a few distinct functions per mode (participation ratio up to 2.07).

Comparison only: sparse wiring gives a few distinct functions per mode

With lateral plasticity on, sparse wiring makes members differ slightly in $h$ but their voltages stay correlated above 0.997. Without it, the population fragments into many small clusters of clones, and the number of distinct functions per block rises with sparsity: a few functions per mode, not a reservoir of random features. The explorer and every other figure stay dense.

What would have to change

  • Private noise (tested): lawful but winner-take-all allocation and no diversity; the best graded trade-off needs a floor lethal to dense lateral networks.
  • Sparse wiring (tested): distinct functions up to the mode's dimension.
  • Activity-dependent thresholds or a nonlinearity (speculation): could put $n_k$ into the fixed-point equation.

Caveat

Dense, symmetric, linear model, weight-only regulariser. Noiseless allocations depend on the initial distribution by construction; fitted laws hold on the tested families only (noise: diagonal and 4-block covariances). The explorer's random start differs from the sweeps'; tour notes quote the explorer.
