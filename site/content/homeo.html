The note ends on a conjecture: the homeostatic parameters are two knobs, one setting how many eigenmodes of the input get resolved and one setting how many random functions each mode gets, and together they act as a spectral filter, a number of neurons per mode as a function of its energy. Here I tested it. Short version: the resolution knob is real ($\theta$), an allocation filter exists but is set by the start of the run (or, with private noise, by the noise, and then it is winner-take-all), and the expressivity knob does not exist in this model.

The knobs, translated

The regulariser is the note's, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$, with weights clipped at a floor $w_{\min}$. After dividing by the Hebbian rate $\eta$ only two combinations matter, and three things outside $\rho$ matter at least as much.

How the parts that "exist" are counted (exact enumeration for $B \le 16$; a checked search for MNIST)

A part counts as stable here (findings F1) when $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}$, its eigenvector is positive, and $(\Sigma_g h)_b < \theta$ strictly for every input off the support. This test is sufficient (the nondegenerate case), not necessary: with a zero margin or gap a part can still be stable, e.g. the dominant input of $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1)$ at $\theta = 0$. For single inputs the exact statement is simple: input $b$ is a part iff $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every $a \ne b$. For the synthetic covariances hebb/homeo_cov.py enumerates all $2^B - 1$ supports at every $\theta$ on a grid of step 0.01 and stores the stable ones in data/homeo/covs.json; where a grid value sits exactly on a correlation level some parts are marginal, and the step line has a gap. For MNIST ($B = 49$) the script runs the lone-neuron flow from 849 starts and checks every distinct end point with the exact test: a lower bound in principle, exact for every part it lists. None of this involves $N$, $\gamma$, $\omega$ or the initial weights.

Neurons per mode: a filter, set by the start

The note's question: how does the number of neurons on a mode depend on its energy? On the families tested (planted blocks, graded diagonal variances, the power-law covariance of chapter 2):

  • There is a filter, and it is steep. Fitted $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$, with $\lambda_k$ the part's top eigenvalue and $\beta$ from about 7.6 at $\theta = 0.02$ to 1.5 at $\theta = 0.45$; parts with $c \le 0$ do not exist and get nothing. An empirical fit, not a law.
  • It is set in the transient. At $\theta = 0.2$ a narrow start (log-sd 0.5) gives $\beta \approx$ 5.9, a very wide one (log-sd 3) 0.76. For compatible, disjoint parts in the zero-floor limit, any allocation is a fixed point; a finite floor adds only a weak coupling. With $\gamma = 1$ below the heuristic crossover, everything goes to the loudest part.
  • Private noise makes it a law, but winner-take-all wherever parts stay intact (below).

At $\theta = 0.25$ without lateral plasticity $\beta \approx$ 2.9, at $\theta = 0.05$ 6.8; with $\gamma = 1$ above the crossover it is about the same (3.1). What sets the share is the block's top eigenvalue: a bigger block at fixed top eigenvalue gets slightly fewer neurons, not more.

Why the start decides: a lone neuron does power iteration, the fixed-point equation has no $n_k$ in it, and a table of what changes $\beta$

A neuron's log-weights move as $\tfrac{d}{dt}\log h_b \supset v_b/s - \theta/w_b$. The first term rewards energy, so as $\theta \to 0$ the loudest input wins everything; the second rewards whichever input the neuron happened to start strongest on, so at higher $\theta$ the initial weights decide more. More precisely (findings F12), write a lone neuron's input weights as $w = s\,h$ with $s = \mathbf 1^\top w$: in the rescaled time $d\tau = dt/s$, $dh/d\tau = Ah - h(\mathbf 1^\top A h)$ with $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, continuous power iteration on $A$ clipped to non-negative $h$. Without a floor the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ never decreases (with a positive floor it can dip slightly) and its strict local maxima are the stable parts, so a neuron ends where power iteration from its initial weights ends.

Any allocation is a fixed point (F4), under two hypotheses: the parts are mutually compatible and disjoint, and the floor tends to zero. A cluster's fixed-point equation, $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}h_S = c\,h_S$, does not contain its number of members. With a finite floor $n_k$ enters through $h_k(|w_{kB}| + w_{\min}(N - n_k)) = w_{kB} + w_{\min}\sum_{l\ne k} n_l h_l$, a pull of relative size $\omega/c$ towards the population mean, so the equilibria depend weakly on the allocation. The same holds for any weight-only regulariser ($w = f(\mathrm{Cov})$ gives $h \propto f(\Sigma_g h)$) and for per-neuron synaptic scaling (which $h$ ignores). Converged runs that differ only in the seed sit at different allocations (three corners in chapter 1: 34/30/26, 34/32/24, 35/28/27). Try changing the seed in the explorer.

Private noise: a law, but winner-take-all

If the transient decides, the obvious fix is noise that keeps neurons hopping until the allocation forgets where they started. Shared minibatch noise cannot do it (chapter 1: every neuron sees the same samples). Private noise, independent per synapse, was measured (findings F11–F17) for lone neurons on $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(0.5, \dots, 3)$ and a planted 12-input family, and in $N = 120$ networks on two 4-block covariances, with three Itô models reflected at the floor: input noise (each synapse estimates its covariance from its own $1/\sigma^2$ samples per unit time), additive ($\sigma\,dW$) and multiplicative ($\sigma w\,dW$). Input and additive noise forget the start (total variation $\le 0.004$ between the extreme starts, for all but the smallest $\sigma$). Multiplicative noise at the default floor never does, for a verified reason: a pruned synapse leaves the floor at rate $\sim e^{-x}$ with $x = 2\theta/(\sigma^2 w_{\min})$, a mean escape time between $e^{112}$ and $e^{16\,000}$ time units in the cases computed, so pruned synapses never return and the allocation stays inherited.

Wherever parts stay intact (at most about 5–9% of neurons in transit), at least 99% of neurons sit on the loudest part. Under input noise the filter is $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta(\sigma)}$, $\beta \approx$ 6.9–7.2 at $\sigma = 0.25$ (11–25% in transit) falling to 1.9–2.0 at $\sigma = 0.8$ (76–83%); on the three thresholds tested, $\theta$ only decides which parts exist. $\beta$ is still 1.16 and falling at $\sigma = 5$, so the large-noise limit is not established. Additive noise gives a Boltzmann-like law, $n_k \propto e^{\lambda_k/T}$. The best trade-off found is multiplicative noise with a large floor, 13% off the loudest part at 8% in transit, but that floor means $\omega = N w_{\min} \ge 1.2$ at $N = 120$, which is lethal for dense lateral networks, and it was tested only for lone neurons. With lateral plasticity, private noise gives one bound cluster on the loudest part from any start, even above the heuristic crossover ($\gamma = 1$, 4-block networks; try it).

The stationary laws for each model, three starts over time (multiplicative noise at the default floor never mixes), and how the noise is integrated

Integration. With $h$ frozen over a step, a synapse drifts towards $c$ at rate $\lambda$. Additive and input noise use a Strang splitting: an exact half-step of the drift that sticks at the floor, the noise increment reflected at $w_{\min}$, another half-step; it reproduces the analytic floor mean (0.0207 vs 0.0210). Multiplicative noise uses the same splitting in $\log w$, validated against exact escape rates. An earlier version of this section said multiplicative noise never hops at all: that came from an integrator that multiplied after the drift step and then clipped, so a floored synapse could not leave the floor; it is retracted. The explorer uses the same schemes.

Key point

On the families tested there is a spectral filter, but not where the conjecture put it. Without noise it is set during the transient by $\theta$ and the initial spread, and any allocation of compatible parts is a fixed point. With private noise it becomes a law set by $\sigma$, winner-take-all wherever parts are intact; a graded allocation needs a sizeable fraction of neurons between parts.

How many clusters?

$\theta$ fixes the menu: the number of stable parts depends on $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$ alone ((b) in the explorer). Which parts get neurons is decided early by the lateral synapses. Two starting neurons covary by about $\kappa_0$, so their lateral drift is $\eta(\gamma\kappa_0 - \theta)$: below this heuristic crossover (not a derived boundary) lateral synapses grow before anyone has chosen, and the bound population lands in one part, the loudest; above it neurons choose independently. The test repeats coarse to fine, with the parent part's self-covariance in place of $\kappa_0$. It predicted every tested transition within 0.03–0.08, independent of $N$ (60–240) and of the initial input scale (F3); a dense random lateral start acts like a larger $\gamma$. Packing, the ring example and the hierarchy tour are in Many inputs.

The floor is a hazard, not a dial: above $\omega \approx 0.02$ it leaks clusters together and kills the network at high $\theta$ (figure)

Every synapse at the floor still conducts $w_{\min}$, so each neuron is tied to the whole population by about $N w_{\min}$. When $\theta$ exceeds every initial drive, input synapses decay, and a neuron survives only if its embedding concentrates before its input conductance falls to about $\omega$. Across network sizes the extinction threshold roughly collapses on $\omega$ (F5; empirical, no derivation): about 0.8 at $\omega = 0.024$, falling to about 0.35 at $\omega = 1.2$. Below extinction a large floor can also hold the network in states that are not stable parts ($w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$, $\theta = 0.45$ sits on single inputs that are unstable as $w_{\min} \to 0$). Keep $\omega \le 0.02$, the explorer's default.

History: a laterally bound population never splits as $\theta$ rises; most of the $\gamma = 0$ hysteresis is a symmetry artefact (figure)

Sweeping $\theta$ up and back down with the state carried over, the clean network keeps merged clusters merged and symmetric states symmetric, even unstable ones: a near-zero residual does not certify stability (F8). A little private jitter breaks the symmetry. With $\gamma = 0$ and jitter, $K$ then tracks the number of stable parts both ways within a grid step or two. With $\gamma = 1$ the memory is real: $K = 1$ along the entire loop for both covariances and both noise levels, while a fresh start at the same $\theta$ gives 3 or 12 clusters. (This jitter, $w \leftarrow w\,e^{\sigma\xi}$ after each clipped Euler step, is not one of the noise models below; it serves only to break symmetries.)

Many representatives per mode?

The note's expressivity knob asks for many neurons per mode, each a different random feature of it. In the dense model, no. Members of a cluster end as clones in every condition tested (member-to-centroid spread below $10^{-5}$); permutation symmetry only says that neurons which become identical stay identical, so this is an empirical result. Private noise adds jitter, not diversity: a neuron's deviation from its cluster mean does not persist (correlation between the third and fourth quarter of a run −0.02 to 0.04) and voltages within a cluster stay correlated at 0.9989 or more with lateral plasticity (F16). The only source of distinct functions found is sparse random wiring without lateral plasticity, and it gives a few per mode (participation ratio up to 2.07), bounded by the mode's own dimension.

Comparison only: sparse random wiring gives at most a few distinct functions per mode (figure)

With lateral plasticity on, sparse wiring makes members differ slightly in $h$ but their voltages stay correlated above 0.997. Without it, the population fragments into many small clusters of clones, and the number of distinct functions per block rises with sparsity: a few functions per mode, not a reservoir of random features. The explorer and every other figure stay dense.

What would have to change to get the conjectured behaviour

  • Private noise (tested): it makes the allocation lawful but winner-take-all, and it does not create diversity. Grading it costs neurons in transit; the best trade-off found needs a floor that is lethal for dense lateral networks. No model found keeps parts intact and grades the allocation; none was ruled out.
  • Sparse wiring (tested, comparison only): distinct functions per mode, up to the mode's dimension; a reservoir needs a richer lift than the mode itself.
  • Per-neuron thresholds or a nonlinearity (speculation): a threshold that adapts to the neuron's own activity, or to how many neurons share its part, would put $n_k$ into the fixed-point equation, which a weight-only $\rho$ cannot.

Caveat

The dense, symmetric, linear (resistor) model with a weight-only regulariser; the noiseless allocation results describe where a transient from a random start ends, so they depend on the initial distribution by construction, and every fitted law holds on the tested families only (without noise: diagonal, block and power-law covariances; with private noise: diagonal and 4-block covariances). MNIST is one linear lift at $7\times7$; its border pixels carry no variance and get no neurons. The explorer's random start (mulberry32) is not the sweeps' (numpy): for a given seed it lands in a different, statistically equivalent place, and tour notes quote what the explorer gives.
