The note ends on a conjecture: the homeostatic parameters are two knobs, one setting how many eigenmodes of the input get resolved and one setting how many random functions each mode gets, and together they act as a spectral filter, a number of neurons per mode as a function of the mode's energy. This chapter is where I tried to find out what the knobs actually do. Short version: the resolution knob is real, the allocation filter is real, but neither is where the conjecture put it, and the expressivity knob does not exist in this model.

The knobs, translated

The regulariser is the note's, a function of the weight only: $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$, with weights clipped at a floor $w_{\min}$ (the "term that stops weights getting too small"). Three numbers, but after dividing by the Hebbian rate $\eta$ only two combinations matter, and three knobs that are not part of $\rho$ turned out to matter at least as much.

Two definitions are used throughout. $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$ is the expected covariance of two neurons at the start, assuming independent, exchangeable initial embeddings (lateral weights at the floor). A part is where a cluster can sit (chapters 1 and 2): a non-negative vector $h$ on a support $S$ of inputs with $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}\,h_S = c\,h_S$, $c > 0$ the top eigenvalue there, and $(\Sigma_g h)_b < \theta$ off the support. Its energy is $\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{SS})$, the top eigenvalue of the input covariance on its support; $c$ is what is left above threshold.

Key point

Of the three homeostatic parameters, one is a resolution knob ($\theta$), one does nothing ($\lambda$), and one is a leak that can only hurt ($\omega$). What decides how many of the possible clusters you get, and how many neurons each receives, is outside $\rho$: the lateral gain and the initial weights.

Explore it

The explorer runs the real engine on a covariance you choose. Eight are synthetic, each chosen to make one thing easy to see; one is real: the covariance of MNIST digits average-pooled to $7\times7$ pixels, so its parts are little images. The main view (a) is the question of the note's last paragraph: for each cluster, how many neurons it got against how much energy its part has, on log–log axes, with the fitted exponent $\beta$ and, dashed, proportional allocation $\beta = 1$. Panel (b) is the $\theta$ axis: how many stable parts exist at each $\theta$ (computed from the exact test, see below), with the collective region shaded, against how many are populated now.

What to try

  • The filter. On the graded variances (six independent inputs, variances 0.5 to 3), with no lateral plasticity, low $\theta$ sends almost everything to the loudest input, and $\theta = 0.4$ spreads neurons over five inputs with a much gentler slope. The slope in (a) is the "spectral filter" of the note, and $\theta$ sets it.
  • The collective regime. Turn lateral plasticity back on at $\theta = 0.2$, below $\gamma\kappa_0$: winner takes all. The step line in (b) says six parts exist; one is populated.
  • The floor. On block12 at $\theta = 0.65$, $\omega = 0.15$ kills every neuron; $\omega = 0.0015$ gives twelve clusters, one per input.
  • The initial spread. Same inputs and $\theta$, only the spread of the starting weights changes: narrow versus wide.
  • Real data. MNIST pixels: the parts are small non-negative sets of pixels that brighten together, not the signed, global eigen-images shown under the explorer.
How the parts that "exist" are counted

A part counts as stable here (findings F1) when $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}$, its eigenvector is positive, and $(\Sigma_g h)_b < \theta$ strictly for every input off the support; then $h$ is a strict local maximum of $h^\top(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)h/h^\top h$ over non-negative $h$. This test is sufficient, not necessary: in degenerate cases (a zero margin or gap) a part can still be stable, e.g. the dominant input of $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1)$ at $\theta = 0$. For single inputs the exact statement is simple: input $b$ is a part iff $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every $a \ne b$. For the synthetic covariances ($B \le 16$) hebb/homeo_cov.py enumerates all $2^B - 1$ supports at every $\theta$ on a grid of step 0.01 (the slider's step), and stores the stable ones in data/homeo/covs.json. At a few grid values $\theta$ equals a correlation level exactly and some parts are marginal (exactly the degenerate case); there the step line has a gap and the tooltip gives the counts just below and just above. For MNIST ($B = 49$, too many supports) the same script runs the lone-neuron flow $\dot w = \Sigma_g h - \theta - w$ from 849 starts (every pixel, 500 random, 300 random $3\times3$ patches) and checks every distinct end point with the exact test; that count is a lower bound in principle (parts with tiny basins could be missed), but it is exact for every part it lists.

None of this involves $N$, $\gamma$, $\omega$ or the initial weights: which parts exist depends on $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$ only. How many of them get neurons depends on everything else.

How many clusters?

The conjecture says the number of clusters is set by the homeostatic parameters. Partly. $\theta$ fixes the menu: the number of stable parts is a function of $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$ alone, the step line in (b). Which of them get populated is decided early in the run by the lateral synapses. At the start, two neurons covary by about $\kappa_0$, so their expected lateral drift is $\eta(\gamma\kappa_0 - \theta)$. That gives a heuristic (not a derived phase boundary): if $\theta < \gamma\kappa_0$, lateral synapses grow before anyone has chosen, the population becomes one equipotential blob, and a blob behaves like one neuron: it lands in one part, the loudest. Otherwise neurons choose independently. The same test then repeats inside each coarse part: neurons heading for a coarse part with self-covariance $E_{\text{parent}}$ stay bound while $\gamma E_{\text{parent}} > \theta$ and pick one finer part together. On block12 at $\theta = 0.2$, three parts exist; with $\gamma = 0$ all three are populated, with $\gamma = 1$ only one (4 of 4 seeds, F2).

The heuristic boundaries predicted every tested transition within 0.03–0.08 (F3), and do not move with $N$ (60 to 240) or with the scale of the initial input weights. Two more rules come with the gating. Packing: when the edges between two clusters are clipped at the floor, they can coexist only if $\gamma h_k^\top\Sigma_g h_l \le \theta + \lambda w_{\min}/\eta$, so with lateral plasticity on, overlapping parts exclude each other (chapter 2's ring: 16 overlapping bumps with $\gamma = 0$, 3–4 non-overlapping ones with $\gamma = 1$). And initial lateral weights act like a larger $\gamma$: a dense random start $W_{II} \sim U(0, 1)$ moves the $1 \to 3$ boundary on block12 from 0.27 to 0.53 without changing $\kappa_0$ (F3).

The floor is the third knob, and it is a hazard rather than a dial. Every synapse at the floor still conducts $w_{\min}$, so each neuron is tied to the whole population by about $N w_{\min}$ of conductance. When $\theta$ exceeds every initial drive, input synapses decay, and a neuron survives only if its embedding concentrates before its input conductance falls to about $\omega$.

Across network sizes the threshold roughly collapses on $\omega$ (F5): about 0.8 at $\omega = 0.024$ falling to about 0.35 at $\omega = 1.2$. The collapse is empirical; I have no derivation. Below extinction, a large floor can also hold the network in states that are not stable parts at all ($w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$, $\theta = 0.45$ sits on single inputs that are unstable as $w_{\min} \to 0$). Keep $\omega \le 0.02$; the explorer's default is $0.02$.

Key point

$\theta$ sets which clusters can exist. Whether a densely wired population resolves them is a race decided in the first moments, roughly by $\gamma\kappa_0$ against $\theta$, and the floor can only lose neurons.

Neurons per part: a filter, but in the transient

Now the note's last question: how does the number of neurons on a mode depend on its energy? To measure it cleanly I planted four blocks of three inputs (correlation 0.6 within a block, 0 across) and changed one block's variance or size while holding everything else fixed, eight paired seeds per point (SPEC §7.5).

On these families there is a filter, and it is steep. At $\theta = 0.25$, without lateral plasticity, the exponent is $\beta \approx$ 2.9; at $\theta = 0.05$ it is 6.8. With $\gamma = 1$ above the (heuristic) collective crossover it is about the same (3.1); below it (here $\theta = 0.05$ and $0.15$) every neuron goes to the loudest block. What controls the share is the block's top eigenvalue $\lambda_k = \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{S_kS_k})$: making a block bigger at fixed top eigenvalue (the "size, same λ" family) barely changes anything, with slightly fewer neurons for bigger blocks. A part whose supra-threshold eigenvalue $c$ is not positive does not exist and gets nothing: a hard cutoff.

So the fitted filter is $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$ (an empirical fit on the covariances tested, not a law) with $\beta$ falling from about 7.6 at $\theta = 0.02$ to about 1.5 at $\theta = 0.45$, just below the smallest variance. Why: a neuron's log-weights move as $\tfrac{d}{dt}\log h_b \supset v_b/s - \theta/w_b$. The first term rewards energy, so as $\theta \to 0$ the loudest input wins everything; the second rewards whichever input the neuron happened to start strongest on, so at higher $\theta$ the initial weights decide more. That is why the spread of the initial weights is the other dial: at $\theta = 0.2$, a narrow log-normal start (log-sd 0.5) gives $\beta \approx$ 5.9, a very wide one (log-sd 3) gives 0.76. Proportional allocation, $\beta = 1$, is reachable, but only by tuning the initial spread against $\theta$.

There is a tidy way to see why the start decides. Write a lone neuron's input weights as $w = s\,h$ with $s = \mathbf 1^\top w$. Then in the rescaled time $d\tau = dt/s$ its embedding obeys $dh/d\tau = Ah - h(\mathbf 1^\top A h)$ with $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$: continuous power iteration on $A$, clipped to non-negative $h$ (findings F12). Without a floor, the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ never decreases along the way (with a positive floor it can dip slightly), its strict local maxima are the stable parts, and so where a neuron ends up is where power iteration from its initial weights ends up.

And here is the catch. Any allocation is a fixed point (F4), for mutually compatible parts in the zero-floor limit. The fixed-point equation of a cluster, $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}h_S = c\,h_S$, does not contain the number of neurons in it. The only place $n_k$ enters is the floor: $h_k(|w_{kB}| + w_{\min}(N - n_k)) = w_{kB} + w_{\min}\sum_{l\ne k} n_l h_l$, a pull of relative size $\omega/c$ towards the population mean, so with a finite floor the equilibria depend weakly on the allocation. This holds for any weight-only regulariser ($w = f(\mathrm{Cov})$ gives $h \propto f(\Sigma_g h)$) and for per-neuron synaptic scaling (which $h$ ignores). Converged runs that differ only in the seed sit at different allocations with residuals below $10^{-5}$ (three corners in chapter 1: 34/30/26, 34/32/24, 35/28/27). The equilibrium does not choose $n(\text{energy})$ (beyond that weak floor coupling); the race at the start does, and once run, the network stays where it landed. Try changing the seed in the explorer.

Does noise make allocation a law?

If the transient decides, the obvious fix is noise: let neurons keep hopping between parts until the allocation forgets where they started, and see what law the stationary state obeys. Shared minibatch noise cannot do this (every neuron sees the same samples). Private noise, independent for every synapse, was measured in a follow-up sweep (docs/findings_private_noise.md, F11–F17) with three models. Under input noise, the one I would call biological, each synapse estimates its covariance from its own stream of samples, $1/\sigma^2$ per unit time. Under additive noise, $w \mathrel{+}= \sigma\sqrt{dt}\,\xi$. Under multiplicative noise, $w \leftarrow w\,e^{\sigma\sqrt{dt}\,\xi}$, the sweep found that nothing hops; an independent review showed that result to be an artefact of the integrator (a floored synapse could not leave the floor at all), so multiplicative noise is open, being rerun, and is not shown. The additive and input-noise results stand for now but are also being re-checked with a reflected integrator.

Under input and additive noise the three starts converge to the same allocation (total variation $\le 0.004$ between the extreme starts for input noise with $\sigma \ge 0.1$, F13), so with private noise the allocation is a law of the parameters. But look at what the law is.

In every run where the parts stay clean (at most 5% of neurons in transit), at least 99% of neurons sit on the loudest part: the stationary law is winner-take-all. A graded filter $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta(\sigma)}$ appears only at noise levels that keep many neurons between parts: $\beta \approx 7$ at $\sigma = 0.25$ with 9–23% in transit, falling to about 1.8 at $\sigma = 0.8$ with 68–79% in transit; additive noise gives a Boltzmann law in $\lambda$ instead (F14). The temperature is $\sigma^2$, and on the three thresholds tested $\theta$ drops out except for the existence cutoff: compare the $\theta$ buttons. These are fits on one diagonal family, not general laws. $\beta$ reaches 1 only as $\sigma \to \infty$, where the allocation is just the argmax statistics of noise-dominated weights, not energy tracking. With lateral plasticity it is starker: private noise binds the whole population into one cluster on the loudest part, even above the heuristic collective crossover (F15, on the one planted family tested; try it). In the explorer, compare $\sigma = 0.2$ with $\sigma = 0.4$.

Key point

There is a spectral filter: parts with $c \le 0$ get nothing, and on the families tested the rest get $n_k \propto \lambda_k^\beta$ with $\beta$ between about 7 and 1.5. But it is set by $\theta$ and the initial weights during the transient, it becomes winner-take-all whenever lateral plasticity binds the population first, and the equilibrium barely cares: every allocation of compatible parts is a fixed point up to the floor's weak coupling. Private (input or additive) noise turns it into a law, set by $\sigma$ rather than $\theta$ in the cases tested, and that law is winner-take-all unless many neurons are kept between parts.

History matters

If the allocation and the cluster count are decided by the transient, slow changes of $\theta$ should leave a memory. They do, but part of it is an artefact. Sweeping $\theta$ up and back down with the state carried over, the clean (deterministic) network keeps merged clusters merged and symmetric states symmetric, even unstable ones: a near-zero residual does not certify stability (F8). Adding a little private noise to every synapse breaks that symmetry.

With $\gamma = 0$ and jitter, $K$ tracks the number of stable parts both ways within a grid step or two, so most of the deterministic $\gamma = 0$ loop was the symmetry artefact. With $\gamma = 1$ the memory is real: $K = 1$ along the entire loop for both covariances and both noise levels, while a fresh start at the same $\theta$ gives 3 or 12 clusters. A laterally bound population never splits as $\theta$ rises.

Many representatives per mode?

The note's second knob was expressivity: many neurons per mode, each "a random feature applied to that eigenmode", a reservoir. In the dense model it fails in every condition tested. Members of a cluster end up as clones (member-to-centroid spread below $10^{-5}$ in every run); the symmetry argument only guarantees that neurons which become identical stay identical, since the dynamics is symmetric under permuting neurons and shared minibatch noise treats them alike. That they all become identical is an empirical result. The only way I found to get several distinct functions per mode is to change the wiring, shown here as a labelled comparison (the explorer and every other figure stay dense).

With lateral plasticity on, sparse wiring makes members differ slightly in $h$ but their voltages stay correlated above 0.997. Without it, the population fragments into many small clusters of clones, and the number of distinct functions per block rises with sparsity, up to a participation ratio of 2.07 at $p = 0.15$: a few functions per mode, bounded by the mode's own dimension, not a reservoir of random features. Private noise does not help either: members then differ at each instant, but the differences do not persist (correlation between a neuron's deviations in successive quarters of a run between −0.02 and 0.03 at $\gamma = 1$) and voltages within a cluster stay correlated at 0.9989 or more with lateral plasticity (0.97 or more without). That is jitter, not diversity (F16).

What would have to change to get the conjectured behaviour

Speculation, one line each, except the first, which has now been partly tested.

  • Private noise (tested, above, for input and additive noise; multiplicative being rerun). It does make the allocation forget its start, but the law it selects is winner-take-all unless the parts are blurred; no private-noise model found keeps the parts clean (under 5% in transit) and grades the allocation, though none was ruled out (F14, F17).
  • Sparse wiring. Each neuron reading a random subset of inputs gives distinct functions per mode, up to the mode's dimension (the comparison above); a reservoir needs a richer lift than the mode itself.
  • Per-neuron thresholds or a nonlinearity. A threshold that adapts to the neuron's own activity (or to how many neurons share its part) is the obvious way to put $n_k$ into the fixed-point equation, which weight-only $\rho$ cannot do.

Caveat

Everything here is the dense, symmetric, linear (resistor) model with a weight-only regulariser and Euler steps; the allocation results are about where a transient from a random start ends, so they depend on the initial distribution by construction. The MNIST covariance is one linear lift of one dataset at $7\times7$; its border pixels never change, so their inputs carry no variance and get no neurons. The explorer's random start (mulberry32) is not the sweeps' (numpy), so for a given seed it lands in a different, statistically equivalent place; tour notes quote what the explorer gives.
