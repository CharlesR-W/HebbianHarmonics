Appendix. How the distance rule fuses neurons into components, and where the percolation regimes lie, in more detail than the main path needs. It backs up Reading the weights and Nonlinear neurons.

This is where the new model first moves. Each neuron is tied to the inputs by fixed attachments and to the other neurons by plastic conductances, and the rule changes a conductance according to how far apart the two neurons' response functions are. I wanted to know three things. Is the learned state exactly the one the theory states? How do neurons fuse as the fusion scale $b$ grows? And does fusion produce a few clean clusters? The first answer is yes, to solver precision. Fusion reinforces itself, so it is bistable, and $\alpha$, not $b$, decides how tight it is and how much it depends on history. The third answer is no. With random attachments the network goes from singletons, through chains, to one blob, and no range of $b$ holds a small number of clusters that the inputs determine.

The model, as far as this chapter needs it

$B$ input neurons are clamped to a stimulus $g$ with covariance $\Sigma_g$, and $N$ interior neurons have voltages $u$. Each interior neuron $i$ is attached to $s$ randomly chosen inputs by fixed conductances $a_{ib} \ge 0$ (random shares of a total of 1), and to the other interior neurons by plastic conductances $w_{ij} \ge 0$. The energy, counting each pair once, is

$$\begin{aligned}E_W(u; g) = {}& \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\,(u_i - u_j)^2 \\ &+ \tfrac12 \sum_{i,b} a_{ib}\,(u_i - g_b)^2 + \sum_i V_i(u_i).\end{aligned}$$

The voltages relax quickly to the minimum, which is unique because $E_W$ is strictly convex once every neuron has an attachment. Setting $\partial E_W/\partial u_i = 0$ gives the node equation

$$\begin{gathered}d_i\,u_i + V_i'(u_i) = \sum_j w_{ij}\,u_j + \sum_b a_{ib}\,g_b, \\ d_i = \sum_j w_{ij} + \sum_b a_{ib},\end{gathered}$$

so $u_i = (d_i\,\mathrm{id} + V_i')^{-1}(\text{input current})$. The derivative of the on-site potential, added to the leak, is the inverse of the neuron's transfer function. The default potential has two slopes, $V_i(u) = \tfrac{k_{lo}}{2}(u - t_i)_-^2 + \tfrac{k_{hi}}{2}(u - t_i)_+^2$. With $k_{hi} = 0$ and $k_{lo} > 0$ it is a leaky rectifier: below its own threshold $t_i$ a neuron's gain drops by the factor $d_i/(d_i + k_{lo})$. The potential is piecewise quadratic, so equilibria can be computed exactly. In the linear case $k_{lo} = k_{hi} = 0$ we have $u = Hg$, and the rows of $H$ are non-negative and sum to one: each neuron is a point in the triangle (for $B = 3$) whose corners are the inputs.

Neuron $i$'s equilibrium voltage, as a function of the stimulus, is its response function $F_i(g)$. Two response functions are compared by the mean squared distance

$$D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g\big[(F_i(g) - F_j(g))^2\big],$$

where the expectation is the plain (uncentred) mean over a fixed training bank of stimuli, the same bank that learning uses.

Learning descends one objective, the input-averaged equilibrium energy plus a regulariser on each conductance:

$$\begin{aligned}J(W) = {}& \mathbb E_g\Big[\min_u E_W(u; g)\Big] \\ &+ \sum_{i<j}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\,w_{ij}^2 - b\,w_{ij}\Big).\end{aligned}$$

By the envelope theorem, the derivative of the first term in $w_{ij}$ is $\tfrac12 (u_i - u_j)^2$ evaluated at the equilibrium and averaged over stimuli, which is $\tfrac12 D_{ij}^2$. Gradient descent is therefore the rule

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha\,w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2 \quad (\text{projected at } w_{ij} \ge 0), \\ w_{ij}^\ast = \frac{(b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+}{\alpha}.\end{gathered}$$

An edge lives exactly when $D_{ij}^2 < 2b$, and then its conductance is a known linear function of the distance. So $b$ sets the range of fusion and $\alpha$ its strength: $b/\alpha$ is the largest conductance an edge can reach, to be compared with each neuron's total attachment conductance of 1.

The envelope step, and why explicit Euler steps are safe

Write $u^\ast(g; W)$ for the equilibrium. Then $\partial_{w_{ij}} E_W(u^\ast; g) = \tfrac12 (u_i^\ast - u_j^\ast)^2 + \sum_k \partial_{u_k} E_W \cdot \partial_{w_{ij}} u_k^\ast$, and the second term vanishes because $\partial_u E_W = 0$ at the minimum. $V$ is continuously differentiable, so this holds for the two-slope potential too. Averaging over the bank gives $\partial_{w_{ij}} \mathbb E_g[E_W^\ast] = \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2$.

$E^\ast(W) = \mathbb E_g \min_u E_W$ is a minimum of functions that are linear in $W$, so it is concave in $W$. The Hessian of $J$ is therefore $\alpha I$ plus a negative semidefinite part, and the Euler step $dt = \min(0.5, 1/\alpha)$ used here (the same as in the precomputed sweeps) is stable at stable fixed points. That concavity is the self-reinforcement of fusion, seen from the weights.

Fusion as an attraction between response functions. $J$ is minimised jointly over the voltages (for each stimulus) and the conductances. If we minimise over the conductances first, holding the response functions fixed, each $w_{ij}$ goes to (5), and what remains is an objective for the response functions alone:

$$\begin{aligned}\hat J(F) = {}& \mathbb E_g \sum_i \Big[\tfrac12 \sum_b a_{ib}\,(F_i - g_b)^2 + V_i(F_i)\Big] \\ &- \sum_{i<j} \frac{(b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+^2}{2\alpha}, \qquad \min_W J(W) = \min_F \hat J(F).\end{aligned}$$

Each response function is tethered to its own attachments and potential, and is attracted to every other response function within distance $\sqrt{2b}$. The pair potential has depth $b^2/(2\alpha)$, and its force at distance $D$ is $w_{ij} D$, the pull of a spring of conductance $w_{ij}$. As a function of $D^2$ the potential is concave: the closer two responses are, the stiffer the spring between them, and the harder they are pulled together. The next section shows what that does to two neurons.

Two neurons: fusion is bistable

The smallest case can be solved by hand. Take two linear neurons, neuron 1 attached only to input 1 and neuron 2 only to input 2 (conductance 1 each), joined by $w$. Subtracting the two node equations gives $(1 + 2w)(u_1 - u_2) = g_1 - g_2$, so

$$\begin{gathered}D^2(w) = \frac{D_0^2}{(1 + 2w)^2}, \\ \frac{dw}{dt} = b - \alpha w - \frac{D_0^2}{2\,(1 + 2w)^2},\end{gathered}$$

where $D_0^2 = \mathbb E(g_1 - g_2)^2$ is the distance of the uncoupled pair. The stronger the edge, the closer the two responses, and the weaker the term that pulls the edge down. The uncoupled state $w = 0$ is stable exactly when $b < D_0^2/2$. A fused state exists above a saddle-node: with $c = D_0^2/2$, the tangency of the line $b - \alpha w$ with the curve $D^2(w)/2$ is at $1 + 2w = (4c/\alpha)^{1/3}$, which gives

$$\begin{gathered}b_{sn} = 3\cdot 2^{-4/3}\,(c\,\alpha^2)^{1/3} - \frac{\alpha}{2}, \\ \text{both states stable for } b_{sn} < b < \frac{D_0^2}{2},\end{gathered}$$

a window that is non-empty if and only if $D_0^2 > \alpha/2$. As $\alpha \to 0$, $b_{sn} \to 0$ and the window opens to the whole interval $(0, D_0^2/2)$: weak stiffness means strong hysteresis.

The example in SPEC.md (b = 0.2, α = 0.1, with w = 0 and a fused state both stable) needs D₀² = 1.

The drift in (7) is homogeneous of degree one in $(b, \alpha, D_0^2)$, so doubling all three leaves the fixed points where they were. That is why the bistable preset ($D_0^2 = 2$, $b = 0.4$, $\alpha = 0.2$) has the same two stable states as the SPEC example at $D_0^2 = 1$.

Thirty neurons on three inputs

Now the same rule acts on thirty neurons. Each neuron is attached to all three inputs ($s = 3$) with random shares, so at $W = 0$ the neurons are scattered over the triangle. By default the inputs are independent with unit variance. Learning uses a training bank of 256 Gaussian stimuli, and every equilibrium is solved exactly on it. Component diameters are also measured on an independent evaluation bank of 1,024 stimuli, because a statement about the population should not depend on the sample that trained it. The run starts from $W = 0$. Moving $b$, $\alpha$, $k_{lo}$ or $\sigma_t$ while it runs keeps $W$ (a continuation); ↺ restarts from $W = 0$.

The fixed point is the stated one

During the run, the pairs in panel (b) move about. At the fixed point every live edge lies on the line $w = (b - D^2/2)/\alpha$, and every dead pair sits at $w = 0$, to the right of $D^2 = 2b$. In panel (a) the ink dots then fill exactly the region inside the orange contour. The plasticity residual $r$ measures the distance from that picture, since $|dw_{ij}/dt| = |\alpha w_{ij} - (b - D_{ij}^2/2)|$ on a live edge. For the claim to mean anything, the $D^2$ must come from true equilibria, which the neuronal residual checks. The envelope tile checks the derivation itself. When the run converges, it differentiates the mean equilibrium energy numerically in one conductance (a central difference with step $10^{-4}$) and compares the result with $D_{ij}^2/2$, once for the strongest live edge and once for the nearest dead pair. The mismatch is at the level of the finite-difference error, in every tour.

Singletons, chains, one blob

At small $b$ the edge range $\sqrt{2b}$ is shorter than almost every distance between two neurons, so almost every neuron stays alone, and the few live edges join neurons whose attachments happen to be nearly the same. As $b$ grows, more pairs come within range. Each edge pulls its pair together, which brings further pairs within range. Near percolation the components are chains: every link is shorter than $\sqrt{2b}$, but the component as a whole spans several edge ranges (table (d)). Its diameter on the evaluation bank is within a few per cent of the training-bank value, so this is a property of the population and not of the sample. At large $b$ every pair is within range, and the single component contracts until its span is a fraction of an edge range. The diameter is therefore not monotone in $b$: it grows while chains form and shrinks once everything is fused (the scan below shows it).

$b$ sets the range; $\alpha$ sets tightness and hysteresis

From $W = 0$ the drift of every edge is $b - D_{ij}^2/2$, so which edges switch on first does not depend on $\alpha$ at all. $b$ alone decides that. What $\alpha$ decides is how strong a live edge becomes, up to $b/\alpha$. With $\alpha = 0.1$ the springs are up to ten times stiffer than at $\alpha = 1$, and they beat the attachments that hold each response in place. Components then contract to well under one edge range, and are no longer chains.

Stiff springs also give the two-neuron bistability, now for the whole network. The hysteresis tour fuses everything at $b = 0.3$ and then lowers $b$ in steps to $0.03$. Each step goes to its fixed point and carries $W$ over to the next. The network stays one component the whole way down. From $W = 0$ at the same $b$, the fixed point has several components. Both are fixed points with residuals far below the tolerance, so the fixed point that is reached is set by history, not by $(b, \alpha)$. At $\alpha = 1$ the same experiment shows almost no hysteresis (try it with the $\alpha$ slider, or see the down-continuation in the scan).

Rectifying neurons

With the default leaky rectifier, responses are no longer linear functions of $g$. They cannot be drawn in the triangle, so the view switches to MDS of $D$. The drawing is then only approximately isometric: its corner gives how much of the geometry the two dimensions hold. Nothing in the fixed-point statement changes, since (5) never assumed linearity. The potential moves the distances in two opposite ways. Compressing every response below threshold brings responses closer together; set $\sigma_t = 0$ to see that alone. Different thresholds add an offset between neurons that the attachments do not cancel, and this pushes the closest pairs apart. At the default $\sigma_t$ the second effect wins for the pairs that matter, so the same $b$ fuses less than in the linear network (set $k_{lo}$ to 0 to compare). The sequence from singletons through chains to one blob is unchanged, and so are the scan's conclusions when it is run on this network.

No plateau of a few clean clusters

The hope behind the note was that, between "everything alone" and "everything fused", the network would settle on a few clusters and hold them over a range of $b$. The scan below asks this of the network above. For each $b$ on a grid it runs to the fixed point from $W = 0$ (cold start), and again by lowering $b$ from the fused state (down-continuation), at $\alpha = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.1$.

The scan has not run yet.

How to read it. At $\alpha = 1$ (blue) the count falls from nearly $N$ to one over one to two decades of $b$. On the way the widest component spans several edge ranges, so these are chains, and its diameter stays large until $b$ is big enough for every pair to be in range at once. At $\alpha = 0.1$ (orange) the cold start is chain-like only around percolation and is tight on either side of it. The count still falls with $b$ without levelling off, not always monotonically, and near percolation refinement can stop being nested (the last column of the table). Meanwhile the down-continuation (dashed) stays fused well below the $b$ at which the cold start fuses. If the table finds a value of $b$ with a few tight clusters, compare that value with its neighbours and with the down-continuation at the same $b$.

So the number of components is not a property of the inputs. $b$ fixes a length, the edge range, and not a number. The count at a given $b$ is however many $\sqrt{2b}$-connected groups the random attachments happen to leave, and the feedback, strong or weak according to $\alpha$, makes them tighter or leaves them as chains, and decides between the coexisting fixed points by history. With random attachments, then, a few clusters that mean something need help: something that groups the neurons before fusion does, for example attachments that already follow the structure of the inputs (the later chapters use such block attachments, and say so), or a further principle that fixes how many groups there should be. Neither is in the basic model. Fusion alone is not always powerless: when the neurons differ along a single input mode, small stiffness condenses them into a few tight clusters (many inputs), but their number moves with $b$ and with history there too.

What we found

  • holdsThe fixed point is the stated one: $w_{ij} = (b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+/\alpha$, with plasticity and neuronal residuals below tolerance, and the envelope identity $\partial E^\ast/\partial w_{ij} = D_{ij}^2/2$ checked by finite differences, live and in the precomputed sweep.
  • holdsFusion is self-reinforcing. For two neurons it is bistable exactly in $b_{sn} < b < D_0^2/2$, and the engine lands on the analytic branches. The SPEC example needs $D_0^2 = 1$; at unit variance its $b$ lies below $b_{sn}$.
  • holds$b$ sets the range (which edges can live), and $\alpha$ sets tightness and hysteresis. At small $\alpha$ the fused state survives far below the $b$ at which a cold start fuses.
  • failsA regime of a few clean clusters from random attachments. Weak fusion gives chains several edge ranges wide; strong fusion gives tight components whose number keeps falling with $b$ and depends on history. Neither has a plateau, and the count is not set by the inputs.
