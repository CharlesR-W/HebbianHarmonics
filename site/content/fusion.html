When two neurons respond alike, the distance rule gives them a synapse, and the synapse makes them respond more alike still. This page follows that feedback, first for two neurons and then for thirty neurons that each mix three inputs at random. Fusion can be bistable (when $\alpha$ is small enough), so where the network ends can depend on its history, and as $b$ grows the neurons go from alone, through long chains, to one blob; on the grid of $b$ we scanned, no range holds a few tight clusters.

The rule and its fixed point

Each neuron is attached to the inputs by fixed conductances that sum to one, and to the other neurons by plastic weights $w_{ij} \ge 0$. Write $D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g[(u_i - u_j)^2]$ for the mean squared difference of two neurons' responses over a fixed bank of stimuli. The distance rule moves each weight by the drift

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha\,w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2, \\ \text{fixed point } w_{ij}^\ast = \frac{(b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+}{\alpha}.\end{gathered}$$

Weights cannot go negative: a weight at zero whose drift is negative stays at zero. A synapse can be live ($w_{ij} > 0$) only between neurons closer than $\sqrt{2b}$, the edge range, and it is at most $b/\alpha$ strong, against each neuron's total attachment of 1. So $b$ sets how far fusion reaches and $\alpha$ how hard it pulls. A component is a set of neurons connected by live synapses (a neuron with none is a component on its own), and a tight cluster is a component whose span, the largest distance between two members, is within one edge range; a wider component is a chain. The catch is that $D_{ij}^2$ depends on the weights: a strong edge pulls its two neurons' responses together, which lowers $D_{ij}^2$ and makes the edge stronger.

Where the rule comes from

The rule is gradient descent on $J(W) = \mathbb E_g\big[\min_u E_W(u; g)\big] + \sum_{i<j}\big(\tfrac{\alpha}{2} w_{ij}^2 - b\,w_{ij}\big)$, where $E_W$ is the network's energy. By the envelope theorem the derivative of the first term in $w_{ij}$ is $\tfrac12 D_{ij}^2$. That first term is a minimum of functions linear in $W$, hence concave: this is the self-reinforcement of fusion, seen from the weights. Against the convex ridge $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}w^2$ it can make $J$ non-convex, as in the bistable two-neuron example below; for large enough $\alpha$ it does not.

Two neurons: fusion can be bistable

Take two neurons, each attached to one input only, joined by $w$. Their distance is $D^2(w) = D_0^2/(1 + 2w)^2$, where $D_0^2$ is the distance of the uncoupled pair, so the rule becomes $dw/dt = b - \alpha w - D_0^2/\big(2(1 + 2w)^2\big)$. The uncoupled state $w = 0$ is stable when $b < D_0^2/2$, and a fused state exists above a saddle-node $b_{sn}$. Between the two, both are stable. The window is non-empty if and only if $D_0^2 > \alpha/2$, and it widens as $\alpha$ shrinks: weak stiffness means strong hysteresis.

Thirty neurons on three inputs

Now thirty neurons, each attached to three independent unit-variance inputs with random shares, so that at $W = 0$ they are scattered over the triangle whose corners are the inputs. The figure runs the rule from $W = 0$. Moving $b$ or $\alpha$ keeps the current weights (a continuation); ↺ resets to $W = 0$.

At small $b$ almost every neuron stays alone. As $b$ grows, each edge pulls its pair together, which brings further pairs within range. Near percolation (the default $b = 0.015$) the components are chains: every link is shorter than $\sqrt{2b}$, but the component as a whole spans several edge ranges. At large $b$ every pair is within range and the single component contracts to a fraction of an edge range.

$\alpha$ does not change which edges start to grow from $W = 0$ (there every edge's drift is $b - D_{ij}^2/2$), but it sets how strong they become, and so it changes the responses, which edges survive and the final components. At $\alpha = 0.1$, away from percolation, components contract to well under one edge range, and the two-neuron bistability returns for the whole network. The hysteresis tour fuses everything at $b = 0.3$ and lowers $b$ in steps to $0.03$, each time to the fixed point, carrying $W$: the network stays one component. Press ↺ and then play to start from $W = 0$ at the same $b$: it ends in several components. At $\alpha = 1$ the same experiment shows almost no hysteresis.

The run reaches the fixed point (1) to numerical tolerance, as the next figure shows for the pairs of the same run (all pairs with $D_{ij}^2 \le 4b$).

No plateau of a few tight clusters

Between "everything alone" and "everything fused", does the network hold a few clusters over a range of $b$? The scan runs the same thirty neurons to the fixed point at each $b$, from $W = 0$ (cold start) and by lowering $b$ from the fused state (down-continuation), at two values of $\alpha$. It is computed in your browser.

At $\alpha = 1$ the count falls from nearly $N$ to one over one to two decades of $b$, and on the way the widest component is a chain. At $\alpha = 0.1$ the components are chains only around percolation; the count does not level off (it even rises once), and the down-continuation stays fused below the $b$ at which the cold start fuses. Where a single grid value gives a few tight clusters, the table under Details names it; in this network the count there differs one grid step either side and on the down-continuation.

So in these runs $b$ fixes a length, the edge range, rather than a number of clusters: the count is however many $\sqrt{2b}$-connected components the random attachments happen to leave, and history picks between coexisting fixed points. One way to get a few meaningful clusters is attachments that already follow the structure of the inputs. The many-inputs page finds the same picture with twelve inputs, and one exception: neurons that differ along a single input direction condense into a few tight clusters at small $\alpha$.

Limits. All inputs are synthetic and Gaussian. The live figures and the scan use one seeded network of thirty neurons on three independent inputs.
