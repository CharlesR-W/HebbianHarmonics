Start as small as possible. Three input neurons $g_1, g_2, g_3$ are clamped to the stimulus. A hundred interior neurons are wired to them, and to each other, all to all, through plastic synapses that start out dense and random. Each interior neuron's voltage is a weighted average of the three inputs, so it is a point in a triangle with the inputs at its corners. I wanted to watch Hebb's rule move those points, and see whether they really fall into clusters.

Neurons as points in a triangle

Think of the network as resistors. Synapse $w_{ij} \ge 0$ is a conductance; the inputs' voltages are held at $g$ by the rest of the brain; the interior settles to whatever Kirchhoff says. At an interior node no current is lost, so its voltage is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours' voltages. Solving all of those averages at once gives

$$u = H g, \qquad H = L_{II}^{-1} W_{IB},$$

where $L_{II}$ is the graph Laplacian restricted to the interior and $W_{IB}$ holds the interior-to-input conductances. Row $i$ of $H$ is neuron $i$'s harmonic embedding $h_i$. Its entries are non-negative and sum to one, so $h_{ib}$ reads as "the fraction of my voltage that comes from input $b$", and $h_i$ is a point in the triangle whose corners are $g_1, g_2, g_3$. It depends only on the weights, not on the stimulus.

The maths: why rows of $H$ are probability vectors

Kirchhoff's current law at interior node $i$: $\sum_j w_{ij}(u_j - u_i) + \sum_b w_{ib}(g_b - u_i) = 0$, i.e. $L_{II}u = W_{IB}g$ with $L_{II} = \mathrm{diag}(\deg) - W_{II}$. With every weight at least $w_{\min} > 0$, $L_{II}$ is a non-singular M-matrix, so $L_{II}^{-1}$ has non-negative entries and $H = L_{II}^{-1}W_{IB} \ge 0$. If every input is held at the same voltage $c$, the solution is $u = c\mathbf 1$; hence $H\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$. (Probabilistically, $h_{ib}$ is the chance that a random walk from $i$, stepping along edges with probability proportional to conductance, first reaches the inputs at $g_b$.)

The plasticity rule is the note's, with a regulariser that depends only on the weight:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = w_0 - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j), \\ w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(u_i,u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j.\end{gathered}$$

The last identity is just $u = Hg$: the voltage covariance is $H\Sigma_g H^\top$, so Hebb sees only the input covariance $\Sigma_g$ and the embeddings. For a synapse onto an input, the covariance is $(H\Sigma_g)_{ib}$. I write the constant as $w_0 = -\eta\theta$, so that $\theta$ is in covariance units: with $\theta > 0$, a synapse grows only where its two neurons covary by more than $\theta$, and otherwise is pruned down to the floor $w_{\min}$. Lateral synapses use $\eta_{II} = \gamma\eta$; since $w_0$ is shared, their effective threshold is $\theta/\gamma$.$\lambda/\eta$ only sets the weight scale and the clock; I use $\lambda = \eta = 1$ and Euler steps of $dt = 0.05$. The floor $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$ keeps $L_{II}$ invertible; it is small but not nothing, because $N w_{\min}$ of floor conductance still connects each neuron to everyone (try $w_{\min} = 10^{-2}$ under "More controls").

Two properties drive everything below. The rule is bounded: each $u_i = h_i^\top g$ is a convex combination of the inputs, so no covariance exceeds $\max_b \Sigma_{bb}$ and no weight exceeds $\max(1,\gamma)\,\eta(\max_b\Sigma_{bb} - \theta)/\lambda$. And it is self-reinforcing: a strong synapse between $i$ and $j$ pulls their embeddings together, neurons with similar embeddings covary more, and that strengthens the synapse further. Whether the interior ends up as one blob or several clusters is a race between this feedback and the pruning.

What to notice

  • The first moments decide. Neurons start spread uniformly over the triangle (their input weights are independent exponentials, their lateral weights at the floor). By $t \approx 1$ the lateral synapses have either thickened everywhere, and the cloud contracts to one blob, or only within groups, and it tears into clusters (at speed ×1 that is a third of a second: use ×¼, or scrub back). After that, clusters move as rigid bodies.
  • Members of a cluster coincide. At the fixed point the neurons of a cluster agree to about $10^{-8}$ in $L_1$: the heatmap shows solid blocks, and the 3D view shows one dot where there are thirty.
  • Height is not what is being climbed. Corners are the highest points for independent inputs and the centroid the lowest, yet the no-pruning tour settles at the centroid. The pull is towards inputs a neuron already covaries with by more than $\theta$, not up the variance surface.
  • Convergence is visible. The residual falls exponentially once the structure is settled; a shoulder before that is a cluster still sliding. The run pauses itself when $r < 10^{-6}$.
  • Between clusters, lateral synapses sit at the floor (panel d), and their covariance in the heatmap is below $\theta/\gamma$; within a cluster, every synapse is alive.

One blob, unless something prunes

With independent inputs and $\theta = 0$ (no pruning), every pair of neurons starts with non-negative covariance, so every lateral synapse grows, the feedback loop closes, and the whole interior becomes one blob at the centroid. That is the note's starting point, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$ with $w_0 \ge 0$, and it gives one cluster whatever the (non-negative) input.

I expected any $\theta > 0$ to fix this. It does not. At $\theta = 0.2$ (weak pruning) the interior still becomes one blob, because initially most pairs of neurons covary by more than $0.2$ (88% of pairs at the default start). Pruning does change where the blob goes: it slides, as one body, to a single corner, and the other two inputs get no neurons. A blob behaves like one neuron, and for one neuron with independent inputs the only stable resting places when $\theta > 0$ are the corners (the maths below). Only when $\theta$ exceeds the typical initial covariance, $\tfrac13$ here (the average of $h_i^\top h_j$ for uniformly spread embeddings), are lateral synapses between neurons near different corners pruned before a blob can form: three corners.

Key point

There are two thresholds with two jobs. The input threshold $\theta$ decides where a group of neurons settles (the centroid when $\theta = 0$, a corner or a shared part when $\theta > 0$). The lateral threshold $\theta/\gamma$ decides how many groups there are: it must beat the covariance neurons have at the start, or they wire into one blob first. With $\gamma = 0.5$, $\theta = 0.2$ already gives three corners; with $\gamma = 2$, $\theta = 0.4$ gives one.

Correlated inputs: parts, not eigenvectors

Now let $g_1$ and $g_2$ correlate, $\rho_{12} = 0.6$, and set $\theta = 0.45$, between the cross-correlation $0.6$ and the uncorrelated level $0$ (a correlated pair). Two clusters form: one at $g_3$, and one at the midpoint of the $g_1$–$g_2$ edge, $h = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)$, holding the neurons that started near either $g_1$ or $g_2$ (about 70 of 100). That second cluster is not an eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$; it is a non-negative mixture of the inputs that move together, a shared part. Raise $\theta$ above $0.6$ and the midpoint stops being stable, but the cluster does not split into two: its members are identical, covary by their full variance $0.8 > \theta$, and stay wired together, so the whole cluster slides to $g_1$ or to $g_2$. A fresh start at $\theta = 0.8$ does give three corners.

The maths: what a surviving synapse says at the fixed point, and which parts are stable

At a fixed point, every synapse above the floor has zero drift, so with $\eta = \lambda = 1$:

$$\begin{gathered} w_{ib} = (\Sigma_g h_i)_b - \theta \quad\text{(input synapses)}, \\ w_{ij} = \gamma\, h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j - \theta \quad\text{(lateral synapses)},\end{gathered}$$

and a synapse at the floor has $(\Sigma_g h_i)_b \le \theta + w_{\min}$, respectively $\gamma h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j \le \theta + w_{\min}$. Each surviving synapse reads one entry of $H\Sigma_g$ or $H\Sigma_g H^\top$ off its own weight; that is what makes the note's weight-space readout possible.

Now take a cluster whose members share one embedding $h$. Lateral synapses inside it join nodes at equal voltage and carry no current, and those to other clusters sit at the floor, so up to $O(N w_{\min})$ Kirchhoff gives $h_b = w_{ib}/\sum_c w_{ic}$. Substituting the fixed-point weights, with $\mathbf 1^\top h = 1$:

$$\begin{gathered} h \;\propto\; (\Sigma_g h - \theta\mathbf 1)_+ \\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad (\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \\ (\Sigma_g h)_b \le \theta \text{ for } b \notin S,\end{gathered}$$

where $S$ is the support of $h$. So a cluster sits at a non-negative eigenvector of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ restricted to some subset of inputs; it is stable when that eigenvector is the top one (Perron) and the inequality off the support is strict. Independent inputs: on a support of size $k$ the uniform vector has eigenvalue $1 - k\theta$, below the other eigenvalues ($1$) whenever $\theta > 0$, so only corners are stable; at $\theta = 0$ everything is marginal and a blob stays where it formed. The pair: on $S = \{1, 2\}$ the uniform vector $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ has eigenvalue $1 + \rho_{12} - 2\theta$ against $1 - \rho_{12}$ for $(1, -1)$, so the midpoint is the stable part exactly when $\theta < \rho_{12}$; and the corner $g_1$ is stable only if $(\Sigma_g e_1)_2 = \rho_{12} < \theta$.

Anticorrelation is pruning in disguise

The $\theta \le 0$ story assumed non-negative covariances. Clipping at the floor prunes any synapse whose covariance is negative, whatever $\theta$, so anticorrelated inputs give clusters with no pruning drift at all: $\Sigma_g = I - \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/3$ at $\theta = 0$, every pair of inputs correlated $-\tfrac12$, gives three corner clusters. But it is not a separate mechanism. Because every row of $H$ sums to one,

$$\begin{gathered} h_i^\top(\Sigma_g + c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)h_j = h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j + c, \\ \big(H(\Sigma_g + c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)\big)_{ib} = (H\Sigma_g)_{ib} + c,\end{gathered}$$

so with $\gamma = 1$ the pairs $(\Sigma_g, \theta)$ and $(\Sigma_g + c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \theta + c)$ produce identical dynamics: the network only ever sees $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. The anticorrelated tour is therefore exactly independent unit inputs at $\theta = \tfrac13$ (I checked: the weights agree to $2\times10^{-14}$ after 400 steps), which sits right on the threshold of the previous section; that is why some seeds give two clusters there. "Pruning by anticorrelation" and "pruning by drift" are the same thing, $\theta$ measured from the level the inputs share. (With $\gamma \ne 1$ the two thresholds shift differently and the identity is only approximate. The same symmetry is why $\theta$ cannot be read off the weights later: it is indistinguishable from a constant added to $\Sigma_g$.)

History, seeds and noise

The fixed point you reach depends on how you got there. Run to three corners at $\theta = 0.4$ and then lower $\theta$ to $0.1$: the clusters stay apart, because corners covary by zero, while a fresh start at $0.1$ gives a single cluster. Near the threshold, the seed alone decides whether there are two clusters or three; and if the lateral synapses start wired up, the interior is one blob from the outset and stays one.

I hoped noise would help: with minibatch covariances instead of the exact $\Sigma_g$, perhaps members of a cluster would spread out into a cloud of slightly different features. They do not. With 16 samples per step, every neuron sees the same samples, so the noise is common to all of them and a cluster stays a point. What the noise does do is now and then push two clusters' covariance above $\theta$; they wire together, and merged clusters never separate. Three clusters become two, then one, which then wanders. Noise acts as a ratchet towards fewer clusters, and the smaller the batch (relative to $dt$), the faster it turns.The noise per unit time scales like $dt/\text{batch}$; at batch 64 and 256 the three corners lasted to $t = 300$ for seeds 1–4 at $\theta = 0.4$ and $0.5$.

Who gets the neurons?

With three independent unit inputs, the corner clusters get roughly a third of the neurons each, but only roughly: between 20 and 43 of 100 per corner over seeds 1–6, set by the basins of the starting positions. Make one input louder, $\mathrm{Var}\,g_1 = 2$ (one louder input), and $g_1$'s cluster takes about half. An input whose variance is below $\theta$ gets none: no neuron can covary with it by more than $\theta$. So the allocation does track input energy, in the direction the note guesses; whether it is proportional, or some other "spectral filter" set by $\theta$ and the starting distribution, is a question for the Homeostasis chapter, which measures it with controlled sweeps.

  • amendedClusters need pruning: a constant drift $w_0 = -\eta\theta < 0$, or negative input covariances, which are the same thing up to $\Sigma_g \to \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. And the lateral threshold $\theta/\gamma$ has to beat the covariance neurons have at the start, or they merge into one blob first.
  • amendedCluster positions are corners or shared parts (the midpoint of a correlated pair): non-negative Perron vectors of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a subset of inputs. Not signed eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$.
  • fails"Each neuron in a cluster is a random feature": in this limit the members of a cluster coincide to $10^{-8}$. That is redundancy, not a reservoir. Shared minibatch noise does not diversify them either; it merges clusters.

Caveat

Everything here is one tiny world: $B = 3$, all-to-all symmetric synapses, a linear (resistor) network, a floor at $w_{\min}$, Euler steps of $0.05$, and the specific random start described above. The tours are exact states and were checked against their notes for the seeds quoted; other seeds can land elsewhere near a threshold, which is itself one of the findings.
