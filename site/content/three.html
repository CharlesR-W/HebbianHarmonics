Start as small as possible. Three input neurons $g_1, g_2, g_3$ are clamped to the stimulus. A hundred interior neurons are wired to them, and to each other, all to all, through plastic synapses that start out dense and random. Each interior neuron's voltage is a weighted average of the three inputs, so it is a point in a triangle with the inputs at its corners. I wanted to watch Hebb's rule move those points, and see whether they really fall into clusters.

Neurons as points in a triangle

Think of the network as resistors. Synapse $w_{ij} \ge 0$ is a conductance; the inputs' voltages are held at $g$ by the rest of the brain; the interior settles to whatever Kirchhoff says. At an interior node no current is lost, so its voltage is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours' voltages. Solving all of those averages at once gives

$$u = H g, \qquad H = L_{II}^{-1} W_{IB},$$

where $L_{II}$ is the graph Laplacian restricted to the interior and $W_{IB}$ holds the interior-to-input conductances. Row $i$ of $H$ is neuron $i$'s harmonic embedding $h_i$. Its entries are non-negative and sum to one, so $h_{ib}$ reads as "the fraction of my voltage that comes from input $b$", and $h_i$ is a point in the triangle whose corners are $g_1, g_2, g_3$. It depends only on the weights, not on the stimulus.

The maths: why rows of $H$ are probability vectors

Kirchhoff's current law at interior node $i$: $\sum_j w_{ij}(u_j - u_i) + \sum_b w_{ib}(g_b - u_i) = 0$, i.e. $L_{II}u = W_{IB}g$ with $L_{II} = \mathrm{diag}(\deg) - W_{II}$. With every weight at least $w_{\min} > 0$, $L_{II}$ is a non-singular M-matrix, so $L_{II}^{-1}$ has non-negative entries and $H = L_{II}^{-1}W_{IB} \ge 0$. If every input is held at the same voltage $c$, the solution is $u = c\mathbf 1$; hence $H\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$. (Probabilistically, $h_{ib}$ is the chance that a random walk from $i$, stepping along edges with probability proportional to conductance, first reaches the inputs at $g_b$.)

The plasticity rule is the note's, with a regulariser that depends only on the weight:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = w_0 - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j), \\ w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(u_i,u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j.\end{gathered}$$

The last identity is just $u = Hg$: the voltage covariance is $H\Sigma_g H^\top$, so Hebb sees only the input covariance $\Sigma_g$ and the embeddings. For a synapse onto an input, the covariance is $(H\Sigma_g)_{ib}$. I write the constant as $w_0 = -\eta\theta$, so that $\theta$ is in covariance units: with $\theta > 0$, a synapse grows only where its two neurons covary by more than $\theta$, and otherwise is pruned down to the floor $w_{\min}$. Lateral synapses use $\eta_{II} = \gamma\eta$; since $w_0$ is shared, their effective threshold is $\theta/\gamma$.$\lambda/\eta$ only sets the weight scale and the clock; I use $\lambda = \eta = 1$ and Euler steps of $dt = 0.05$. The floor $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$ keeps $L_{II}$ invertible. It is small but not nothing: every clipped synapse still conducts, so each neuron leaks towards the population mean with total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$. At $w_{\min} = 10^{-2}$ (the "floor too high" tour) that leak kills the network.

Two properties drive everything below. The rule is bounded: each $u_i = h_i^\top g$ is a convex combination of the inputs, so no covariance exceeds $\max_b \Sigma_{bb}$ and no weight exceeds $\max(1,\gamma)\,\eta(\max_b\Sigma_{bb} - \theta)/\lambda$. And it is self-reinforcing: a strong synapse between $i$ and $j$ pulls their embeddings together, neurons with similar embeddings covary more, and that strengthens the synapse further. Whether the interior ends up as one blob or several clusters is a race between this feedback and the pruning.

What to notice

  • The first moments decide. Neurons start spread uniformly over the triangle (their input weights are independent exponentials, their lateral weights tiny). By $t \approx 1$ the lateral synapses have either thickened everywhere, and the cloud contracts to one blob, or only within groups, and it tears into clusters (at speed ×1 that is a third of a second: use ×¼, or scrub back). After that, clusters move as rigid bodies.
  • Members of a cluster are exact clones. At the fixed point they agree to about $10^{-8}$ in $L_1$: the heatmap shows solid blocks, and the 3D view shows one dot (drawn larger) where there are thirty. The dynamics is symmetric under swapping neurons, so identical neurons stay identical forever.
  • Height is not what is being climbed. Corners are the highest points for independent inputs and the centroid the lowest, yet the no-pruning tour settles near the centroid. The pull is towards inputs a neuron already covaries with by more than $\theta$, not up the variance surface.
  • Convergence is visible, but not proof. The residual falls exponentially once the structure is settled; a shoulder before that is a cluster still sliding. The run pauses itself when $r < 10^{-5}$. A tiny residual can also mean the run is balanced on an exactly symmetric state that is unstable; "private jitter" under More controls nudges every synapse independently and lets such a state fall.
  • Between clusters, lateral synapses sit at the floor (panel d), and their covariance in the heatmap is below $\theta/\gamma$; within a cluster, every synapse is alive.

One blob, unless something prunes

With independent inputs and $\theta = 0$ (no pruning), a lone neuron would be happy anywhere in the triangle: nothing is pruned and nothing is preferred. Lateral coupling then makes consensus: every lateral synapse grows, the feedback loop closes, and all the neurons end on one point, the population's starting mean, close to the centroid. That is the note's starting point, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$ with $w_0 \ge 0$. With positively correlated inputs the general statement is sharper: if $\theta$ is below every covariance, $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ is entrywise positive and there is exactly one place a cluster can sit, its full-support Perron vector (all correlated $0.3$, $\theta = 0.1$).

I expected any $\theta > 0$ to give one cluster per input once the corners became the only stable places. It does not. At $\theta = 0.2$ the corners are the only stable parts, yet the interior still becomes one blob, which slides as one body to a single corner. The reason is in the first moments. Two neurons' lateral synapse starts to grow if $\gamma\,h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j > \theta$, and for a random start the average of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$ is

$$\kappa_0 = \frac{\mathbf 1^\top \Sigma_g \mathbf 1}{B^2}\quad(=\tfrac13 \text{ for independent unit inputs}).$$

Below the collective threshold $\theta_c = \gamma\kappa_0$, most lateral synapses grow before neurons have committed to anything, the population binds into one equipotential blob, and a blob behaves like a single neuron: it lands on one part. Above it ($\theta = 0.4$) neurons choose independently and every corner is populated. The page shows $\kappa_0$ and $\gamma\kappa_0$ under the $\theta$ slider.

Key point

There are two thresholds with two jobs. $\theta$ decides which parts exist: where a group of neurons can settle. The lateral gain decides how many of them a densely wired population resolves: below $\theta = \gamma\kappa_0$ it binds and takes one. With $\gamma = 0.5$, $\theta = 0.2$ already gives three corners.

Correlated inputs: parts, not eigenvectors

Now let $g_1$ and $g_2$ correlate, $\rho_{12} = 0.6$, and set $\theta = 0.5$, between the cross-correlation $0.6$ and the uncorrelated level $0$ (a correlated pair). Two clusters form: one at $g_3$, and one at the midpoint of the $g_1$–$g_2$ edge, $h = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)$. The second is not an eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$; it is a non-negative mixture of the inputs that move together, a shared part. Raise $\theta$ to $0.65$, above $\rho_{12}$, and the midpoint stops being stable, but the neurons heading for it are still bound (their self-covariance $0.8$ exceeds $\theta/\gamma$), so they pick one of $g_1$, $g_2$ together. Lower the lateral gain to $\gamma = 0.5$ and they split over all three corners. The binding works level by level, coarse to fine: a group stays together as long as its own covariance beats $\theta/\gamma$.

The maths: what a surviving synapse says at the fixed point, and which parts are stable

At a fixed point every synapse above the floor has zero drift, so with $\eta = \lambda = 1$:

$$\begin{gathered} w_{ib} = (\Sigma_g h_i)_b - \theta \quad\text{(input synapses)}, \\ w_{ij} = \gamma\, h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j - \theta \quad\text{(lateral synapses)},\end{gathered}$$

and a synapse at the floor has $(\Sigma_g h_i)_b \le \theta + w_{\min}$, respectively $\gamma h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j \le \theta + w_{\min}$. Each surviving synapse reads one entry of $H\Sigma_g$ or $H\Sigma_g H^\top$ off its own weight; that is what makes the note's weight-space readout possible.

Now take a cluster whose members share one embedding $h$. Lateral synapses inside it join nodes at equal voltage and carry no current, so up to the floor leak Kirchhoff gives $h_b = w_{ib}/\sum_c w_{ic}$. Write $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ and use $\mathbf 1^\top h = 1$:

$$\begin{gathered} h \;\propto\; (A h)_+ \\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \\ (A h)_b \le 0 \text{ for } b \notin S,\end{gathered}$$

where $S$ is the support of $h$ and $c = \sum_b w_{ib} = (h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$ is a member's summed input conductance (shown as $c$ in the tooltips). So a cluster sits on a non-negative eigenvector of a principal submatrix of $A$; neither $N$, nor $\gamma$, nor the lateral weights appear. It is stable exactly when $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ and the off-support inequality is strict, i.e. when $h$ is a strict local maximum of $h^\top A h / h^\top h$ over non-negative $h$: a local optimum of non-negative PCA of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. Independent inputs: on a support of size $k$ the uniform vector has eigenvalue $1 - k\theta$, below the other eigenvalues ($1$) when $\theta > 0$, so only corners are stable. The pair: on $\{1, 2\}$ the midpoint has eigenvalue $1 + \rho_{12} - 2\theta$ against $1 - \rho_{12}$ for $(1, -1)$, so it is stable exactly when $\theta < \rho_{12}$, and the corner $g_1$ is stable only if $\rho_{12} < \theta$. A corner $g_b$ exists only if $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$. (Derivation and checks: docs/findings.md, F1.)

Anticorrelation is pruning in disguise

Clipping at the floor prunes any synapse whose covariance is negative, whatever $\theta$, so anticorrelated inputs give clusters with almost no pruning drift: $\Sigma_g = I - \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/3$ at $\theta = 0.02$, every pair of inputs correlated $-\tfrac12$, gives three corner clusters. But it is not a separate mechanism. Because every row of $H$ sums to one,

$$\begin{gathered} h_i^\top(\Sigma_g + c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)h_j = h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j + c, \\ \big(H(\Sigma_g + c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)\big)_{ib} = (H\Sigma_g)_{ib} + c,\end{gathered}$$

so with $\gamma = 1$ the pairs $(\Sigma_g, \theta)$ and $(\Sigma_g + c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \theta + c)$ produce identical dynamics: the network only ever sees $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. The anticorrelated tour is exactly independent unit inputs at $\theta = \tfrac13 + 0.02$ (I checked at $\theta = 0$ against $\tfrac13$: the weights agree to $2\times10^{-14}$ after 400 steps), just above the collective threshold $\kappa_0 = \tfrac13$; that is why it needs the small $\theta$, and why it is close enough to the threshold that one seed in three gives two clusters here. "Pruning by anticorrelation" and "pruning by drift" are the same thing: $\theta$ measured from the level the inputs share. (With $\gamma \ne 1$ the two thresholds shift differently and the identity is only approximate. The same symmetry is why $\theta$ cannot be read off the weights later: it looks exactly like a constant added to $\Sigma_g$.)

History and noise

The fixed point you reach depends on how you got there. Run to three corners at $\theta = 0.4$ and then lower $\theta$ to $0.1$: the clusters stay apart, because corners covary by zero, while a fresh start at $0.1$ gives one cluster. Going the other way, a population bound at low $\theta$ does not split when $\theta$ rises past the collective threshold; the findings show that this memory survives private jitter, so it is real and not a symmetry artefact. The start matters too: if the lateral synapses start wired up, the interior is one blob from the outset and stays one. What counts is the initial lateral conductance relative to the input conductance.

I hoped noise would help: with minibatch covariances instead of the exact $\Sigma_g$, perhaps members of a cluster would spread out into a cloud of slightly different features. They do not. With 16 samples per step, every neuron sees the same samples, so the noise is common to all of them and a cluster stays a point. What the noise does do is now and then push two clusters' covariance above $\theta$; they wire together, and merged clusters never separate. Three clusters become two, then one. Shared noise acts as a ratchet towards fewer clusters, and the smaller the batch (relative to $dt$), the faster it turns.The noise per unit time scales like $dt/\text{batch}$. At batch 64 and 256 the three corners lasted to $t = 300$ for every seed I tried; the findings report that batch $\ge 128$ reproduces the mean-field clusters and allocation.

Who gets the neurons?

Every allocation is a fixed point: the condition a cluster has to satisfy (the maths above) does not contain its number of members, so nothing at equilibrium prefers one split over another: three corners ends at 40/30/20 for seed 0 here, 35/31/24 for seed 1, and the findings' runs gave 34/30/26, all converged. The allocation is decided during the transient. Make the inputs unequal, variances $1$, $1.5$ and $2.5$: below the collective threshold the bound population goes entirely to the loudest input; with no lateral plasticity every input is populated, but very unequally. An input whose variance is below $\theta$ is not a part at all and gets nothing. The controlled sweeps in docs/findings.md (F6) put a number on the tilt: in the independent regime the share goes like $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta(\theta)}$, a power of the part's top eigenvalue with $\beta$ falling from about 7 to 1.5 as $\theta$ rises, and a hard cutoff where a part's supra-threshold energy vanishes. So the note's "spectral filter" exists, but it is set by the transient and the starting distribution, not by the equilibrium; the Homeostasis chapter measures it.

What this already tells us about the conjecture

What we found (three inputs, dense, mean-field)

  • holdsThe embeddings separate into a few discrete clusters at a fixed point, and how many is set by the homeostatic parameters ($\theta$, $\gamma$, and the floor $\omega = N w_{\min}$).
  • amendedClusters need pruning: a constant drift $w_0 = -\eta\theta < 0$, or negative input covariances, which are the same thing up to $\Sigma_g \to \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. And with lateral plasticity, $\theta$ must exceed the collective threshold $\gamma\kappa_0$, or the population binds into one blob first.
  • amendedCluster positions are parts: corners, or the midpoint of a correlated pair. Each is a non-negative Perron vector of a principal submatrix of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, with an exact stability criterion. They are not signed eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$.
  • fails"Each neuron in a cluster is a random feature": with dense synapses the members of a cluster are exact clones. That is redundancy, not a reservoir. Shared minibatch noise does not diversify them either; it merges clusters.
  • amendedAllocation: any allocation is a fixed point. The transient tilts it towards louder parts ($n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta(\theta)}$ when neurons choose independently, winner-takes-all when they are bound), and parts quieter than $\theta$ get nothing.

Caveat

Everything here is one tiny world: $B = 3$, all-to-all symmetric synapses, a linear (resistor) network, a floor at $w_{\min}$, Euler steps of $0.05$, and the specific random start described above. The first twelve tours are the verified presets of docs/findings.md (three seeds there); I re-ran each in this page's engine for seeds 0–2 and they agree on the number and position of clusters, except that one anticorrelated seed gives two clusters instead of three. Cluster sizes differ from the findings' runs because the random starts differ, which is itself one of the findings.
