The first version of this page took the note's rule as written: Hebb's covariance rule, on a network of linear resistors. I simulated it, swept it, and derived what could be derived. The rule does organise the network into clusters. But almost everything about those clusters turned out to be degenerate, and that is why the rest of this page uses a different model. This chapter condenses what the old rule did, for a reader who has not seen the first version.

The model

Neurons are nodes of a resistor network and synapses are symmetric conductances $w_{ij} \ge 0$. $B$ input neurons are clamped to the stimulus $g$, which has covariance $\Sigma_g$; the $N$ interior neurons settle where Kirchhoff's laws put them,

$$u = H g, \qquad H = L_{II}^{-1} W_{IB},$$

where $L_{II}$ is the interior block of the graph Laplacian and $W_{IB}$ the interior-to-input conductances. Row $i$ of $H$ is neuron $i$'s harmonic embedding $h_i$. It is non-negative and sums to one, so it is a point in the simplex whose corners are the inputs, and it depends on the weights only. Every interior voltage is therefore a fixed linear function of the input, $u_i = h_i^\top g$, and the covariance that Hebb's rule needs is an inner product of embeddings. The rule, with a regulariser that depends on the weight only, is

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = w_0 - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j), \\ \mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}.\end{gathered}$$

A synapse onto input $b$ sees $(H\Sigma_g)_{ib}$ instead. Write $w_0 = -\eta\theta$: with $\theta > 0$ a synapse grows only where its two neurons covary by more than $\theta$, and otherwise decays to the floor $w_{\min}$. Lateral synapses learn with gain $\gamma\eta$, so their threshold is $\theta/\gamma$.In the live figure and the synthetic sweeps: $\lambda = \eta = 1$, Euler steps $dt = 0.05$, $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$ (the MNIST and private-noise experiments used their own settings, listed in docs/v1/). The floor is not innocent: every clipped synapse still conducts, so each neuron leaks towards the population mean with total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$, and a floor that is too high kills the network.

Because every row of $H$ sums to one, adding a constant $c$ to every entry of $\Sigma_g$ adds exactly $c$ to every covariance the rule sees. With $\gamma = 1$ the pairs $(\Sigma_g, \theta)$ and $(\Sigma_g + c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \theta + c)$ give identical dynamics: the network only ever sees the shifted covariance

$$A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top.$$

So pruning by a drift $\theta$ and pruning by negative covariances are one mechanism: $\theta$ is measured from the level the inputs share.

Watch it

Here is the old rule on three inputs, so that every embedding is a point in a triangle. The tours set up each of the findings below; the text links to them.

Without pruning, one cluster

With independent inputs and no pruning, a lone neuron would be content anywhere in the triangle. But every lateral synapse grows, the feedback closes, and all 90 neurons end on one point near the centroid. With positively correlated inputs the statement is exact: if $\theta$ is below every entry of $\Sigma_g$, then $A$ is entrywise positive and there is exactly one place a cluster can sit, the full-support Perron vector of $A$. That covers the note's starting point, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$ with $w_0 \ge 0$, for any inputs whose covariances are all positive.

Pruning is necessary but not sufficient. At $\theta = 0.2$ the three corners are the only stable places for a cluster, yet the interior still becomes one blob, which slides as one body to a single corner. A lateral synapse that starts near zero grows if $\gamma\,h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j > \theta$ (exactly, the drive must also beat $\lambda w_{ij}/\eta$), and for independent random starting embeddings the mean of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$ is $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$ ($\tfrac13$ here). From such a start, below $\theta \approx \gamma\kappa_0$ the population binds before any neuron has committed, and a bound population behaves like a single neuron. Above it ($\theta = 0.4$) neurons choose independently and every corner is populated. This crossover is a heuristic read off the initial drift, not a derived phase boundary: it moves with the starting weights, and a population that is already bound can stay bound above it. In the sweeps it predicted every tested transition to within 0.03–0.08.

On real data the window between “one blob” and “nothing” was narrow. On MNIST pixels, raising $\theta$ went from one part to a few, then to a growing pool of dead neurons beside small surviving parts, then to extinction; the minibatch runs on the default grid never populated more than about a dozen parts (one mean-field run reached 16).

Clusters are parts, not eigenvectors

The note expected one cluster per eigenmode of the input. What a cluster actually sits on is fixed by a short calculation. Inside a cluster all members share one embedding $h$, so they are at equal voltage for every stimulus and the lateral synapses among them carry no current. If the synapses to other clusters are pruned to the floor, and the floor is negligible, no lateral current reaches the cluster at all. Kirchhoff then gives $h_b \propto w_{ib}$, and the fixed point of the rule (2) on the input synapses gives

$$\begin{gathered}A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \qquad c > 0, \qquad h_S > 0, \\ (A h)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S,\end{gathered}$$

where $S$ is the support of $h$ and $c = (h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$ is a member's summed input conductance. A cluster is a non-negative eigenvector of a principal submatrix of $A$: a part. Neither $N$, nor $\gamma$, nor the lateral weights appear; a finite floor adds small corrections. A part is stable if $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ and the off-support inequality is strict; this test is sufficient, not necessary (a part with zero off-support margin can still be stable). In words, a stable part is a local maximum of non-negative PCA of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$: a thresholded Perron vector, localised on a support whose size $\theta$ sets.

Two worked cases on three inputs

Independent unit inputs. On a support of size $k$, $A_{SS} = I - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$; the uniform vector has eigenvalue $1 - k\theta$ and the others have $1$. For $\theta > 0$ the uniform vector is not the top one unless $k = 1$, so for $0 < \theta < 1$ only the corners are stable parts (each with $c = 1 - \theta$); for $\theta \ge 1$ there are none and the network dies.

A correlated pair ($\rho_{12}$, unit variances, $g_3$ independent). On $\{1, 2\}$ the midpoint $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ has eigenvalue $1 + \rho_{12} - 2\theta$ and $(1, -1)$ has $1 - \rho_{12}$, so the midpoint is a stable part exactly when $\theta < \rho_{12}$. In general a corner $g_b$ is a part if and only if $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every other input $a$; here the corner $g_1$ needs $\rho_{12} \le \theta$.

With $g_1$ and $g_2$ correlated at $0.6$ and $\theta = 0.5$, one cluster sits at $g_3$ and one at the midpoint of the $g_1$–$g_2$ edge. In this small case the midpoint happens to lie along the top eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$, but the signed eigenvector $(1, -1, 0)$ has no cluster, and the number of clusters is set by $\theta$, not by the spectrum. Raise $\theta$ above $\rho_{12}$ and the midpoint stops being a part; the corners $g_1$ and $g_2$, neither of which is an eigenvector of this $\Sigma_g$, become parts instead. With $\gamma = 1$ the group heading for the midpoint is still bound and picks one of them; with $\gamma = 0.5$ both are populated.

With more inputs the difference is plain. For 12 inputs in three correlated blocks, and for a hierarchy of 8, the parts lie in the span of the top eigenvectors but are its non-negative, localised rotation: the best single eigenvector has cosine 0.71–0.74 with a part. On a ring of 16 inputs the eigenvectors are Fourier modes, while the parts are localised bumps, place fields whose width shrinks from 10 inputs to 1 as $\theta$ rises from 0.07 to 0.85; the best eigenvector's cosine falls from 0.76 to 0.46. A part is close to an eigenvector only in special cases, such as a non-negative top eigenvector at low $\theta$ (cosine 0.96 in a non-negative factor model).

Members of a cluster are clones

The note hoped that each cluster would be a reservoir: many neurons, each a different random feature of its mode. Watch the member-spread tile in any tour: by the time the run converges it is below $10^{-6}$, and the heatmap shows solid blocks. With dense synapses, every member of every cluster ended as an exact copy of the others in every condition tested (member-to-centroid $L_1$ below $10^{-5}$ for floors from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$, lateral gains 0–2, $N$ from 30 to 240, and minibatches from 8 to 512 samples; about $10^{-5}$ on MNIST). The symmetry of the rule under swapping neurons only proves that identical neurons stay identical; that neurons which start apart become identical is an empirical finding.

Two things were tried to break this. Sparse random wiring without lateral plasticity does leave a few distinct functions per input block, but as separate clusters that are each internally cloned: the participation ratio of a block's voltages rose from 1 to 2.06 as the connection probability fell to 0.15. That is bounded by the dimension of the mode, not a reservoir. Private noise on each synapse makes members differ at any instant but not persistently: the correlation between a neuron's deviation from its cluster mean in successive quarters of a run was −0.02 to 0.04, that is, jitter. There is also a structural reason. A neuron in this network computes $u_i = h_i^\top g$, a linear function; a neuron whose embedding is a part computes the same function as every other neuron on that part, and even if they differed slightly they would only be different linear mixtures of the same few inputs.

Allocation is inherited from the transient

The note guessed that the homeostatic parameters would set a spectral filter, a number of neurons per mode as a function of its energy. At the fixed point there is no such filter. The part equation (4) does not contain the number of members, so for mutually compatible, disconnected parts any split of the neurons among them is a fixed point, in the limit of a zero floor; a finite floor couples the clusters and makes the equilibria only weakly, $O(\omega/c)$, dependent on the split. Compare seed 0 (40, 30 and 20 neurons) with seed 1 (35, 31 and 24): both converge, to different allocations.

So allocation is decided on the way. With a zero floor, a lone neuron, in rescaled time, runs continuous non-negative power iteration on $A$ from its initial weights (a small floor perturbs this), and where it ends depends on where it starts. Louder parts tend to win more neurons: with unequal variances and no lateral plasticity every input is populated, very unequally. In the families swept, with neurons choosing independently, the fraction of neurons on part $k$ fitted $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$, where $\lambda_k$ is the top eigenvalue of $\Sigma_g$ on the part's support and $\beta$ falls from about 7 to 1.5 as $\theta$ rises from 0 to the smallest variance. That is a fit on the tested families, not a law: $\beta$ also moves with the spread of the initial weights, and below the collective crossover a bound population goes entirely to one part. Nor is the drift gradient descent on a potential (the antisymmetric part of its Jacobian was a median 8–9% of the symmetric part). The lone neuron does climb the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ when the floor is zero, but that only says which parts are stable, not how many neurons each gets; no objective that selects an allocation was found.

Noise can make allocation a property of the parameters rather than the start. With private noise on every synapse, of the input-sampling or additive kind and reflected properly at the floor, populations at moderate noise levels forget where they started. But the stationary law, in the families tested, is winner-take-all: at low noise, where the parts stay intact, at least 99% of the neurons are nearest to the loudest part (with up to 9% of them between parts at any moment). Allocation becomes graded only at noise levels that keep a large fraction of the neurons between parts: for input-sampling noise on independent inputs of six variances it then fitted $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$, with $\beta$ falling from about 7 to 2 as the noise grew; additive noise fitted an exponential in $\lambda_k$ instead. Noise proportional to the weight did not equilibrate at the default floor: in the cases computed, a pruned synapse's mean escape time was $e^{112}$ time units or longer. With lateral plasticity, private noise mostly gave one bound cluster on the loudest part, from any start.

Rectified output adds a preference, not a better code

The old rule sees only second moments, because voltages are linear in the input. One extension kept Kirchhoff linear but let plasticity see firing rates, $r_i = \mathrm{ReLU}(u_i - \mathbb E u_i - \tau\,\mathrm{sd}\,u_i)$, rectified at each neuron's own mean (or $\tau$ standard deviations above it). For Gaussian inputs this provably adds no information: the rule becomes the old one with $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$ replaced by a known, convex function of the cosine between $h_i$ and $h_j$ in the $\Sigma_g$ geometry, scaled by the two norms. That changes the geometry of the fixed points (on a synthetic ring, the same pairwise threshold gave 10 clusters instead of 2–3) but still depends on $\Sigma_g$ only.

On MNIST pixels the higher moments matter, in a recognisable way. The rectified rule prefers heavy-tailed projections: its dominant part is two vertical bars, the sides of a “0”, where the linear rule's is the central slanted stroke, and the skewness of the part's voltage is 1.37–1.54 against 0.34–0.49. On a Gaussian surrogate with the same mean and covariance, with $\theta$ set at the same quantile of the surrogate's own rate covariances, the preference vanishes (cosine 0.997–0.998 to the linear part). That is projection pursuit, the classical behaviour of nonlinear Hebbian rules. It did not give a better code. The rectified network stayed one cluster up to thresholds where the linear one had 3–6, and a linear probe for the digit class on its 980 rates scored 0.20–0.25, against 0.38–0.57 for the linear rule, 0.62 for the untrained network, 0.925 for the raw pixels and 0.97 for 980 random ReLU features.

The weights store a sketch of the parts, not $\Sigma_g$

The note's most striking claim was that the weights can be read backwards: each surviving synapse states a covariance, so solving $H C H^\top = R$ should recover $\Sigma_g$. At a clustered fixed point the weights are a function of the parts, their conductances and their sizes, plus inequalities from the synapses at the floor. Each active input synapse gives one equation, $(\Sigma_g h_k)_b = \theta + c_k h_{kb}$ on part $k$'s support; the lateral synapses add only the energy of each populated part. Clones multiply the equations but not their rank. For 12 inputs in blocks, the measurement operator had rank 5–24 out of the 78 independent entries of $\Sigma_g$; in the headline MNIST run, 67 out of 19,306. A natural estimator, the parts-sum $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k h_k h_k^\top/\lVert h_k\rVert^2$, has rank at most $K + 1$ for $K$ parts.

The readout is therefore accurate on what the weights measure and blind elsewhere. On MNIST the part energies matched $\Sigma_g$ to about three decimals, and in the headline run the recovered matrix had cosine 0.84 with $\Sigma_g$ on the 30 of 196 pixels that the parts covered. On the whole contrast subspace the cosine was 0.17–0.61 across the live runs, about the same as simply taking the diagonal of $\Sigma_g$ (0.52) for pixels. These are scores of a readout given the true $\theta$ and $\lambda/\eta$; recovery without them was not shown. In the Bures–Wasserstein distance, which charges for missing variance, the equilibrium readout never beat the identity matrix. It was better mid-training, before pruning had finished (contrast cosine 0.90–0.95 on the block covariance), but there the equilibrium equations it relies on do not hold. And even a perfect centred covariance would not give the distribution of stimuli the note hoped for: the mean is lost, and, for example, two-atom mixtures with weights $p$ and $1 - p$ have the same centred feature covariance up to a factor $p(1 - p)$.

What the old rule did, against the note

  • holdsEmbeddings separate into a few discrete clusters at a fixed point, and how many is set by the homeostatic parameters ($\theta$, $\gamma$ and the floor $\omega = N w_{\min}$).
  • amendedClusters need pruning (a drift $\theta > 0$, or negative covariances, which are the same thing), and with lateral plasticity, from a fresh random start, a population with $\theta$ below roughly $\gamma\kappa_0$ (a heuristic crossover that moves with the start) binds into one cluster.
  • amendedClusters sit on parts, non-negative localised Perron vectors of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a support, not on eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$.
  • failsEach cluster as a reservoir of random features: with dense synapses the members are exact clones in every tested condition; sparse wiring gives a few distinct functions, private noise only jitter.
  • failsA spectral filter set by the homeostatic parameters: at equilibrium any allocation among compatible parts is a fixed point (up to the floor), so allocation comes from the start; private noise turns it into a law that, in the tested families, is winner-take-all.
  • amendedWeight-space readout: accurate on the populated parts and blind elsewhere, a sketch of the parts rather than $\Sigma_g$.
  • amendedRectified output (an extension, not in the note): a preference for heavy-tailed projections on real data, but not a better code.

Why a new model

These degeneracies have common causes, and each points to a change.

The objects being clustered were too poor. A harmonic embedding is a probability vector over the inputs, and a neuron's response is the linear function $h_i^\top g$. Two neurons on the same part compute the same function, so a cluster cannot be a set of different features of its mode. The new model clusters whole response functions $F_i(g)$, each neuron's equilibrium voltage as a function of the input, compared by an exact geometry, the mean squared distance $D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g[(F_i - F_j)^2]$.

The rule descended no objective. Its drift has a curl, its outcome was decided by the transient, and pruning had to be added as a constant. In the new model the rule is $dw_{ij}/dt = b - \alpha w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2$, which by the envelope theorem is gradient descent on one energy: the network's equilibrium energy, averaged over inputs, plus a regulariser on the weights. Its fixed point is explicit, $w_{ij} = (b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+/\alpha$, and pruning is built in: an edge lives exactly when $D_{ij}^2 < 2b$. For linear neurons and zero-mean inputs, with the expectation over the input distribution, $-\tfrac12 D_{ij}^2 = h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \tfrac12\big(h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_i + h_j^\top\Sigma_g h_j\big)$: the old covariance term, minus the two neurons' variances. Neurons are drawn together by being alike, not by being loud. (The energy brings its own bias: in prototypes where the attachments to the inputs were made plastic under it, with each neuron's total input conductance held fixed, they drifted to the quietest inputs, which is why the new model keeps them fixed.)

The neurons were linear. In the new model each neuron has an on-site potential $V_i(u_i)$; its derivative, added to the neuron's leak, is the inverse of the neuron's transfer function. The default is a two-slope quadratic, a leaky rectifier with a threshold $t_i$ of the neuron's own; it is piecewise linear, so equilibria are exact. Neurons with the same attachments but different thresholds respond differently, which gives a cluster the chance to hold different functions that the old model never had.

None of this guarantees that the degeneracies go away. Whether clusters now hold diverse neurons, whether allocation follows a law, and how much of the input statistics the weights record are what the chapters before this one test, and they say where the basic model still falls short.
