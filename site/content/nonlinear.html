So far every neuron was linear: its voltage was a fixed mixture of the inputs, $h_i \cdot g$, and two neurons on the same part computed the same function. This chapter gives each neuron an on-site potential, which makes it a nonlinear unit with a transfer function of its own. The distance rule and its exact fixed point carry over unchanged, now in the space of response functions; the weights still state exact response distances. What does not follow is the hoped-for payoff: neurons within a cluster differ only slightly, and there is still no allocation law.

The on-site potential is a transfer function

Give each interior neuron a convex on-site potential $V_i$. The energy of the network, counting each pair once, becomes

$$\begin{aligned}E_W(u; g) = {}& \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\,(u_i - u_j)^2 \\ &+ \tfrac12 \sum_{i,b} a_{ib}\,(u_i - g_b)^2 + \sum_i V_i(u_i),\end{aligned}$$

and for a given stimulus the voltages relax to its unique minimum (strictly convex once every neuron has an attachment), where

$$\begin{gathered}d_i\,u_i + V_i'(u_i) = I_i, \\ I_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\,u_j + \sum_b a_{ib}\,g_b, \qquad d_i = \sum_j w_{ij} + \sum_b a_{ib}.\end{gathered}$$

Read it as a neuron. A current $I_i$ arrives through the synapses and attachments, and the neuron answers with the voltage $u_i = (d_i\,\mathrm{id} + V_i')^{-1}(I_i)$: the derivative of the potential, added to the leak $d_i u$, is the inverse transfer function. Convexity bounds the gain: the slope is $1/(d_i + V_i''(u))$, never more than $1/d_i$. The default here is a two-slope quadratic with a threshold $t_i$ of the neuron's own,

$$V_i(u) = \tfrac{k_{lo}}{2}\,(u - t_i)_-^2 + \tfrac{k_{hi}}{2}\,(u - t_i)_+^2 ,$$

a leaky rectifier when $k_{hi} = 0$: below threshold the gain drops from $1/d_i$ to $1/(d_i + k_{lo})$. It is piecewise quadratic, so equilibria are computed exactly. Thresholds are drawn per neuron, $t_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_t^2)$; with $\sigma_t = 0$ every neuron has the same transfer function (the "homogeneous rectifier").

The equilibrium voltage of neuron $i$, as a function of the stimulus, is its response function $F_i(g)$; it depends on the weights. For linear neurons $F_i(g) = h_i \cdot g$ and response functions are the harmonic embeddings; with a potential they are nonlinear functions of the input, and two neurons with the same attachments but different thresholds respond differently (panel (b)). That is the room for diversity the linear model lacked.

What carries over

The derivation of the rule does not use linearity. The envelope theorem gives $\partial_{w_{ij}} \min_u E_W = \tfrac12 (F_i - F_j)^2$ whatever the potentials, so the distance rule is still exact gradient descent on the mean equilibrium energy plus the weight cost,

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2, \qquad D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g\big[(F_i - F_j)^2\big],$$

with the same exact fixed point $w_{ij} = (b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+/\alpha$: a live edge certifies $D_{ij}^2 = 2(b - \alpha w_{ij})$, a dead one $D_{ij}^2 \ge 2b$. Only the space changes. $D_{ij}$ is now the $L^2$ distance between whole response functions, so clusters form in response-function space, and every figure of it below is a classical MDS or principal-axes projection of measured response distances, labelled so. What no longer holds is linearity in the input statistics: $D_{ij}^2$ is not a quadratic form in any fixed matrix of input moments, so the linear inversion of the covariance readout does not carry over; a nonlinear inversion was not tested.

Linear and rectifier, side by side

To see what the potential changes, the runs below share everything else (the matched protocol): the seed, the attachments ($s = 3$ random inputs per neuron), the start $W = 0$, the training bank of 256 stimuli, the evaluation bank of 4096, and $b$ and $\alpha$. One arm is linear, one is the rectifier ($k_{lo} = 3$, $k_{hi} = 0$, $\sigma_t = 0.7$); a third, the homogeneous rectifier ($\sigma_t = 0$), is a control that is in the table but not filmed. The settings are named after the linear arm's regime at that $b$.

At the same $b$ the two arms are not in the same regime. Where inputs are few (family A), threshold spread makes the rectifier fuse much later: at the linear arm's percolation point it is still almost all singletons. With twelve inputs the two fuse at about the same $b$ (the rectifier a little later on the blocks, a little earlier on the power law). The homogeneous rectifier fuses much earlier in every case: a common threshold roughly halves the squared distances between uncoupled neurons, while spread thresholds push the closest pairs apart. Matched by edge density instead of by $b$ (labelled as such in dist_*.json), the answer depends on the inputs and the regime: in family A the rectifier needs a $b$ 2–6 times larger; in family B about 2 times larger while fragmented, about the same near percolation on the blocks, and a smaller one (0.6–0.9 times) on the power law from percolation to fusion. Near percolation both arms form chains several edge ranges long.

What the weights say: a partial response geometry

Without a linear inverse problem, what remains is the fixed point itself, and three kinds of pair: exactly known (live edges, $D_{ij} = \sqrt{2(b - \alpha w_{ij})}$); bounded on both sides (same component, no edge: $D$ is a metric, so the shortest path through live edges bounds it above, and $D \ge \sqrt{2b}$ below); and bounded below only (different components). The figure trains a small rectifying network in the page and shows all three, with a classical MDS of the measured distances (response-function space) beside the MDS of the weights' completion (an approximation: live edges exact, shortest paths elsewhere).

On the big runs (the rectifier arm, $\sigma_t = 0.7$, $\alpha = 1$: all … runs of both families, regime and grid values of $b$), live synapses state response distances exactly on the training bank (error at most …, the larger values where tiny distances amplify the solver's residual through the square root) and to within a median …–… on held-out inputs, which is sampling; dead pairs obey their lower bound for at least … of cross-component pairs on held-out inputs. Together they fix a partial geometry. Its MDS is a good picture once most pairs are live edges (fused blocks: disparity …) and a poor one near percolation, where shortest paths overestimate distances inside chains (disparity …). These are distances between response functions: the linear covariance inversion does not apply to them, and a nonlinear inversion to input statistics was not tested.

Diversity within clusters: real, and small

The note hoped that each cluster would be an expansion code: many neurons on one mode, each a different function of it. Two measures, both on the held-out evaluation bank: per-neuron nonlinearity, the relative error of the best affine fit of a neuron's response from the full input (0 for a linear neuron); and within-cluster diversity, the relative error of the best fit $F_i \approx a + c\,F_{\text{proto}}$ of each member from its component's mean response, weighted by component size. The second is meaningful only where components are compact; in a chain several edge ranges long it measures the chain's width, not diversity, so the figure shows it only where the largest component spans less than two edge ranges.

In compact components (span at most two edge ranges) of the fragmented and fused regimes of family B, the rectifier's members differ from their prototype by …–… of their variance, against …–… for linear neurons (exact affine copies in principle; that residue is the components' spread) and …–… for the homogeneous rectifier. In family A's fragmented regime, where components are mostly pairs, every arm stays at or below …. That is an order of magnitude more than linear, and still small. It agrees in size with the controlled experiment of the diversity appendix, where every neuron is driven by one shared mode and members differ by about 2% of variance (that number belongs to that experiment). Fusion also linearises the heterogeneous rectifier: its per-neuron nonlinearity falls from about 0.09 near $W = 0$ to … at the fused $b$ of family B, as neurons share ever more current with their neighbours, and the members become copies of one another. (The homogeneous rectifier stays nonlinear, about 0.115, but its members are copies too.) Threshold spread delays fusion, as above, but does not escape this: the reason is structural, since a live edge certifies $D_{ij}^2 < 2b$, so being fused and being different are one quantity pulling in opposite directions.

The bottom line

What nonlinear neurons give, in the basic model

  • holdsThe distance rule is still exact gradient descent on the mean equilibrium energy with the potentials, and its fixed point $w = (b - D^2/2)_+/\alpha$ still holds exactly, now in response-function space (derived; residuals $\le 10^{-9}$ in every run).
  • holdsThe weights state exact response distances on live edges and lower bounds on dead pairs: a partial response geometry, whose MDS is good once fused and poor near percolation.
  • openA readout of input statistics: the linear covariance inversion does not carry over to response distances; a nonlinear inversion was not tested.
  • failsAn expansion code: within-cluster diversity is real but small, about 1–2% of variance (compact components, family B, heterogeneous thresholds; at most 0.07% in family A's fragmented pairs), and fusion linearises the heterogeneous rectifier; threshold spread delays fusion but does not change this.
  • failsAn allocation law or spectral filter: the attachments decide how many neurons are directly attached to each group of inputs; a neuron's response support can be wider, since lateral synapses carry other inputs (Allocation).

Nonlinearity keeps the geometry and the readout of distances, and adds a little diversity. It does not deliver the expansion code or the spectral filter in the basic model; both would need an ingredient the energy does not contain. The appendix has the details: Fusion (two-neuron bistability, singletons to chains to one blob, live), Many inputs (hysteresis and the resolution law), Diversity (the controlled test, and clusters against eigenmodes) and Allocation.
