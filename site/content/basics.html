Hebbian learning, "neurons that fire together wire together", changes each synapse using only the activity of the two neurons it joins. Nothing computes a loss and hands it down. So what does the network end up doing? In the simple model on this page you can watch the answer: every neuron is a fixed mixture of a few inputs, a point in a triangle, and Hebb's rule moves those points until they gather into groups.

The model: a resistor network

Think of the neurons as nodes of an electrical circuit. Neuron $i$ has a voltage $u_i$; a synapse is a resistor whose conductance $w_{ij} \ge 0$ is the synaptic weight. A few nodes are inputs, with voltages $g_1, g_2, g_3$ set from outside. Every other neuron settles where the currents into it balance, at the weighted average of its neighbours' voltages (inputs included):

$$u_i = \frac{\sum_j w_{ij}\,u_j}{\sum_j w_{ij}} .$$

Everything here is linear (no thresholds, no saturation) and the synapses are symmetric. We assume every neuron is joined to some input by a chain of synapses, so the resting state is unique. The inputs are synthetic random numbers with a chosen covariance.

Each neuron is a mixture of the inputs

Averaging is linear, so at rest every neuron's voltage is a fixed weighted sum of the inputs:

$$u_i = H_{i1}\,g_1 + H_{i2}\,g_2 + H_{i3}\,g_3, \qquad u = H g .$$

The row $h_i = (H_{i1}, H_{i2}, H_{i3})$ depends only on the wiring. Its entries are never negative and add up to one (if every input sat at the same voltage, so would every neuron). So $h_i$ is a mixture, "50% $g_1$, 20% $g_2$, 30% $g_3$": the neuron's harmonic embedding, named after harmonic functions, which equal the average of their neighbours. ($H$ is known as the discrete Poisson kernel.)

A mixture of three things is a point in a triangle with the inputs at the corners. A neuron's share of $g_1$ is its distance from the side opposite the $g_1$ corner, as a fraction of the triangle's height: on the corner it is all $g_1$, on the opposite side none, in the middle a third of each.

The rest of the site keeps these two pictures apart. In neuron space a neuron is a node drawn at a random place. In input space it is the point $h_i$, which is all there is to know about what it computes: two neurons at the same point settle to the same voltage for every input. One way to read a mixture: start a random walk at neuron $i$ that crosses each synapse with probability proportional to its conductance; $H_{ib}$ is the probability that it reaches input $b$ before any other input.

Wiring pulls a neuron towards its neighbours

The averaging rule (1) holds for mixtures too: each neuron's point is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours' points, an input counting as a neighbour on its own corner. So a synapse acts like a spring in the triangle.

$$h_i = \frac{\sum_j w_{ij}\,h_j}{\sum_j w_{ij}} .$$

Firing together is overlap of mixtures

Let the inputs fluctuate with covariance matrix $\Sigma_g$ (entry $bc$ is $\mathrm{Cov}(g_b, g_c)$). Since $u_i = h_i \cdot g$, how much two neurons fire together is fixed by their mixtures:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = h_i^\top\, \Sigma_g\, h_j .$$

This is a covariance-weighted overlap of the two mixtures. For independent inputs of unit variance it is the plain overlap $\sum_b H_{ib} H_{jb}$; correlated inputs make neurons on different corners fire together too.

Hebb's rule, watched in the triangle

Hebb's rule strengthens a synapse whose two neurons fire together and weakens the others. Here it runs on 60 neurons and three inputs; the rule itself is written out below the figure.

Hebb's rule grows a synapse with the covariance of its two neurons, at a learning rate $\eta$, plus a regulariser $\rho(w) = -\eta\theta - \lambda w$ with threshold $\theta$ and decay rate $\lambda$. A synapse grows while $\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) > \theta + (\lambda/\eta)\,w_{ij}$ and shrinks when the covariance is below that, though never below a small floor $w_{\min}$; synapses onto the inputs learn the same way, from $\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, g_b)$.

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = \eta\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) + \rho(w_{ij}) = \eta\big(h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j - \theta\big) - \lambda\,w_{ij}, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}.$$

The rule looks only at pairs of neurons, but what it reads, covariance, is set by their mixtures (4), and what it changes, wiring, moves the mixtures (3). So it is really a motion of points in input space.

Nobody wrote down a loss function: every synapse followed its own local rule, and the groups came out anyway. Where they sit and how many there are is the subject of the next page, Perron clusters.

A cousin with an energy

Reading the weights uses a variant, the distance rule, in which only synapses between neurons learn, with growth rate $b > 0$ and decay rate $\alpha > 0$:

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha\,w_{ij} - \tfrac12\,\mathbb E\big[(u_i - u_j)^2\big], \qquad w_{ij} \ge 0 .$$

It is exactly gradient descent on one global quantity $J$: half the power the network dissipates, averaged over inputs, plus a cost per synapse (derivation in Details).

At its fixed point, $w_{ij} = \big(b - \tfrac12\mathbb E[(u_i - u_j)^2]\big)_+/\alpha$: a synapse survives only between neurons whose root-mean-square voltage difference is below $\sqrt{2b}$, and its weight records how close they are, which is what makes the weights readable. The covariance rule (5) is not gradient descent on this objective, and no global objective is known for it.
