Suppose you have a trained network's synaptic weights, you know how its neurons are wired to the inputs, and you know the learning rule and its constants. Can you work out what the network was trained on? For linear neurons and the distance rule of The basics, largely yes: from those three ingredients you can solve for the second moment of the training inputs (the average of $g g^\top$), apart from one part that the weights cannot see. The inputs here are synthetic and zero-mean by construction, so this second moment is their covariance up to sampling: 256 Gaussian samples of 12 inputs $g_1, \dots, g_{12}$ that fall into four correlated groups of three, loud to quiet.

Each synapse is a measurement

Each of $N$ neurons is wired to three of the $B = 12$ inputs by fixed wires $A$, and the neurons are connected to each other by plastic synapses $w_{ij} \ge 0$. A neuron's response is a fixed mixture of the inputs, $u_i = h_i^\top g$, and its mixture $h_i$ (its harmonic embedding) is set by the weights and the wiring. The synapses learn by the distance rule

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha\, w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2, \qquad D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E\big[(u_i - u_j)^2\big],$$

where $D_{ij}^2$ is the mean squared difference between the two neurons' responses over the training inputs, $b > 0$ is a constant growth term and $\alpha > 0$ a decay rate. When learning has stopped, the rule can be read backwards:

$$\begin{aligned} w_{ij} > 0 &:\quad D_{ij}^2 = 2\,(b - \alpha\, w_{ij}),\\ w_{ij} = 0 &:\quad D_{ij}^2 \ge 2b . \end{aligned}$$

A live synapse states exactly how differently its two neurons respond; a pruned one says only that they respond differently by at least $2b$.

The distance is a quadratic form in the second moment $C$: $D_{ij}^2 = (h_i - h_j)^\top C\, (h_i - h_j)$. And the mixtures can be computed from the weights: $H = \big(L + \operatorname{diag}(A\mathbf 1)\big)^{-1} A$, where $H$ has rows $h_i$, $L$ is the graph Laplacian of the weights and $A$ the input wiring. So each live synapse gives one linear equation on $C$:

$$(h_i - h_j)^\top C\, (h_i - h_j) = 2\,(b - \alpha\, w_{ij}) .$$

Collect the equations of all live synapses and solve them by least squares for the part of $C$ they can see. That is the whole recipe, and it is what the first figure did.

Settling removes the drift error

During learning, the live-synapse equality in (2) is off by exactly $2\,dw_{ij}/dt$, the synapse's remaining drift. So a readout taken mid-training carries this drift error, and the error shrinks as the weights stop moving. What remains at the end depends on whether the equations are numerous and independent enough.

Many more neurons than inputs

The second moment of 12 inputs has 78 distinct entries, and 66 independent combinations of them are visible to the weights (next section). Each live synapse adds one equation, and in the runs below the number of live synapses grows much faster than the number of neurons. The equations determine the visible part once they span all 66 directions; to recover it accurately they must also be well conditioned.

What the weights cannot see

Every neuron's mixture sums to one, $h_i^\top \mathbf 1 = 1$ with $\mathbf 1$ the all-ones vector, so the difference of two mixtures sums to zero. A signal added equally to all inputs therefore moves every neuron by the same amount and changes no distance $D_{ij}$, and no weight. Written as matrices, the part of $C$ of the form $\mathbf 1 a^\top + a \mathbf 1^\top$ drops out of every equation (3). What the weights record is the rest, $P C P$ with $P = I - \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top / B$: the second moment of the inputs after subtracting, sample by sample, their average.

It depends on knowing the rule

The recipe inverts the fixed point of one particular rule, so it needs that rule's exact form and constants. The covariance rule with pruning (of Perron clusters) also has a fixed point that is linear in $C$, and the same approach applies: each surviving synapse, input synapses included, gives one equation. Unlike the distance rule, these equations can see the shared part too. But in the pruned run below the neurons form two groups with nearly identical mixtures, so most of the equations repeat one another.

Knowing the distance rule's constants matters too. In the 80-neuron example, assuming $b$ 10% too large gives an error of …, assuming $\alpha$ 10% too large …, and multiplying every weight by $1 + 0.1\,\xi$ ($\xi$ standard normal) …. That network is in …. Strongly fused networks (large $b$) are more fragile, because their weights sit near $b/\alpha$ and the distances they encode are small next to a weight perturbation: in a precomputed sweep at $N = 80$ (data/sweeps2/readout.json, scored by cosine similarity rather than relative error), 1% weight noise leaves a cosine of … with the truth at $b = 0.083$ (networks in … pieces) but … at $b = 0.44$ (…).

The form of the rule matters as much as its constants. The readout turns a weight into a distance through equation (2), which comes from this rule's regulariser $b - \alpha w$: a fixed amount $b$ added to every synapse and a decay in proportion to the weight. A regulariser of a different form can change the fixed-point equation, so the same weights would be read differently, and can change the objective the rule descends. A related distinction is old: for Hebbian learning under a constraint on the total weight, Miller and MacKay (1994) showed that subtractive and multiplicative versions of the constraint lead to different kinds of solutions. So we regard reading the weights as an important idea, but one whose details are tied to the model: to read the weights you have to know what wrote them.

Limits

The weights record second moments only, and only their contrast part $P C P$; a second moment is not the input distribution. The recipe assumes linear neurons, known input wiring $A$ and known rule constants, weights that have stopped moving and carry no noise, and enough well-conditioned equations. It recovers the second moment of the inputs the network was actually trained on, not of the population they were drawn from.

Details: the estimator

Work in an orthonormal basis $Q$ of the contrasts ($Q^\top \mathbf 1 = 0$, 11 columns), with unknown $Y = Q^\top C Q$, a symmetric $11 \times 11$ matrix (66 numbers). Live synapse $e = (i, j)$ gives $c_e^\top Y c_e = 2(b - \alpha w_e)$ with $c_e = Q^\top (h_i - h_j)$. We solve $\min_Y \sum_e \big(c_e^\top Y c_e - 2(b - \alpha w_e)\big)^2 + \rho \lVert Y\rVert_F^2$ with a tiny ridge $\rho = 10^{-10}\sigma_{\max}^2$, where $\sigma_{\max}$ is the largest singular value of the equations; directions the equations do not measure are set to zero. The result is projected onto positive semidefinite matrices and mapped back, $Q Y Q^\top$. Ranks count singular values above $10^{-8}\sigma_{\max}$ (Householder QR, then a one-sided Jacobi SVD). The inequalities from pruned pairs are not used. Full rank is necessary but not sufficient: a nearly singular system identifies $Y$ in exact arithmetic, but the ridge shrinks its weakest directions.
