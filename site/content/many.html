With 24 inputs each neuron's harmonic embedding is a point in a 23-dimensional simplex. Two questions change: how do you see what the network has learned, and can you read the input statistics back out of its weights? The model and the rule are those of the idea and three inputs; the inputs are synthetic Gaussian signals with a covariance $\Sigma_g$ I chose, and everything runs live in your browser.

Set-up: the rule, the defaults, the five covariance families and sparse wiring
$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = -\eta\,\theta - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j,$$

clipped at $w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}$, with $\eta_{ij} = \eta$ for interior–boundary (I–B) synapses and $\gamma\eta$ for interior–interior (I–I) ones. Everything depends on $\Sigma_g$ only through the $\theta$-shifted covariance $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ (why).

Defaults, as in the parameter sweeps of docs/findings.md: $\lambda = \eta = 1$, $\gamma = 1$, Euler steps $dt = 0.05$, every potential synapse present, I–B weights drawn from an exponential distribution with mean 0.2, lateral weights uniform on $[0, 0.03]$ (which behaves like starting at the floor), and $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$. Every clipped synapse still conducts $w_{\min}$, so a neuron leaks toward the population mean with total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$ (0.015 here); keep $\omega \lesssim 0.02$, or at high $\theta$ the leak kills the networkWith $N = 120$, planted blocks die for $\theta \ge 0.57$ at $w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$ and survive to $\theta = 0.8$ at $10^{-4}$ (findings F5).. Plasticity sees the exact $\Sigma_g$ (mean-field); under More controls it can see minibatches instead.

The covariance families, all synthetic synthetic Σ:

  • Planted blocks: groups of inputs, correlation within and between groups (optionally negative, and optionally unequal sizes and variances).
  • Hierarchical blocks: groups of groups, correlation 0.6 between siblings, 0.3 one level up, 0.05 across the root.
  • Ring: $B$ inputs on a circle with a wrapped Gaussian covariance, $(\Sigma_g)_{bc} \propto \sum_m e^{-(b - c + mB)^2/2\ell^2}$, unit variances. All correlations are positive and the eigenvectors are Fourier modes, signed waves around the ring.
  • Power-law spectrum: a random orthogonal eigenbasis with eigenvalues $\propto k^{-\alpha}$, correlations of both signs; the correlations ≥ 0 variant adds the smallest $c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ that removes every negative one.
  • Wishart: the sample covariance of a few Gaussian draws, i.e. no structure at all.

Sparse wiring, the connection probability $p$: with $p < 1$ a fixed random mask removes potential synapses, so each neuron only ever touches a random subset of inputs and neurons. The model is dense and stays dense by default; $p < 1$ is a labelled comparison, and it matters for the readout.

How to read the four linked views
  1. 3D view. The embeddings $h_1, \dots, h_N \in \mathbb R^B$, centred and projected orthogonally onto their top three principal axes, rotated within that subspace to match the previous frame so the picture never flips. A cluster near one square lives on that input; squares piled up in the middle are inputs the three axes do not see. The corner overlay says how much variance the axes hold.
  2. Voltage covariance $H\Sigma_g H^\top$, neurons sorted by cluster. Clusters are diagonal blocks whose value is the cluster's voltage variance $h^\top\Sigma_g h$; between two clusters at a fixed point it is at most $\theta/\gamma$, or the synapse between them would grow.
  3. Parts. One panel per cluster: bars for its mean embedding over the inputs (what the cluster represents: each member's voltage is $h^\top g$), and as dots either the nearest eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ or the part predicted by equation (3) below. Each panel prints both cosines, the cluster's $c$ and the stability test.
  4. Time series of the cluster count $K$, the effective rank of $H$ ($K$ for $K$ orthogonal clusters) and the residual $r$, which must fall below $10^{-6}$ before I call anything an equilibrium. $K$ counts clusters with at least $\max(2, 2\%\,N)$ live members. Dead neurons (every I–B weight at the floor) all share the floor's embedding; they are counted separately, drawn grey and sorted last, and are not a cluster. Pretend you only have the weights

The note's boldest claim: at a fixed point every active synapse is a linear equation in the unknown input covariance, so the weights can be solved for it. The panel does that by masked least squares from the current weights of the run above, next to the parts-sum sketch (below), and scores both against the true $\Sigma_g$.

The equations, the three scoring settings, the extra rows, and why Bures–Wasserstein is never shown alone
$$\begin{aligned} \text{I–B:}\quad (HC)_{ib} &= \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ib}, \\ \text{I–I:}\quad \gamma\, h_i^\top C h_j &= \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$

Parameters known: $\theta$, $\lambda/\eta$ and $\gamma$ are given and the full covariance is scored. Unknown scale: $\lambda/\eta$ is unknown, so the best scale is fitted. $\theta$ unknown: $\hat C$ is defined only up to $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ (rows of $H$ sum to one), so both matrices are projected with $P$ and the best scale is fitted (the contrast-subspace score; it also discards every $\mathbf 1a^\top + a\mathbf 1^\top$). Each setting reports the Frobenius cosine and the Bures–Wasserstein distance; the contrast setting adds the overlap of the top eigenspaces.

The table also reads the I–I and I–B synapses alone, the derivative-aware readout (uses the observed $dw/dt$ of the last step, so it is exact away from equilibrium), the note's own formula $\hat C = H^+RH^{+\top}$ (it treats every lateral weight as an equation, pruned ones included, and guesses the unobserved diagonal of $R$: it matches least squares with dense wiring, 0.914, and collapses with sparse wiring, 0.23, because absent synapses look like zero weights), an untrained network read as if at equilibrium, and three baselines.

Bures–Wasserstein alone misleads. The identity matrix scores BW 0.138 in the contrast setting, much better than the dense readout's 0.535, though it knows nothing about $\Sigma_g$ (eigenspace overlap 0.03). BW punishes an estimate that is right on a few directions and silent on the rest more than one vaguely spread over all of them, so the cosine and the overlap always sit next to it.

What a cluster represents: parts, not eigenvectors

The note conjectured one cluster per eigenmode of $g$. Read the cosines in the parts panels on the default, the ring or the signed tour: no. A cluster sits on a part, a non-negative, localised bump over a group of correlated inputs $S$,

$$\begin{gathered} A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \quad h_S > 0, \\ (A h)_b \le 0 \;\text{ for } b \notin S, \end{gathered}$$

the Perron vector of a principal submatrix of $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, with $c$ a member's summed input conductance (derivation; in a triangle). Against that Perron vector the cosine is 1.000 at convergence; eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$ are signed and spread over every input.

Key point

A cluster stands for a non-negative part of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$: closer to non-negative PCA than to PCA, with $\theta$ setting how fine the parts are.

Which parts are stable, and what that gives on blocks, rings and signed inputs

Linearising around a lone neuron on $S$ gives a sufficient test (the nondegenerate case): the part is stable if $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ and $(Ah)_b < 0$ strictly for every input off $S$; then $h$ is a strict local maximum of $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ over non-negative vectors. Each panel prints the largest $(Ah)_b$ off its support. Three consequences, checked in docs/findings.md (F1) by enumerating every support for $B \le 16$:

  • If $\theta$ is below every covariance, $A$ is positive and there is exactly one part, on all inputs: the correct form of "$\theta \le 0$ gives one cluster", which fails at $\theta = 0$ for signed $\Sigma_g$.
  • For uniform blocks (correlation $\rho$ within, $\rho_a$ across) a block's part is stable exactly when $\rho_a < \theta < \rho$. Above every correlation the parts are single inputs, and an input with variance below $\theta$ cannot host one.
  • $\theta$ is a resolution knob. On the ring, parts are bumps whose width shrinks as $\theta$ grows (width 4 at $\theta = 0.4$, $\ell = 1.5$), one centred on each input: place fields, while every eigenvector is a Fourier wave. For blocks and hierarchies the parts lie in the span of the top eigenvectors, as their non-negative, localised rotation (cosine with the nearest single eigenvector 0.6–0.8 on the default blocks).

Which parts get neurons: gating and packing

Equation (3) says which parts exist, not which get neurons. With lateral plasticity two rules decide. Gating: with $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$, the mean covariance of two starting neurons, lateral synapses first grow if $\theta < \gamma\kappa_0$ (a heuristic crossover from the expected initial lateral drift, not a derived phase boundary); then the population binds and lands on one part, however many exist: four stable block parts, one cluster; with $\gamma = 0$, four. Packing: two clusters coexist only if $\gamma\, h_k^\top\Sigma_g h_l \le \theta$, so overlapping parts exclude each other: 16 overlapping bumps without lateral plasticity, a few disjoint ones with it. The $\theta$ slider marks $\gamma\kappa_0$.

Gating coarse to fine, packing on the ring, memory without restarting, and how many neurons each part gets

Coarse to fine. The gating rule applies again at each level of a hierarchy: neurons heading for a coarse part stay bound while $\gamma$ times that part's own energy exceeds $\theta$, and pick one finer part together (the tree tour). In the sweeps this predicts every transition to within 0.03–0.08 in $\theta$ (findings F3).

Packing on the ring. Parts on disjoint supports always pass the packing test for $\gamma \le 1$, overlapping ones may not. Without lateral plasticity, $\theta = 0.4$, all 16 width-4 bumps are populated although neighbours share three inputs and have cross-covariance 0.645. With $\gamma = 1$ only a packing survives: bumps that share at most one input (the note under the parts panels prints the largest cross-covariance, below the bound 0.4).

Memory. In the tree tour without restarting $\theta$ rises in steps while the weights are kept: the population narrows from 16 inputs to one half, then to one group of four, and never splits. At $\theta = 0.65$ that group is no longer a stable part, yet the cluster sits on it until the residual has dipped below $10^{-6}$, then leaves, still whole, for a single input: a small residual does not certify stability. History and noise in chapter 1 shows the same with three inputs.

How many neurons each part gets is not an equilibrium property. Equation (3) does not contain the cluster sizes, so any allocation is a fixed point (why). The sizes come from the transient: winner-takes-all below $\gamma\kappa_0$ (one block takes everything), a steep power of each part's energy above it, measured in the homeostasis chapter.

What the weights can and cannot tell you

At a clustered fixed point the members of cluster $k$ share $h_k$, so the weights depend only on the parts $h_k$, their conductances $c_k$ and the cluster sizes. They store a parts-sum sketch $\hat C = \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k h_k h_k^\top/\lVert h_k\rVert^2$, not $\Sigma_g$: covariance between parts, below $\theta$, or inside a part beyond its Perron direction is gone. With sparse wiring members differ, and each probes $\Sigma_g$ differently. The table is computed in your browser when you scroll to it:

Key point

Equilibrium weights store a parts-sketch of $\Sigma_g$: on these blocks dense weights pin down 24 of 300 directions and least squares recovers exactly the sketch (0.914 for both). Reading more needs neurons that stay different (sparse wiring: 84 directions, 0.987 here) or the weights before they settle (0.988 at $t = 7.5$).

What is lost in principle, and the table row by row (floor leakage, sparse wiring on other covariances, mid-training)

Some of $\Sigma_g$ is out of reach whatever the clusters do. Equations (2) involve $C$ only through $H$, so: without $\lambda/\eta$, $\hat C$ is known only up to a factor (scale); with $\theta$ unknown, adding $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ to $C$ changes nothing (shift); a synapse at the floor says only that the covariance it saw was at most $\theta$, an inequality the readout does not use; and with no self-synapses the diagonal of $H\Sigma_g H^\top$ is never observed.

The clusters remove much more. The active I–B synapses of cluster $k$ say $(\Sigma_g h_k)_b = \theta + c_k h_{kb}$ on its support and the I–I equations add nothing new, so for disjoint supports the sketch satisfies every active-synapse equation; it is non-negative with rank at most $K + 1$ ($K$ after removing the $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ term). The measurement operator $A$ (of the readout) maps a symmetric $C$ to the left-hand sides of (2); a direction of $\mathrm{Sym}(B)$ is pinned down when its eigenvalue of $A^\top A$ exceeds $10^{-5}$ of the largest. With dense wiring the equations of one cluster are copies of each other: at the default $4 \times 6 = 24$ directions, and the difference heatmap shows that inside each block the readout knows only the block's row sums.

  • The floor leaks a little information. With "parameters known" the dense readout scores 0.956, but 0.918 with a 100× lower floor, while the contrast cosine hardly moves (0.914 against 0.912). The between-block entries the dense readout gets right come through the $O(w_{\min})$ tails of $h$ that floor synapses add.
  • Sparse wiring on other covariances. The page computes this only when you ask: select a setting, then press "Re-run this comparison". On the signed power-law family with $\theta = 0.3$, $p = 0.5$ and the other settings at their defaults, it gave least-squares contrast cosines 0.502 (dense) and 0.707 (sparse) at $B = 24$, $\alpha = 1$, $\Sigma_g$ seed 1, and 0.867 and 0.901 at $B = 16$, $\alpha = 1.5$, seed 2: better than dense, far from exact. I have not mapped other settings. The parts-sum is the wrong estimator for sparse nets (0.890 on the blocks): members differ, and averaging them throws the difference away.
  • Mid-training beats equilibrium. Applied as if at equilibrium, least squares peaks at 0.988 around $t = 7.5$ (step 150), while the clusters form, then settles at 0.914. Watch "Readout quality over time" from a fresh start of the dense tour. The derivative-aware row is exact (cosine 1.000) for the first few dozen steps and falls to the equilibrium value as the clusters collapse.

What each tour shows
  • θ sweep on blocks ($\gamma\kappa_0 = 0.22$): one cluster on all 24 inputs at 0.05; at 0.15 one cluster on one block (four block parts exist, but the population is still bound); one per block at 0.3; single inputs at 0.6.
  • Signed Σ at θ = 0: with no pruning drift, negative covariances alone split the population into two clusters on disjoint inputs; make every correlation non-negative and the split disappears.
  • One block takes everything: eight equally strong blocks, $\theta = 0.1$ between the across-block correlation and $\kappa_0 = 0.135$; eight parts exist and one gets all the neurons.
  • θ walks down a tree, restarting at each $\theta$: all 16 inputs; one half (the population is bound below $\kappa_0 = 0.275$); one group of four in each half; all four groups; single inputs. Without restarting it never splits.
  • Dense against sparse wiring: the same clusters, very different readouts; in the measurement spectrum, a cliff after 24 directions when dense, a long tail when sparse.
  • In minibatch mode the residual never reaches the tolerance, and the readout panel says the equilibrium assumption is violated, as it should.
Engineering choices in this chapter
  • Integration. Euler steps with $dt = 0.05$, $\lambda = \eta = 1$, as in the sweeps. At ×1 the sim runs one step per frame; faster speeds run up to 16 steps within a 14 ms budget. Structure tours start at ×16, readout tours at ×1.
  • Equilibrium. Claimed only when the projected residual $r/\eta = \max|dw/dt|/\eta$ is below $10^{-6}$; a free run pauses at $10^{-8}$, a tour moves to its next $\theta$ at $10^{-6}$. (The sweeps used $10^{-5}$.)
  • Clusters. Single linkage on the $L_1$ distance between embeddings, cut at the largest multiplicative gap in the merge heights (at least 0.05); "not clustered yet" while that gap is below 3×. Dead neurons (every I–B weight at most $1.5\,w_{\min}$) are then split off, and $K$ counts groups with at least $\max(2, 2\%\,N)$ live members; smaller ones are stragglers. This is the convention of the sweeps (which used average linkage cut at 0.1; at converged states the two agree, because clusters are exact clones) and of chapter 1. Colours are carried from frame to frame by majority vote of members.
  • Ring. The wrapped Gaussian is built as a circulant from its non-negative Fourier spectrum, so $\Sigma_g$ is positive semi-definite for every $B$ and $\ell$; a Gaussian of the shortest ring distance is not (at $B = 12$, $\ell = 4$ its smallest eigenvalue is −0.247). For $\ell \ll B$ the two agree ($B = 16$, $\ell = 1.5$: within $10^{-6}$).
  • Projection. PCA of the current embeddings, Procrustes-aligned to the previous frame within the new top-3 subspace.
  • Readout. Masked ridge least squares over symmetric $C$ by conjugate gradients (ridge $10^{-6}$ relative), warm-started, in a web worker; for $\theta$ unknown the ridge acts only on $PCP$ ("project" mode), exact for $\gamma = 1$ and for $\gamma = 0$ (I–B equations only); otherwise $\theta$ is fitted (set to 0 when no I–I synapse is active, since it is then not identifiable and a fitted value made the solver wander) and the estimate projected for display. Over the first 400 steps the worker runs only the main, parts-sum and derivative-aware rows, every 20 steps, and the simulation waits for it.
  • Measurement spectrum. The Gram matrix $A^\top A$ is accumulated per neuron rather than per synapse (the same matrix, checked against the engine to $10^{-14}$) and its eigenvalues found without eigenvectors. At $B \ge 32$ ($528$ or $1{,}176$ directions) it still takes seconds, so it is recomputed only while the run is paused or converged.
  • Subspace overlap. $k$ is placed at the largest eigen-gap among the first six eigenvalues of $P\Sigma_g P$ (ratio at least 1.5), else $k = 3$. The rank-$k$ baseline truncates $\Sigma_g$ itself, whose top direction is close to $\mathbf 1$, so in the contrast setting it keeps only $k - 1$ useful directions.
  • Speed. Per frame: one or more engine steps, the PCA projection and the 3D view; clustering and the effective rank every 3rd–4th frame; the heatmap, parts and time series staggered over 8 frames. In headless Chrome without a GPU on a 4-core server this gave about 50 frames per second at the default size and 25–30 at $B = 48$, $N = 250$.
