With three inputs you can watch every embedding move inside its triangle. With 24 inputs each neuron's harmonic embedding is a point in a 23-dimensional simplex, and the questions change: how do you see what the network has learned, and can you read the input statistics back out of its weights? The inputs here are synthetic Gaussian signals whose covariance $\Sigma_g$ I chose: planted blocks, hierarchies, a ring, random spectra. The network and the rule are exactly those of chapter 1, with many more boundary neurons, and everything runs live in your browser.

The set-up

Same model as before: $B$ boundary neurons clamped to $g$, $N$ interior neurons with voltages $u = Hg$, and the rule

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = -\eta\,\theta - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j,$$

clipped at $w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}$, with $\eta_{ij} = \eta$ for interior–boundary (I–B) synapses and $\gamma\eta$ for interior–interior (I–I) ones. A synapse survives where the covariance of its two ends beats the pruning threshold $\theta$ (for I–I synapses, $\theta/\gamma$). Everything below depends on $\Sigma_g$ only through the $\theta$-shifted covariance $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$: since the entries of each $h_i$ sum to one, $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \theta = h_i^\top A\, h_j$. So pruning by a positive $\theta$ and pruning by negative covariances are one mechanism.

The defaults are those of the parameter sweeps in docs/findings.md: $\lambda = \eta = 1$, $\gamma = 1$, Euler steps $dt = 0.05$, a dense start (every potential synapse exists, I–B weights drawn from an exponential distribution with mean 0.2, lateral weights uniform on $[0, 0.03]$, which behaves like starting at the floor), and $w_{\min} = 10^{-4}$. What the floor does depends on $N$: every clipped synapse still conducts $w_{\min}$, so a neuron leaks toward the population mean with total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$ (0.015 here). Keep $\omega \lesssim 0.02$; at $w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$ and high $\theta$ the leak kills the networkWith $N = 120$, planted blocks die for $\theta \ge 0.57$ at $w_{\min} = 10^{-3}$ and survive to $\theta = 0.8$ at $10^{-4}$ (findings F5).. By default plasticity sees the exact $\Sigma_g$ (mean-field); under More controls it can instead see the covariance of a minibatch of samples.

The covariance families, all synthetic synthetic Σ:

  • Planted blocks: inputs in groups, correlation within a group and between groups (optionally negative, and optionally unequal block sizes and variances).
  • Hierarchical blocks: groups of groups, correlation 0.6 between siblings, 0.3 one level up, 0.05 across the root.
  • Ring: $B$ inputs on a circle, $(\Sigma_g)_{bc} = e^{-d(b,c)^2/2\ell^2}$. All correlations are positive and the eigenvectors are Fourier modes: signed waves around the ring.
  • Power-law spectrum: a random orthogonal eigenbasis with eigenvalues $\propto k^{-\alpha}$. Its correlations have both signs; the correlations ≥ 0 variant adds the smallest $c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ that removes every negative one.
  • Wishart: the sample covariance of a few Gaussian draws, i.e. no structure at all.

One control has a large effect on the readout: sparse wiring, the connection probability $p$. With $p < 1$ a fixed random mask removes potential synapses, so each neuron can only ever touch a random subset of inputs and other neurons. The model is dense ($p = 1$) and stays dense by default; $p < 1$ is a labelled comparison. As the readout section shows, on block-structured inputs it is what makes reading the weights back nearly exact.

Pretend you only have the weights

The note's boldest claim is that you can do weight-space interpretability: given only the weights, compute $H$ and solve for the input covariance. At a fixed point every active synapse satisfies a linear equation in the unknown $C$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{I–B:}\quad (HC)_{ib} &= \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ib}, \\ \text{I–I:}\quad \gamma\, h_i^\top C h_j &= \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$

The panel below solves these equations by masked ridge least squares, from the current weights of the run above, about twice a second in a background thread (every 20 steps over the first 400, where the readout changes fastest; the simulation waits for it there). Next to it is a second estimator that uses the clusters directly, the parts-sum sketch explained below. It scores the estimate $\hat C$ in the three settings that should be kept apart. Parameters known: $\theta$, $\lambda/\eta$ and $\gamma$ are given, and the full covariance is scored. Unknown scale: $\lambda/\eta$ is unknown, so the best scale is fitted. $\theta$ unknown: then $\hat C$ is only defined up to $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, so both matrices are projected with $P = I - \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/B$ (the contrast-subspace score). Each setting reports the Frobenius cosine and the Bures–Wasserstein distance, and the contrast setting adds the overlap of the top eigenspaces. Bures–Wasserstein on its own misleads (see below), so it is never shown alone.

What a cluster represents: parts, not eigenvectors

The note conjectured that each cluster becomes "isomorphic to the eigenmode of $g$ which it represents". The parts panel answers that live, and the answer is no. Run the default (four planted blocks), the ring or the signed power-law tour, switch the dots to the nearest eigenvector and read the cosines. A cluster's part is a non-negative, localised bump over a group of mutually correlated inputs: flat over one block, zero elsewhere. The eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$ are signed and spread over every input. Bumps and eigenvectors meet only where the top eigenvector happens to be non-negative.

What the part is turns out to be exact. In the dense network all members of a cluster share one embedding $h$ (to within $O(w_{\min})$), so they sit at the same voltage for every stimulus and the synapses among them carry no current. Kirchhoff's law then makes $h$ proportional to a member's I–B weights, and at a fixed point each active I–B weight is $\tfrac{\eta}{\lambda}(Ah)_b$. With $w_{\min} \to 0$, on the support $S$ of the part:

$$\begin{gathered} A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \quad h_S > 0, \quad A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \\ (A h)_b \le 0 \;\text{ for } b \notin S. \end{gathered}$$

So a cluster sits on a part: the Perron vector (the non-negative top eigenvector) of a principal submatrix of the $\theta$-shifted covariance. $N$, $\gamma$ and the lateral weights drop out. The eigenvalue has a physical meaning: $c = (\lambda/\eta)\sum_b w_{ib}$ is a member's summed input conductance, and equals $(h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$. The panels print it, and the "Perron vector" dots are the top eigenvector of $A_{SS}$ on the cluster's own inputs: at convergence the cosine is 1.000. It is also close to the Perron vector of $\Sigma_{SS}$ without the shift (the cosine in brackets).

Which supports are stable. Linearising around a lone neuron on $S$ gives an exact criterion: the part is stable if and only if $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ and $(Ah)_b < 0$ strictly for every input off $S$; otherwise synapses to those inputs grow. Equivalently, $h$ is a strict local maximum of $h^\top A h/h^\top h$ over non-negative vectors: a cluster is a local optimum of non-negative PCA of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. Each panel prints the largest $(Ah)_b$ off its support. Three consequences, all checked in docs/findings.md (F1) by enumerating every support for $B \le 16$:

  • If $\theta$ is below every covariance, $A$ is positive and there is exactly one part, on all inputs. This is the correct form of "$\theta \le 0$ gives one cluster": with signed $\Sigma_g$ it fails at $\theta = 0$.
  • For uniform blocks (correlation $\rho$ within, $\rho_a$ across) a block's part is stable exactly when $\rho_a < \theta < \rho$. Above every correlation the parts are single inputs, and an input with variance below $\theta$ cannot host one.
  • $\theta$ is a resolution knob on parts. On the ring, parts are bumps whose width shrinks as $\theta$ grows (width 4 at $\theta = 0.4$, $\ell = 1.5$), one centred on each input: localised place fields, while every eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ is a Fourier wave. For blocks and hierarchies the parts lie inside the span of the top eigenvectors, but as their non-negative, localised rotation (the cosine with the nearest single eigenvector is 0.6–0.8 on the default blocks).
The maths: the part equation and its stability

Kirchhoff at interior neuron $i$: $\deg_i\, h_i = \sum_j w_{ij} h_j + \sum_b w_{ib} e_b$. If every neighbour $j$ in the cluster has $h_j = h_i = h$, and synapses to other clusters sit at the floor, then $h\,(\deg_i - \sum_{j \in \text{cluster}} w_{ij}) = \sum_b w_{ib} e_b + O(w_{\min})$: $h = w_{i\cdot}/s$ with $s = \sum_b w_{ib}$. The I–B fixed point sets $w_{ib} = \frac{\eta}{\lambda}(Ah)_b$ on active synapses and $w_{ib} = w_{\min}$ where $(Ah)_b \le 0$. Hence $s\,h = \frac{\eta}{\lambda}(Ah)_+$, which on $S$ is (3) with $c = \lambda s/\eta$, and multiplying by $h^\top$ gives $c = h^\top A h/\lVert h\rVert^2$.

Stability: for a lone neuron on $S$, $\dot w = \eta(\Sigma_{SS} w/s - \theta\mathbf 1) - \lambda w$. Its Jacobian at the fixed point is $\frac{\lambda}{c}A_{SS}(I - h\mathbf 1^\top) - \lambda I$. In the eigenbasis of $A_{SS}$ the matrix $A_{SS} - c\,h\mathbf 1^\top$ is triangular with diagonal $(0, \mu_2, \mu_3, \dots)$, so the eigenvalues are $-\lambda$ and $\lambda(\mu_k/c - 1)$: stable on $S$ iff $c > \mu_k$ for all $k \ge 2$. Off $S$, a floor weight grows at rate $\eta(Ah)_b$. A cluster's collective mode obeys the same equation.

Key point

A cluster stands for a non-negative part, the Perron vector of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a group of inputs, stable when nothing outside the group would gain a synapse. That is closer to non-negative PCA or non-negative matrix factorisation than to PCA, and $\theta$ sets how fine the parts are.

Which parts get neurons: gating and packing

Equation (3) says which parts exist at a given $\theta$. It does not say which of them the population lands on, and with lateral plasticity that is a separate rule. At the start every embedding is near the centroid, so the lateral drift of a pair is about $\eta(\gamma\kappa_0 - \theta)$, with

$$\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2,$$

the mean covariance of two random initial neurons. Below the collective threshold $\theta < \gamma\kappa_0$ every lateral synapse grows first, the population binds into one equipotential blob, and the blob lands in one part, however many exist. Above it, neurons choose independently. The $\theta$ slider marks $\gamma\kappa_0$ and says which side you are on. The bound tour shows the difference: four blocks, $\theta = 0.15$, so all four block parts are stable, but $\gamma\kappa_0 = 0.22$ and all 150 neurons end on a single block. With $\gamma = 0$ the same start gives four clusters, and so does $\theta = 0.3$ with $\gamma = 1$. The rule applies again at each level of a hierarchy: neurons heading for a coarse part stay bound while $\gamma$ times that part's own energy exceeds $\theta$, and pick one finer part together. In the sweeps this predicts every transition to within 0.03–0.08 in $\theta$ (findings F3).

Lateral plasticity also limits which clusters can coexist. Two clusters at a fixed point need $\gamma\, h_k^\top\Sigma_g h_l \le \theta$ (up to floor terms): otherwise the synapses between them grow and they merge. Parts on disjoint supports always pass for $\gamma \le 1$, but overlapping ones may not. The ring makes this visible. Without lateral plasticity, $\theta = 0.4$, all 16 width-4 bumps are populated, although neighbours share three inputs and have cross-covariance 0.645. With $\gamma = 1$ only a packing survives: in this run four bumps that share at most one input (the note under the parts panels prints the largest cross-covariance, below the bound 0.4).

Bound populations also remember. In the tree tour without restarting $\theta$ rises in steps while the weights are kept: the population narrows from all 16 inputs to one half, then to one group of four, and never splits. At $\theta = 0.65$ that group is no longer a stable part ($\theta$ is above its 0.6 correlation, though its $c = 0.2$ is still positive), yet the cluster sits on it until the residual has dipped below $10^{-6}$, and only then leaves, still as one cluster, for a single input. Watch the residual trace at the last step: a nearly symmetric state can linger on an unstable part, so a small residual does not certify stability. In the sweeps, a little private noise (the jitter toggle under More controls) undoes most such artefacts for $\gamma = 0$, but a laterally bound population still never splits as $\theta$ rises (findings F8).

Caveat: how many neurons each part gets is not an equilibrium property

Equation (3) does not contain the cluster sizes, so any allocation of neurons to parts is a fixed point. The sizes you see come from the transient: winner-takes-all below $\gamma\kappa_0$ (one block takes everything), a steep power of each part's energy above it. The homeostasis chapter measures that law.

What the weights can and cannot tell you

Even in principle, some of $\Sigma_g$ is out of reach. The equations (2) involve $C$ only through $H$, so:

  • Scale. Without $\lambda/\eta$, $\hat C$ is known only up to a factor.
  • Shift. Rows of $H$ sum to one, so with $\theta$ unknown, adding $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ to $C$ changes nothing; the contrast score projects that direction out (together with every $\mathbf 1 a^\top + a\mathbf 1^\top$).
  • Inequalities. A synapse at the floor says only that the covariance it saw was at most $\theta$. Pruned synapses give inequalities, not equations, and the readout uses active synapses only.
  • No variances. There are no self-synapses, so the diagonal of $H\Sigma_g H^\top$ is never observed directly.

The clusters remove much more. At a clustered fixed point every member of cluster $k$ shares $h_k$, so the weights are a function of the parts $h_k$, their constants $c_k$ and the cluster sizes, nothing else. The active I–B synapses of cluster $k$ say $(\Sigma_g h_k)_b = \theta + c_k h_{kb}$ on its support; the I–I equations add nothing new. That suggests reading the weights with a parts-sum sketch

$$\hat C = \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k\, \frac{h_k h_k^\top}{\lVert h_k\rVert^2},$$

with $c_k$ read off as a member's summed input conductance. For disjoint supports it satisfies every active-synapse equation, and it is non-negative with rank $K$. Equilibrium weights store this sketch of $\Sigma_g$, not $\Sigma_g$: only the supra-threshold structure of the populated parts. Covariance between different parts, below $\theta$, and inside a part beyond its Perron direction is gone.

The live panel counts what is left. The measurement operator $A$ (of the readout) maps a symmetric $C$ to the left-hand sides of (2); a direction of $\mathrm{Sym}(B)$ counts as pinned down when its eigenvalue of $A^\top A$ exceeds $10^{-5}$ of the largest. With dense wiring the equations of one cluster are copies of each other, and at the default the active synapses pin down $4 \times 6 = 24$ of the 300 directions. The difference heatmap shows what is missing: inside each block the readout knows only the block's row sums.

Sparse wiring keeps the evidence. With a random mask ($p = 0.5$) the clusters are the same four, but members differ because each reads its own random subset of inputs, and every member is a different probe of $\Sigma_g$. Four things in this table deserve a sentence each.

  • Dense: the least-squares readout and the parts-sum agree to three digits (0.914) in the contrast setting: at equilibrium, least squares recovers the sketch and nothing more. On the block covariance the sketch is good because $\Sigma_g$ is close to a sum of block parts.
  • The floor leaks a little information. With "parameters known" the dense readout scores 0.956, but 0.918 when the floor is 100× lower, while the contrast cosine hardly moves (0.914 against 0.912). The between-block entries that the dense readout gets right come through the $O(w_{\min})$ tails of $h$ that floor synapses add, not from anything the network stores on purpose.
  • Sparse wiring pins down 84 directions and gets contrast cosine 0.987. On this covariance it is the difference between a sketch and a nearly exact readout. It helps much less when $\Sigma_g$ is not made of blocks: on the signed power-law family (re-run the comparison with it selected) I measured contrast cosines 0.50 → 0.71 ($B = 24$, $\alpha = 1$, $\theta = 0.3$) and 0.87 → 0.90 ($B = 16$, $\alpha = 1.5$, $\Sigma_g$ seed 2), still far from exact. The parts-sum is not the right estimator for sparse nets (0.890): members differ, and averaging them throws the difference away.
  • Mid-training is better than equilibrium. Applied to the weights as if they were at equilibrium, least squares peaks at 0.988 around $t = 7.5$ (step 150), while the clusters are forming, then settles at 0.914. Watch the "Readout quality over time" chart in the live panel from a fresh start. The derivative-aware row, which uses the observed $dw/dt$, is exact (cosine 1.000) during the first few dozen steps and falls to the equilibrium value as the clusters collapse.

Caveat: Bures–Wasserstein alone misleads

The identity matrix scores BW 0.138 under the contrast setting, much better than the dense readout's 0.535, though it knows nothing about $\Sigma_g$: its eigenspace overlap is 0.03. BW punishes an estimate that is right on a few directions and silent on the rest more than one that is vaguely spread over all of them. That is why the cosine and the overlap always sit next to it.

One more row of the live table: the note's own formula, $\hat C = H^+ R H^{+\top}$, treats every lateral weight as an equation (pruned ones included) and has to guess the unobserved diagonal of $R$. With dense wiring it ends at the same contrast cosine as least squares (0.914); with sparse wiring it collapses (0.23), because absent synapses look like zero weights.

Key point

Equilibrium weights store a parts-sketch of $\Sigma_g$: each populated part and its input conductance. Reading more needs neurons that stay different from each other (sparse wiring, on block-like inputs) or the weights before they settle.

What to notice

  • θ sweep on blocks ($\gamma\kappa_0 = 0.22$): one cluster on all 24 inputs at 0.05; at 0.15 one cluster on one block (four block parts exist, but the population is still bound); one per block at 0.3; single inputs at 0.6 (23 of 24 populated, two neurons die).
  • Signed Σ at θ = 0: with no pruning drift at all, negative covariances alone split the population into two clusters on disjoint groups of inputs. Make every correlation non-negative and the split disappears.
  • One block takes everything: eight equally strong blocks, $\theta = 0.1$ between the across-block correlation and $\kappa_0 = 0.135$. Eight parts exist and one gets all the neurons.
  • θ walks down a tree, restarting at each $\theta$: all 16 inputs; then one half (at 0.15 and 0.25, because the population is still bound below $\kappa_0 = 0.275$); one group of four in each half at 0.35; all four groups at 0.45; single inputs at 0.65 (14 of 16 populated, two neurons die). Without restarting it never splits, and lingers on an unstable part before leaving it.
  • Ring: place fields and packing: 16 overlapping bumps without lateral plasticity, a few non-overlapping ones with it.
  • Dense against sparse wiring: the same clusters, very different readouts. Watch the measurement spectrum: a cliff after 24 directions when dense, a long tail when sparse.
  • In minibatch mode the residual never reaches the tolerance, and the readout panel says the equilibrium assumption is violated, as it should.

Engineering choices in this chapter
  • Integration. Euler steps with $dt = 0.05$, $\lambda = \eta = 1$, as in the sweeps. At ×1 the sim runs one step per frame; faster speeds run up to 16 steps within a 14 ms budget. Structure tours start at ×16, readout tours at ×1.
  • Equilibrium. Claimed only when the projected residual $r/\eta = \max|dw/dt|/\eta$ is below $10^{-6}$; a free run pauses at $10^{-8}$, a tour moves to its next $\theta$ at $10^{-6}$. (The sweeps used $10^{-5}$.)
  • Clusters. Single linkage on the $L_1$ distance between embeddings, cut at the largest multiplicative gap in the merge heights (at least 0.05); "not clustered yet" while that gap is below 3×. The sweeps used average linkage cut at 0.1; at converged states the two agree, because clusters are exact clones. Colours are carried from frame to frame by majority vote of members. Dead: every I–B weight at most $1.5\,w_{\min}$.
  • Projection. PCA of the current embeddings, Procrustes-aligned to the previous frame within the new top-3 subspace.
  • Readout. Masked ridge least squares over symmetric $C$ by conjugate gradients (ridge $10^{-6}$ relative), warm-started from the previous solve, in a web worker; for $\theta$ unknown the ridge acts only on $PCP$ ("project" mode), which is exact for $\gamma = 1$ and for $\gamma = 0$ (I–B equations only); otherwise $\theta$ is fitted. Over the first 400 steps the worker runs only the main, parts-sum and derivative-aware rows, every 20 steps, and the simulation waits for it.
  • Measurement spectrum. The Gram matrix $A^\top A$ is accumulated per neuron rather than per synapse (the same matrix, checked against the engine to $10^{-14}$) and its eigenvalues found without eigenvectors. Even so, at $B \ge 32$ ($528$ or $1{,}176$ directions) it takes seconds, so it is recomputed only while the run is paused or converged.
  • Subspace overlap. $k$ is placed at the largest eigen-gap among the first six eigenvalues of $P\Sigma_g P$ (ratio at least 1.5), else $k = 3$. The rank-$k$ baseline truncates $\Sigma_g$ itself, whose top direction is close to $\mathbf 1$, so in the contrast setting it keeps only $k - 1$ useful directions.
  • Speed. Per frame: one or more engine steps, the PCA projection and the 3D view; clustering and the effective rank every 3rd–4th frame; the heatmap, parts and time series staggered over 8 frames; the readout table is typeset once and then only its numbers change. In headless Chrome without a GPU on a 4-core server this gave about 50 frames per second at the default size and 25–30 at $B = 48$, $N = 250$ (where one step alone takes about 45 ms).
