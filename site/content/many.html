Appendix. With many inputs the fusion scale $b$ sets a resolution, not a cluster count, and the state depends on the path (hysteresis). It supports Perron parts and Reading the weights.

With three inputs and a handful of neurons, the fusion scale $b$ looks like a knob for the number of clusters. With a dozen or two inputs and dozens of neurons it is something else: a length in the space of response functions. It sets which differences between neurons survive, and it resolves finer differences along input directions of high variance. But the state it produces depends on the path to it, and with random attachments to several input modes no range of $b$ holds a stable count of a few clusters, let alone one the inputs set. The exception is a line of neurons along a single input mode: at small stiffness it condenses into a few tight clusters, how many depending on $b$ and on the path.

Throughout, every neuron is attached to $s$ random inputs with fixed shares summing to one, and the weights follow the distance rule

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2, \qquad w_{ij} \ge 0, \\ \text{fixed point } w_{ij} = \frac{(b - D_{ij}^2/2)_+}{\alpha},\end{gathered}$$

with $D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g[(F_i - F_j)^2]$ over the training bank. An edge can be alive only between neurons whose responses are closer than $\sqrt{2b}$, so $b$ is a squared length. A component is a connected component of the live graph. That is not a cluster in the everyday sense: a chain of neighbours, each within $\sqrt{2b}$ of the next, can be much wider than $\sqrt{2b}$. So every count below comes with widths: the largest distance $D$ of a member from its component's mean response (the prototype), and the span, the largest $D$ between two members, divided by $\sqrt{2b}$. A span ratio above one marks a chain.

Resolution: three statements, kept apart

The note's hope, in this model's terms, was that the homeostatic parameters choose which features of the input the network resolves. There is an exact version of that, but it takes three separate statements to say it, and they hold to different standards: the first is an identity, the second is exact at one moment of the dynamics, and the third is where the dynamics move the answer.

(1) Frozen responses: an anisotropic metric

Take linear neurons with no potential. Then $u = Hg$, each response is $F_i(g) = h_i^\top g$, and for any fixed $W$

$$\begin{gathered} D_{ij}^2 = \frac1S \sum_{s} \big(\Delta h^\top g_s\big)^2 = \Delta h^\top \hat C\, \Delta h \\ = \sum_a \hat\lambda_a \big(\Delta h \cdot \hat v_a\big)^2, \\ \Delta h = h_i - h_j, \qquad \hat C = G^\top G/S, \end{gathered}$$

where $(\hat\lambda_a, \hat v_a)$ are the eigenpairs of the training bank's uncentred second moment $\hat C$. This is an identity, not an approximation. With the eigenpairs of $\Sigma_g$ instead it is off by the sampling error of $\hat C$. Because every row of $H$ sums to one, $\Delta h \cdot \mathbf 1 = 0$: the uniform direction never contributes, and the metric sees only contrasts between inputs. The same displacement of two embeddings costs 64 times more along a contrast of variance 4 than along one of variance 1/16.

In the precomputed check ( networks: seeds, two covariances, $W = 0$ and random $W$), the law with $\hat C$ holds to of the largest $D^2$; with $\Sigma_g$ it is off by up to on a training bank of samples, and by against an evaluation bank of (all three relative to the largest training-bank $D^2$); $|\mathbf 1^\top \Delta h| \le$ .

(2) When an edge first comes alive

At $W = 0$ the rule reads $dw_{ij}/dt = b - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2$, so the edge starts to grow exactly when $D_{ij}^2 < 2b$. For two neurons whose uncoupled embeddings differ along one mode only, $\Delta h = \delta\, v_a$, statement (1) turns this into a threshold on their separation:

$$\begin{gathered}\text{edge lives from } W = 0 \iff \delta < \delta_{on}, \\ \delta_{on} = \sqrt{2b/\hat\lambda_a}, \qquad \hat\lambda_a = v_a^\top \hat C v_a.\end{gathered}$$

This is the resolution law in its exact form. At a fixed homeostatic threshold $b$, neurons that differ along a loud direction stay apart even when the difference is small, and neurons that differ along a quiet direction fuse even when it is large. (3) Self-consistent fusion moves the threshold, both ways

Statement (2) only says when an edge starts to grow. Once it grows, the two responses move towards each other, $D_{ij}^2$ falls, and the edge grows further. Where the network ends is a self-consistent state, and it is not where (2) puts it.

(3a) A pair. For two neurons with unit input conductance, $D^2(w) = D_0^2/(1 + 2w)^2$ with $D_0^2 = \hat\lambda_a\delta^2$, and the fusion chapter's analysis of $dw/dt = b - \alpha w - D^2(w)/2$ applies. With $\kappa = \alpha$ (times the input conductance), a fused pair exists down to a saddle-node; at fixed $b > \kappa/4$ it survives up to a separation

$$\begin{gathered}\delta_{off} = \sqrt{\frac{32\,(b + \kappa/2)^3}{27\,\kappa^2\,\hat\lambda_a}}, \\ \frac{\delta_{off}}{\delta_{on}} = \sqrt{\frac{16\,(b + \kappa/2)^3}{27\,\kappa^2\, b}},\end{gathered}$$

while it first fuses at $\delta_{on}$. The ratio does not depend on $\hat\lambda_a$: feedback widens the resolution cell by the same factor along every mode. It is at $\alpha = 0.1$, $b = 0.1$, at $\alpha = 0.01$, $b = 0.1$, and at $\alpha = 1$, $b = 0.1$. Planted pairs swept in $b$ up and then down switch on at most times the grid step away from $b = D_0^2/2$, and off at most times the grid step away from the predicted saddle-node, identically on two modes whose bank variances differ by a factor .

The condensation tour is the one place in all these experiments where discrete, tight clusters formed out of a continuum. The uncoupled chain is connected, so (2) predicts one component or two; as the edges grow, members are pulled towards local centres, the links between those centres stretch past $\sqrt{2b}$ and die, and the line breaks into a few tight pieces. It needs a small stiffness: with $\alpha = 0.1$ and small $b$ the weights stay weak, the neurons barely move and the count stays close to (2), chains included. History matters here too, and it pushes both ways at once: lowering $b$ from a fused line ends with fewer components than (2) predicts, and the cold start at the same $b$ with more. On the down-continuation, watch the bridged-gap tile: a fused component holds neighbours further apart than $\delta_{on}$, which (2) would have kept apart, as the pair calculation (3a) allows.

What this chapter shows

Many inputs, against the note

  • holdsThe homeostatic threshold sets a resolution, and it is anisotropic: from the uncoupled state an edge lives iff $\delta < \sqrt{2b/\hat\lambda_a}$, so the same $b$ resolves finer differences along high-variance input directions. Exact for the training bank's second moment (statements 1 and 2); against $\Sigma_g$ it carries sampling error.
  • amendedSelf-consistent fusion shifts that resolution in both directions: a cold start ends finer than (2) predicts, a downward continuation coarser. For a pair the shift is a closed-form factor that does not depend on the mode's variance.
  • amendedThe state depends on its history. At small $\alpha$ the up and down branches differ over a wide range of $b$, refinement is not nested near percolation, and neither the cold-start nor the fused branch is always the one with the lower objective.
  • fails"The embeddings separate into a few discrete clusters, their number set by the homeostatic parameters." With random attachments the middle range of $b$ is singletons, chains wider than one edge's range, and a growing giant component. Planted blocks do not produce a plateau at the number of blocks, on 12 inputs or 24.
  • openTight clusters nucleate from a continuum in one setting only: neurons along a single mode, at small stiffness (condensation). Whether some richer input geometry does the same is not tested.

The missing ingredient is plain. The basic model has a length scale, $\sqrt{2b}$, but nothing that makes a neuron choose among its inputs: random fixed attachments place the uncoupled responses on a continuum, and cutting a continuum at a length gives chains and a giant component, not a few clusters. Clean clusters appear when the attachments already separate the neurons, for example when each neuron is wired to one block only (the diversity chapter uses such runs); and attachments made plastic under the same energy, with a ridge on each attachment, drift to the quietest inputs instead (allocation chapter). The condensation above shows that feedback alone can cut a continuum into a few tight pieces when the neurons differ along one mode only, but their number moves with $b$ and with history. For neurons that mix several modes at random, something that sorts them by input, competition or a different attachment rule, would have to be added.
