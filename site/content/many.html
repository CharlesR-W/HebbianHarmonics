With three inputs you can watch every embedding move inside its triangle. With 24 inputs each neuron's harmonic embedding is a point in a 23-dimensional simplex, and the questions change: how do you see what the network has learned, and can you read the input statistics back out of its weights? The inputs here are synthetic Gaussian signals whose covariance $\Sigma_g$ I chose: planted blocks, hierarchies, random spectra. The network and the rule are exactly those of chapter 1, with many more boundary neurons, and everything runs live in your browser.

The set-up

Same model as before: $B$ boundary neurons clamped to $g$, $N$ interior neurons with voltages $u = Hg$, and the rule

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = -\eta\,\theta - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min},$$

with $\eta_{ij} = \eta$ for interior–boundary (I–B) synapses and $\gamma\eta$ for interior–interior (I–I) ones. A synapse survives where the covariance of its two ends beats the pruning threshold $\theta$. The start is generic and dense: every potential synapse exists, I–B weights are drawn from an exponential distribution (mean 0.2), and lateral weights start at the floor. By default plasticity sees the exact $\Sigma_g$ (mean-field); under More controls it can instead see the covariance of a minibatch of samples.

The covariance families, all synthetic synthetic Σ:

  • Planted blocks: inputs in groups, correlation within a group and between groups (optionally negative, and optionally unequal block sizes and variances).
  • Hierarchical blocks: groups of groups, correlation 0.6 between siblings, 0.3 one level up, 0.05 across the root.
  • Power-law spectrum: a random orthogonal eigenbasis with eigenvalues $\propto k^{-\alpha}$. Its correlations have both signs; the correlations ≥ 0 variant adds the smallest $c\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ that removes every negative one.
  • Wishart: the sample covariance of a few Gaussian draws, i.e. no structure at all.

One control matters far more than its modest place suggests: sparse wiring, the connection probability $p$. With $p < 1$ a fixed random mask removes potential synapses, so each neuron can only ever touch a random subset of inputs and other neurons. The default is $p = 1$ (dense), and I treat $p < 1$ as a comparison. As the readout section shows, it is what makes reading the weights work.

Seeing a simplex you cannot draw

Take the current embeddings $h_1, \dots, h_N \in \mathbb R^B$, centre them, and project them orthogonally onto their top three principal axes. Between frames the axes are rotated within the new top-3 subspace to match the previous frame, so the picture never flips. The squares are the simplex corners $e_b$ (the boundary neurons) under the same projection. A cluster near one square lives on that input. A cluster between several squares is spread over them. Squares piled up in the middle are inputs the top three axes do not see. An orthogonal projection can only shrink distances, so clusters that look apart are apart; clusters that overlap may not be. The overlay in the corner says how much of the embeddings' variance the three axes hold.
  2. The voltage covariance $H\Sigma_g H^\top$, with neurons sorted by cluster. Clusters show as diagonal blocks. The value inside a block is the cluster's voltage variance $h^\top\Sigma_g h$. Off the blocks is the covariance between clusters, and at a fixed point that is at most $\theta$; otherwise the synapse between them would grow.
  3. Parts. One small panel per cluster, with bars for the cluster's mean embedding over the boundary neurons. This is what the cluster represents: in the network every member's voltage is $h^\top g$. Each panel also shows, as a line, the eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ closest to the part, and prints the cosine between them.
  4. Time series of the cluster count $K$, the effective rank of $H$ (with $K$ orthogonal clusters it equals $K$), and the stationarity residual $r$, which must fall below $10^{-6}$ before I call anything an equilibrium.

Pretend you only have the weights

The note's boldest claim is that you can do weight-space interpretability: given only the weights, compute $H$ and solve for the input covariance. At a fixed point every active synapse satisfies a linear equation in the unknown $C$:

$$\text{I–B: } (HC)_{ib} = \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ib}, \qquad \text{I–I: } \gamma\, h_i^\top C h_j = \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta} w_{ij}.$$

The panel below solves these equations by masked ridge least squares, from the current weights of the run above, about twice a second (in a background thread). It scores the estimate $\hat C$ in the three settings that should be kept apart. Parameters known: $\theta$, $\lambda/\eta$ and $\gamma$ are given, and the full covariance is scored. Unknown scale: $\lambda/\eta$ is unknown, so the best scale is fitted. $\theta$ unknown: then $\hat C$ is only defined up to $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, so both matrices are projected with $P = I - \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/B$ (the contrast-subspace score). Each setting reports the Frobenius cosine and the Bures–Wasserstein distance, and the contrast setting adds the overlap of the top eigenspaces. Bures–Wasserstein on its own misleads (see below), so it is never shown alone.

What a cluster represents: parts, not eigenvectors

The note conjectured that each cluster becomes "isomorphic to the eigenmode of $g$ which it represents". The parts panel answers that live, and the answer is no. Run the default (four planted blocks) or the signed power-law tour and read the cosines under each panel. A cluster's part is a non-negative bump over a group of mutually correlated inputs: flat over one block, zero elsewhere. The eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$ are signed and spread over every input. Bumps and eigenvectors meet only where an eigenvector happens to be non-negative and localised.

Here is the working hypothesis for which bump. Suppose all members of a cluster share one embedding $h$ (they do, up to $O(w_{\min})$, in the dense network). Kirchhoff's law then makes $h$ proportional to the cluster's I–B weights, and at a fixed point each active I–B weight is $(\Sigma_g h)_b - \theta$ (in units of $\eta/\lambda$). Together:

$$h \;\propto\; \big(\Sigma_g h - \theta\,\mathbf 1\big)_+ ,$$

so on its support $S$ the part is the non-negative eigenvector of $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}$: a thresholded Perron vector. Each panel prints the cosine between the part and $(\Sigma_g h - \theta)_+$. In the dense network it reaches 1.000 at convergence. With sparse wiring it is close to 1 but not equal, because each member sees only its own subset of inputs and the part is an average over different members.

The maths: why $h \propto (\Sigma_g h - \theta)_+$ for a tight cluster

Kirchhoff at interior neuron $i$: $\deg_i\, h_i = \sum_j w_{ij} h_j + \sum_b w_{ib} e_b$. If every neighbour $j$ in the cluster has $h_j = h_i = h$, and synapses to other clusters sit at the floor $w_{\min}$, then $h\,(\deg_i - \sum_{j \in \text{cluster}} w_{ij}) = \sum_b w_{ib} e_b + O(w_{\min})$, so $h \propto w_{i\cdot}$. The I–B fixed point sets $w_{ib} = \frac{\eta}{\lambda}\big((\Sigma_g h)_b - \theta\big)$ on active synapses and $w_{ib} = w_{\min}$ where $(\Sigma_g h)_b \le \theta$. Hence $h \propto (\Sigma_g h - \theta)_+ + O(w_{\min})$. Using $\mathbf 1^\top h = 1$, on the support $S$ this reads $(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S$. Which supports are stable, and which of them the population actually finds, is not decided by this equation: the start matters (see the one-block tour).

Key point

A cluster stands for a non-negative bump over a group of correlated inputs, chosen by $\theta$, not for a signed eigenmode of $\Sigma_g$. That makes it closer to a part in non-negative matrix factorisation or sparse PCA than to PCA.

Which bumps appear is set by $\theta$ and by where the population starts, and the tours show both. Two marks on the $\theta$ slider are useful. The first is the correlation levels of $\Sigma_g$. The second, κ₀, is the mean covariance of the initial neurons ($\approx \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$, because the random initial embeddings scatter around the centroid). Below κ₀ every pair of neurons starts with covariance above $\theta$, so all lateral synapses grow, the population moves as one, and it can end on a single part even when several exist. That is "one block takes everything".

What the weights can and cannot tell you

Even in principle, some of $\Sigma_g$ is out of reach. The equations (2) involve $C$ only through $H$, so:

  • Scale. Without $\lambda/\eta$, $\hat C$ is known only up to a factor.
  • Shift. Rows of $H$ sum to one, so with $\theta$ unknown, adding $\theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ to $C$ changes nothing; the contrast score projects that direction out (together with every $\mathbf 1 a^\top + a\mathbf 1^\top$).
  • Inequalities. A synapse at the floor says only that the covariance it saw was at most $\theta$. Pruned synapses give inequalities, not equations, and the readout uses active synapses only.
  • No variances. There are no self-synapses, so the diagonal of $H\Sigma_g H^\top$ is never observed directly.

The live panel counts what is left. The measurement operator $A$ maps a symmetric $C$ to the left-hand sides of (2). Its spectrum says how many of the $B(B+1)/2$ directions of $\Sigma_g$ the active synapses pin down. A direction counts when its eigenvalue of $A^\top A$ exceeds $10^{-5}$ of the largest.

Dense wiring collapses the evidence. Once the embeddings have collapsed into $K$ clusters, all members of a cluster share the same $h$. Their equations are copies of each other, so a cluster contributes only the vector $\Sigma_g h$ restricted to its own support $S_k$: $|S_k|$ numbers, however many neurons it has. At the default setting that is $4 \times 6 = 24$ of 300 directions. The difference heatmap shows what is missing: inside each block the readout knows only the block's row average, and every within-block entry comes out the same. The between-block entries are recovered only through the $O(w_{\min})$ tails of $h$ that floor synapses leak in. That is a numerical accident, not information the network holds: lower $w_{\min}$ to $10^{-6}$ under More controls and they disappear.

Sparse wiring keeps the evidence. With a random mask ($p = 0.5$) the clusters are the same four, but members differ because each reads its own random subset of inputs. Every member is then a different probe of $\Sigma_g$, and the readout becomes nearly exact. Caveat: Bures–Wasserstein alone flatters the identity

In the table the identity matrix scores BW 0.138 under the contrast setting, much better than the dense readout's 0.535, even though it knows nothing about $\Sigma_g$: its eigenspace overlap is 0.03. BW punishes an estimate that is right on a few directions and silent on the rest more than one that is vaguely spread over all of them. That is why the cosine and the overlap always sit next to it.

Two more rows of the live table are worth a look. The derivative-aware readout also uses how fast each weight is moving. Early in a run, before anything has been pruned, it recovers $\Sigma_g$ exactly (cosine 1.000): the information is there while the network learns, and pruning and collapse throw most of it away. The note's own formula, $\hat C = H^+ R H^{+\top}$, treats every lateral weight as an equation (pruned ones included) and has to guess the unobserved diagonal of $R$. It does reasonably in the dense network but badly with sparse wiring, where absent synapses look like zero weights.

Key point

Weight-space interpretability works, but only if neurons stay diverse. In the dense network a cluster is a set of identical copies, and the weights hold one message per cluster. With sparse wiring every neuron is a different random probe, and $\Sigma_g$ can be read back almost exactly. Sparse wiring also turns the note's "expansion code" from redundancy into an actual reservoir.

What to notice

  • θ sweep on blocks: one cluster, then one per block, then one per input. The thresholds are not just the correlation levels: below κ₀ the population moves as one.
  • Signed Σ at θ = 0: with no pruning drift at all, negative covariances alone split the population in two. This is pruning by anticorrelation, as distinct from pruning by drift ($\theta > 0$). Make every correlation non-negative and the split disappears.
  • One block takes everything: eight equally strong blocks, yet only one gets neurons. Allocation follows the start, not the input energy.
  • θ walks down a tree when every $\theta$ starts afresh; without restarting the population never splits and follows one branch down. The fixed point you reach depends on the path.
  • Dense against sparse wiring: the same clusters, very different readouts. Watch the measurement spectrum: a cliff after 24 directions when dense, a long tail when sparse.
  • The 3D view keeps its frame while the clusters form: clusters drift toward the squares of the inputs they end up representing.
  • In minibatch mode the residual never reaches the tolerance, and the readout panel says the equilibrium assumption is violated, as it should.

Engineering choices in this chapter
  • Integration. Euler steps with $dt = 0.2$ in mean-field mode (fixed points match smaller steps; see the engine notes) and $0.05$ with minibatches, $\lambda = \eta = 1$. At ×1 the sim runs one step per frame; faster speeds run up to 16 steps within a 14 ms budget.
  • Equilibrium. Claimed only when the projected residual $r/\eta = \max|dw/dt|/\eta$ is below $10^{-6}$; the run pauses at $10^{-8}$.
  • Clusters. Single linkage on the $L_1$ distance between embeddings, cut at the largest multiplicative gap in the merge heights (at least 0.05); "not clustered yet" while that gap is below 3×. Colours are carried from frame to frame by majority vote of members.
  • Projection. PCA of the current embeddings, Procrustes-aligned to the previous frame within the new top-3 subspace.
  • Readout. Masked ridge least squares over symmetric $C$ by conjugate gradients (ridge $10^{-6}$ relative), warm-started from the previous solve, in a web worker; for $\theta$ unknown the ridge acts only on $PCP$ ("project" mode), which is exact for $\gamma = 1$.
  • Measurement spectrum. The Gram matrix $A^\top A$ is accumulated per neuron rather than per synapse (the same matrix, checked against the engine to $10^{-14}$) and its eigenvalues found without eigenvectors. Even so, at $B \ge 32$ ($528$ or $1{,}176$ directions) it takes seconds, so it is recomputed only while the run is paused or converged.
  • Subspace overlap. $k$ is placed at the largest eigen-gap among the first six eigenvalues of $P\Sigma_g P$ (ratio at least 1.5), else $k = 3$.
