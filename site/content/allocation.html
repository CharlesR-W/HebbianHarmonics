When some inputs are louder than others, does the network give them more neurons? We tried three versions of the model on synthetic inputs and found no allocation law. With fixed attachments the wiring sets the count and a louder block is only cut finer; when the attachments learn, the neurons drain to the quietest inputs; and an added objective gives a law for how much variance each input direction receives, but not for how many neurons it gets.

With fixed attachments, the wiring decides

Take 12 inputs in four blocks of three. Inside block $k$ the inputs have variance $v_k$ and correlation $\rho = 0.8$; different blocks are uncorrelated. Each neuron is attached to the three inputs of one block with random shares, so the number of neurons directly attached to each block is fixed by design. Only the lateral weights learn, by the distance rule, and at the fixed point a synapse survives only between neurons whose responses differ by less than the edge range: $D_{ij} < \sqrt{2b}$. Neurons joined by live synapses form a component. A synapse between two blocks could pass one block's signals to the other's neurons, but in all … sweep runs below, … component joined two blocks, so each neuron responds to its own block only. Inside a block, distances then grow with the block's loudness:

$$D_{ij}^2 = v_k\,(1-\rho)\,\lVert h_i - h_j\rVert^2 ,$$

where $h_i$ is neuron $i$'s mixture of the block's three inputs. The edge range does not change with $v_k$, so a louder block is resolved more finely. It breaks into more, smaller components, and it gains no neurons.

Make block 1 quiet and its twelve neurons end in one component; attach more neurons to it and its components grow. The precomputed sweep (… seeds per point, from $W = 0$) agrees: at b, block 1 keeps exactly its n attached neurons at every variance, while its number of components goes from … at $v_1 = $ … to … at $v_1 = $ ….

With random attachments (each neuron on 3 of the 12 inputs, regardless of block) the same effect shows as a weak trend: larger components have quieter mean responses. The Spearman correlation between component size and the variance of the component's mean response is … at $b = 0.1$, … at $b = 0.05$ and … at $b = 0.02$ (components of at least two neurons, five seeds pooled). Its sign is the opposite of "more neurons for more energy".

When the attachments learn, neurons drain to the quiet inputs

Now let the attachments $a_{ib}$ learn by the same principle as the lateral weights, with a ridge $\alpha_A$ on each attachment; after each step, each neuron's row of attachments is projected back onto the simplex (non-negative, summing to 1):

$$\frac{da_{ib}}{dt} = -\alpha_A\, a_{ib} - \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_g\big[(u_i - g_b)^2\big], \quad \text{then } a_i \to \text{nearest point with } a_i \ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_b a_{ib} = 1 .$$

For a neuron without lateral synapses, the energy this rule lowers is half the spread of the inputs it listens to around their weighted mean. That is zero for a neuron on a single input, loud or quiet: nothing rewards listening to a loud input. The ridge charges for putting the whole row on one input, so rows spread out, and spreading costs least over quiet, well-correlated inputs.

The cost in one line

With linear neurons and no lateral synapses, $u_i = a_i^\top g$ and neuron $i$'s energy at equilibrium is $E_i = \tfrac12\big(\sum_b a_{ib}\Sigma_{bb} - a_i^\top\Sigma_g a_i\big)$. Inside block $k$, $E_i + \tfrac{\alpha_A}{2}\lVert a_i\rVert^2 = \tfrac12 v_k(1-\rho)(1-s) + \tfrac{\alpha_A}{2}s$ with $s = \lVert a_i\rVert^2 \in [\tfrac13, 1]$. So the best row inside block $k$ is pure (cost $\alpha_A/2$) if $\alpha_A < v_k(1-\rho)$, and spread evenly otherwise, at cost $v_k(1-\rho)/3 + \alpha_A/6$, lowest for the quietest block. Each neuron's objective is strictly convex when $\alpha_A > \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_g)$ (a sufficient condition), and then every neuron converges to the same row.

With lateral learning on (the toggle), the quiet block still dominates at the default ridge. A strong ridge, above the largest eigenvalue of $\Sigma_g$, makes every row the same spread row; its largest share is still the quiet block's, but it is no majority. A weak ridge lets some neurons keep pure attachments to louder inputs. Which input depends on the starting row, so that is a local minimum, not an allocation.

An added objective gives a law for variance, not for neurons

Nothing in the network's energy rewards signal, so this section adds an objective from outside the model. Write neuron $i$'s linear response in whitened coordinates, $F_i(g) = x_i^\top \Sigma_g^{-1/2} g$, so that the covariance of two responses is $x_i^\top x_j$. In the eigenbasis of $\Sigma_g$ (eigenvalues $\lambda_a$), ask the population to reproduce the input covariance with every neuron held to the same variance $\nu$:

$$\min_X\ \tfrac12\,\big\lVert \Sigma_g - X^\top X \big\rVert_F^2 \qquad \text{subject to}\quad \lVert x_i\rVert^2 = \nu \ \text{ for every neuron } i .$$

The variance the population spends on mode $a$, $q_a = \sum_i x_{ia}^2$, then follows water-filling: $q_a = (\lambda_a - \mu)_+$, with the level $\mu$ set by the budget, $\sum_a q_a = N\nu$. Modes below the level get nothing; each mode above it gets its excess. A law for neuron counts, $n_a = q_a/\nu$, would follow only if every neuron were pure, with all its variance on one mode. The objective does not care how a mode's variance is shared among neurons, and the optimiser lands on mixed neurons.

Why water-filling is exact under the hard constraint, and why mixed neurons are just as good

Relax $X^\top X$ to any $M \succeq 0$ with $\operatorname{Tr} M = N\nu$: the best $M$ is diagonal in the eigenbasis, with $q$ the Euclidean projection of $\lambda$ onto $\{q \ge 0,\ \sum_a q_a = N\nu\}$, which is water-filling (if $N\nu > \sum_a \lambda_a$, $\mu < 0$ and every mode is over-represented by $-\mu$). The relaxed optimum has rank $r \le B \le N$, and the row norms of $X$ are the diagonal of the $N \times N$ Gram matrix $XX^\top$, whose eigenvalues are $(q_1, \dots, q_r, 0, \dots, 0)$. By the Schur–Horn theorem such a matrix with constant diagonal $\nu$ exists, since a constant vector is majorised by every vector of the same length and sum; so the hard-constrained minimum equals the relaxed one. Replacing $X$ by $QX$, with $Q$ orthogonal and keeping every row norm at $\nu$, changes nothing, and almost all such solutions have neurons spread over several modes.

We tried three ingredients that could make neurons pure: a fusion term (the base model's lateral energy, $-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{i<j}\big(b - \tfrac12\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert^2\big)_+^2$, which pulls nearby neurons together), non-negativity in the eigenbasis ($x_{ia} \ge 0$; told where the modes are, it forces pure neurons whenever $X^\top X$ is diagonal), and, as a control, non-negativity in a randomly rotated basis.

With no ingredient the masses sit on the law to solver precision in every setting of the sweep (largest deviation …), while only … to … of the neurons are pure. The ingredients help only in some settings. The weak fusion term keeps the law; it raises the share of pure neurons at $\nu = 0.1$ but lowers it at $\nu = 0.05$. The strong fusion term makes every neuron pure at $\nu = 0.05$, with the wrong counts and the masses far off the law, but purifies only a minority at $\nu = 0.2$. Non-negativity in the eigenbasis makes every neuron pure at every budget, and moves the masses off the law. The rotated constraint gives no pure neurons at all.

Limits

All inputs are synthetic, with at most 16 modes and 80 neurons. The pure allocation with the best integer counts exists under non-negativity, but projected gradient from random starts does not find it, and we did not try a global search, a soft variance penalty, or an added objective derived from the network's own energy. Neuron counts equal variance per mode only when neurons specialise.
