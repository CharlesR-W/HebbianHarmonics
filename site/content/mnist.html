You asked for MNIST with a kernel lift whose size is a knob: train the network, read the lifted covariance back out of the weights, and look at the embeddings, their similarity matrix and the graph. I lifted 14×14 digits two ways, the pixels themselves (a linear kernel, $B = 196$) and random Fourier features of a Gaussian kernel ($D = 64$, 256, 1024), and trained 114 networks across $\theta$, $N$, the floor and two initial weightings, then solved each one's weights for $\Sigma_g$ and drew samples from the result. The outcome is narrower than the note hoped: $\theta$ resolves at most about 12 tiny parts before the network dies, no "fragmented" regime is ever reached, every random-Fourier lift gives exactly two parts (a "1" detector and a "0" detector), and the weights give back $\Sigma_g$ accurately on the parts' pixels and essentially nowhere else.

Read it left to right: for the first few steps every neuron sees nearly the same mixture of pixels, so the matrix is flat. By step 11 a few dozen neurons have begun to localise; by step 69 most have lost every input and joined a single grey block (the dead pool), and what survives is a diagonal of small, sharply bounded blocks: 12 parts, each a set of identical neurons.

The sweep: one part, a few parts, a dead pool, extinction

Each lift is scaled by one constant so the mean feature variance is 1, so $\theta$ is in units of a typical feature's variance; the pixel $\theta$ values sit at the 94th–99.3rd percentile of the off-diagonal entries of $\Sigma_g$, the RFF values at the 64th–95th. The dynamics are the base model exactly, with every synapse potential, $\gamma = \lambda = \eta = 1$ and the floor $w_{\min} = \omega/N$. Each run takes 3,000 minibatch steps (or uses the exact $\Sigma_g$ in mean-field mode) and is read out on its weights averaged over the last 600 steps.

Three things hold across the grid. At low $\theta$ there is one part on the whole stroke region: at $\theta = 0.3$ every pixel network converges onto a "0"-shaped ring, the Perron vector of the high-variance strokes. Raising $\theta$ gives two or three large parts, then something the note did not anticipate: most neurons lose all their inputs before they have localised, and join the dead pool, a floor-coupled clump sitting at the centroid. The survivors form 7–12 small parts, and one step further (about $\theta = 1.1$ for pixels) the whole network is extinct, although single pixels with variance up to 2.98 would still be stable parts. Starting near the centroid, a neuron's boundary drive $(\Sigma_g h)_b$ is about a row mean of $\Sigma_g$, at most 0.16, far below $\theta$, so its weights decay before its embedding can concentrate.

Key point

On real data $\theta$ does not walk through ever finer partitions. The cluster count peaks at about 12 just below extinction (16 in the mean-field twin); the "fragmented" regime is never reached. A heavy-tailed initial weighting roughly doubles the number of populated parts and moves extinction from about $\theta = 0.75$ to about 1.0, so which parts are populated is a property of the transient, not of $\theta$ alone.

Random Fourier features change none of this, except that there are always exactly two large parts: at every $D$, bandwidth and init, then the dead pool, then extinction. The feature covariance is symmetric about zero (half of all pairs are anticorrelated), and since conductances cannot be negative, a part must be a set of mutually positively correlated features.

One network, over training

The figure opens on the generic dense init ($W_{IB} \sim \mathrm{Exp}$, mean 0.1), the default you asked for, at the $\theta$ where it populates most parts; the headline run uses the heavy-tailed init at $\theta = 0.9$. All eight fully exported runs have $N = 2048$.

The map makes the dead pool unmistakable: as $\theta$ rises past about 0.6 (pixels), most of the map collapses onto one grey point, and the live parts are small, tight islands. Every cluster is a set of clones: within-cluster $L_1$ spread is about $10^{-5}$ (9.6×10⁻⁶ in the headline run), so the 2,048 neurons compute at most a dozen distinct functions. That agrees with the synthetic chapters: redundancy, not a reservoir of different random functions.

How clusters, the dead pool and the displayed neurons are defined

Clusters are the single-linkage components of "$L_1(h_i, h_j) < 0.1$", which does not depend on any ordering. In all 114 runs the counts at tolerance 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 are identical and equal the leader clustering (hebb.core.clusters). Live clusters exclude the dead pool; major clusters hold at least 1% of $N$. The dead pool is a real cluster: neurons whose every synapse is at the floor, floating at the floor-weighted centroid of everything, with energy far below $\theta$. It is flagged and drawn grey, never counted as a learned part. Displayed neurons: 256 per run in one fixed order, stratified so every cluster has at least $\min(\text{size}, 4)$ members and the rest are proportional to size, so counts in the sample are not cluster sizes; the figures always quote clusters[].size. Stationarity residual: the mean-field drift $|dw/dt|$ at the snapshot weights, projected onto the floor constraint. Minibatch runs end at their noise floor (headline: mean $1.9\times10^{-4}$, but max 0.46 on a few noisy edges); the mean-field twins reach 0.

What the neurons became, as images

For the pixel lift a harmonic embedding $h_i$ is a probability vector over the 196 pixels, so it is literally a 14×14 image. For RFF it is a vector over features, so each part is shown through its response instead: the average stimulus weighted by how strongly the part responds, the test digits it responds to most, and its mean response per digit class. The eigen-images of $\Sigma_g$ sit alongside, because the note expected each population to be "isomorphic to an eigenmode".

The pixel parts are blobs of 2–3 pixels at high-variance stroke locations. Their top digits are mostly heavy-ink 8s, 0s and 3s: they detect ink at a place, not a digit class. None resembles an eigen-image; each is a nonnegative, localised part of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a small support, as on the synthetic covariances. The two RFF parts are a "1" detector and a "0" detector: the two signed ends of the leading kernel-PCA axis, split into two nonnegative parts.

The graph, and who gets the neurons

The note guessed that neurons would be allocated in proportion to mode energy. Here every part's energy $h_k^\top\Sigma_g h_k$ is pinned just above $\theta$ (1.04–1.37 in the headline run, i.e. $\theta$ plus the within-cluster weight), while the part sizes vary 11×. The rank correlation of size with energy is 0.69, but over that tiny range of energies it says little: how many neurons a part gets is inherited from which initial basin they started in, as in the synthetic chapters. With a second seed the sizes change.

Pretend you only have the weights

The note's recipe: at equilibrium every active synapse states one equation in $C$, so solve for it. I use masked ridge least squares over symmetric $C$ on the active edges ($w > 2w_{\min}$): $(HC)_{ib} = \theta + w_{ib}$ for input synapses and $h_i^\top C h_j = (\theta + w_{ij})/\gamma$ for lateral ones, each family's squared residual averaged over its equations so the roughly two million lateral equations do not swamp the input ones. Then score $\hat C$ against $\Sigma_g$ in three settings (parameters known; scale unknown; $\theta$ unknown, the contrast score) with Frobenius cosine, Bures–Wasserstein and subspace overlap side by side.

Told straight:

  • It is no better than knowing the variances. Across all live runs the contrast cosine is 0.28–0.61. For the headline run it is 0.50, against 0.52 for diag $\Sigma_g$, 0.35 for the identity and 0.96 for the rank-$K$ truncation of $\Sigma_g$ (the best a $K$-part sketch could hope for). With the parameters known, the relative Frobenius error is 0.86 (identity 0.94, diag 0.86, rank-$K$ 0.26).
  • For RFF it does beat the trivial baselines: 0.49 against 0.20 for diag at $D = 1024$.
  • Bures–Wasserstein alone misleads. BW calls the readout worse than the identity or diag in every run (0.66–0.94 against 0.24–0.70), because $\hat C$ is nearly zero off the parts and BW punishes missing variance hardest. Cosine and subspace overlap tell the other half of the story.
  • Where it measures, it is accurate. Restricted to the 30 pixels the live parts cover, $\hat C$ reproduces $\Sigma_g$ with cosine 0.84 (0.75–0.84 across the featured pixel runs), and the median part energy is off by 0.3%. 77% of $\hat C$'s mass sits on that block, which holds only 30% of the mass of $P\Sigma_gP$.
  • The limit is rank. The headline weights give 67 independent equations for 19,306 unknowns: 12 from the lateral synapses (one per part: its energy) and 55 from the input synapses, the rows $(\Sigma_g h_k)_b$ on each part's support. Clones multiply the equations (about 22,000 active lateral ones), not the rank. The weights store a parts-sketch, not $\Sigma_g$.
  • Best mid-training. The training curves above include the readout of the instantaneous weights at every snapshot: in the headline run the contrast cosine climbs from 0.35 to about 0.58 near step 50, then falls back as pruning proceeds. Those mid-training readouts violate the equilibrium assumption the estimator rests on, and are labelled as such.
Other estimators: the note's pseudo-inverse and the parts-sum sketch both do worse here

The note's formula $C = H^+RH^{+\top}$ (with $R$ from all lateral weights, clipped ones included, and the diagonal filled with the row maximum) and its input analogue $\mathrm{sym}(H^+R_{IB})$ are worse than least squares in every live run. The parts-sum sketch $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k h_k h_k^\top/\lVert h_k\rVert^2$, with $c_k$ a member's summed input conductance, is also worse in every run (headline 0.41 against 0.50; RFF $D = 1024$: 0.39 against 0.49; from docs/mnist.md, not in the exports). This disagrees with the synthetic finding that the parts-sum is the better estimator: there the parts were disjoint blocks of a nonnegative $\Sigma_g$; MNIST parts overlap and $\Sigma_g$ is signed, and least squares uses every row $(\Sigma_g h_k)_b$ on a support (a rank-55 sketch here), while the parts-sum keeps rank at most $K + 1$. The input-synapse family carries almost everything (least squares on input synapses alone ≈ both families), and the readout of the untrained network equals the identity baseline. No hinge penalty for the clipped edges was used and no PSD projection inside the fit; negative eigenvalues are clipped before BW (10–15% of the mass).

Moment-based sample synthesis under explicit generative assumptions

You asked to sample from the distribution the weights encode, for example to reconstruct digits. Second moments do not determine $p(x)$, so every generator here adds a model, and the samples inherit it. Two things come from outside the weights. The mean: Hebb sees the centred covariance, so no synapse stores it; I supply the training mean. The completion: for pixels, $x \sim \mathcal N(\bar x, \hat C)$ in pixel units, with negative eigenvalues clipped, unconstrained directions at zero variance, and samples clipped to $[0, 1]$. For RFF, a Gaussian draw $g \sim \mathcal N(\mu_g, \hat C)$ in feature space and then a pre-image, $x^\ast = \arg\min_{x \in [0,1]^{196}} \lVert\phi(x) - g\rVert^2$ by projected Adam (300 steps, learning rate 0.02, from the mean image, the same for every row). That is exploratory: a Gaussian $g$ is generally not the image of any digit, so the optimiser, its start and the box shape the result.

For pixels the weight-reconstructed samples look like the mean digit with a few flickering blobs: the generator carries only 21–35% of the true variance, almost all of it on the parts' pixels. It is worse than the diag baseline in MMD and improves as more parts are populated. Its samples do match their own target covariance (to sampling noise), but that target is 80–85% away from $\Sigma_g$: the readout error, showing up as samples. For RFF the inverse map is fine (real digits survive the round trip with residual $10^{-6}$), but Gaussian feature vectors are far from the image of $\phi$, and the reconstructed samples are blurry blends of a 0 and a 1, the two learned parts. Blurry digit-like blobs are the honest ceiling of any second-moment generator here; the true-moment row shows that ceiling.

What this says about the note

What we found

  • amended"The embeddings separate into discrete clusters, their number set by the homeostatic parameters." On digits, yes, but the parts are tiny blobs of 2–3 pixels (pixels) or two detectors (RFF), never a rich set of modes. $\theta$ moves the count from 1 to at most about 12, and then the network dies: extinction, not fragmentation.
  • fails"Each population becomes isomorphic to an eigenmode." Parts are nonnegative localised blobs; no part looks like an eigen-image of $\Sigma_g$.
  • fails"Neurons in proportion to energy." Energies sit in a narrow band just above $\theta$ while sizes vary 11×; sizes follow the initial basins.
  • fails"An expansion code: many different random functions per mode." Members are clones (spread about $10^{-5}$).
  • amended"Solve the weights for $\mathrm{Cov}(g)$." The readout is exact on what the weights measure and blind elsewhere: it is rank-limited by the number of populated parts, so on MNIST it scores about like diag $\Sigma_g$ (pixels) or modestly better than it (RFF).
  • fails"With a rich lift the covariance gives $p(\text{stimulus})$." Not from the centred covariance, and here not even the covariance comes back; samples are the mean digit plus blobs.
  • openStochastic against mean-field. They agree away from transitions and differ near them (pixels, $N = 2048$, $\theta = 0.6$: 6 live parts and 807 dead in mean-field, 3 live and none dead with minibatches), as in the synthetic chapters.
