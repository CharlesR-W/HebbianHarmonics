Take the note's rule literally: Hebb's covariance rule with a pruning threshold $\theta$, on the linear network of the idea chapter. The neurons end up in clusters of identical copies. Where each cluster sits can be worked out by hand, and the answer is not an eigenvector of the input covariance. It is a part: the Perron vector of a block of the thresholded covariance $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. This chapter explains what that means, which blocks qualify, and why, for hierarchically correlated inputs, $\theta$ picks a level of the hierarchy.

The rule only sees a thresholded covariance

A neuron's voltage is $u_i = h_i^\top g$, with $h_i$ its harmonic embedding, so the covariance of two neurons is $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$. Hebb's rule with a constant negative drift is

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = \eta\big(h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j - \theta\big) - \lambda w_{ij}, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}.$$

A synapse grows when its two ends covary by more than $\theta$, and otherwise shrinks to a small floor $w_{\min}$. A synapse from neuron $i$ to input $b$ uses $(\Sigma_g h_i)_b$ in place of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$. Lateral synapses (neuron to neuron) can learn with a different gain $\gamma$, which makes their threshold $\theta/\gamma$; take $\gamma = 1$ until the last section.

Every $h_i$ sums to one, so $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \theta = h_i^\top(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)h_j$. The rule never sees $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$ separately, only

$$A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top .$$

Raising $\theta$ lowers every entry of $A$ by the same amount. Pairs of inputs that covary by less than $\theta$ turn negative: the rule treats them as anticorrelated. In the figure, the red blocks of $A$ are groups of inputs that covary by more than $\theta$; they are what the rest of the chapter is about.

Where a cluster can sit

Suppose the neurons have formed clusters, each a set of identical copies with one embedding $h$, and the lateral synapses between clusters have shrunk to the floor. Then three facts fix $h$.

  1. Copies are at equal voltage for every input, so the synapses among them carry no current. With the floor negligible, no current reaches the cluster from other clusters either. Kirchhoff's law then says $h$ is the cluster's input weights, normalised: $h_b \propto w_{ib}$.
  2. Each input synapse is at the fixed point of (1): $w_{ib} \propto (A h)_b$ where it is above the floor. Combined with 1, on the support $S$ of $h$ (the inputs the cluster listens to), $A_{SS}h_S = c\,h_S$: $h_S$ is an eigenvector of the block of $A$ on $S$, with $c$ the cluster's total input conductance.
  3. An input outside $S$ has its synapse at the floor, which needs $(A h)_b \le 0$: no outside input covaries with the cluster by more than $\theta$. If one did, the cluster would grow a synapse to it and $S$ would not be its support.
$$A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S,\quad h_S > 0,\ c > 0; \qquad (A h)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S.$$

A solution is a part. A part is stable when $c$ is the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ (so $h_S$ is its Perron vector; any other direction would grow) and the outside inequality is strict. Neither the number of neurons nor the lateral weights appear in (3): where a cluster can sit is a property of $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$ alone.With a finite floor each neuron leaks to the population mean through total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$, which shifts parts slightly. Every simulation here keeps $\omega = 0.009$.

On three inputs every candidate part can be listed. Independent inputs give only the corners: a support of two inputs has a positive vector with eigenvalue $1 - 2\theta$ and a contrast with eigenvalue $1$, so the contrast wins. Correlate $g_1$ and $g_2$ by $\rho$ and the midpoint of their edge becomes a part while $\theta < \rho$; it is then the top eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ as well, but the corner $g_3$ is a part too, and the signed eigenvector $(1, -1, 0)$ never is. Move $\theta$ past $\rho$ and the midpoint gives way to the corners $g_1$ and $g_2$, neither of which is an eigenvector.

Which groups of inputs: $\theta$ cuts the correlation tree

Condition 3 has a short reading. Draw a graph on the inputs, joining $a$ and $b$ when $\Sigma_{ab} > \theta$; call it $G_\theta$. Then:

  • A stable part never straddles two pieces of $G_\theta$. If its support spans two groups with no edge between them, the block of $A$ coupling them is $\le 0$, and flipping the sign of $h$ on one group raises $h^\top A h$: the positive vector is not the top one.
  • A stable part inside a coherent group takes all of it. If every input in a group covaries with every other by more than $\theta$, a part made of some of them must contain them all: a left-out member covaries with the part by more than $\theta$, which breaks condition 3.

For a hierarchical covariance these two facts settle everything. Say the inputs are the leaves of a tree and the correlation of two inputs depends only on how far up the tree they meet (an ultrametric). Cut the tree at height $\theta$: each branch below the cut is a group whose pairs all covary above $\theta$, and different branches covary below it. On a branch $A$ is entrywise positive, so its Perron vector is positive and on top; outside inputs covary with it below $\theta$. So the stable parts at $\theta$ are exactly the branches of the cut. Nothing else is stable. The diagram below makes this visible: at each $\theta$, a bar for every stable part over the inputs it covers.

Checked by exact enumeration (all $2^B - 1$ supports) on 29 ultrametric trees with up to 16 inputs, 120–200 values of $\theta$ each: no exceptions, except at $\theta$ exactly equal to a level, where the parent's top eigenvalue is degenerate and nothing at that level is stable. It also survives unequal variances (the Perron vector stops being uniform, and an input whose variance is below $\theta$ is left out).

Without an exact tree the picture blurs at the edges. Add noise of size $\pm 0.03$ to the 16-input hierarchy and the stable parts still match the branches, except within about $0.03$ of a level. On a ring, where correlation falls off smoothly with distance, there is no tree: the parts are bumps, as many as there are inputs, and their width shrinks steadily as $\theta$ rises (choose "ring" above).

So is raising $\theta$ the same as solving the problem again on each block? Statically, yes, for any $\Sigma_g$. Take a piece $S$ of $G_\theta$ and a larger threshold $\theta'$. Every input outside $S$ covaries with every input in $S$ by at most $\theta < \theta'$, so condition 3 holds for them automatically, for any candidate inside $S$. What is left is the same problem with $\Sigma_g$ replaced by its block on $S$. The stable parts are therefore a recursion down the tree of pieces of $G_\theta$ (the single-linkage dendrogram of the correlations), and $\theta$ selects the level. Whether a network actually walks down that tree is a separate question, about dynamics.

Which parts get populated

The parts are the places a cluster can rest. Which of them the network actually fills depends on how it gets there, and lateral synapses decide most of it. A lateral synapse grows while its two ends covary by more than $\theta/\gamma$. Early in learning, embeddings are spread out and two random neurons covary by about $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$, the mean entry of $\Sigma_g$. If $\theta < \gamma\kappa_0$, lateral synapses grow before any neuron has chosen a part, the population binds into one body, and one body can only fill one part.

From a fresh start (300 neurons, the 16-input hierarchy with levels 0.8, 0.5, 0.25, 0.05, so $\kappa_0 = 0.26$), the number of parts filled is a function of $\theta$ and $\gamma$: it was the same in every seed at 28–29 of 30 values of $\theta$. Which of several equal parts gets filled depends on the seed; where parts differ in $c$, the largest wins (24 of 24 seeds). With $\gamma = 1$ everything binds into one cluster below $\theta \approx 0.22$, and a second round of binding inside each branch leaves gaps above it: 2 of 4 quarters filled for $\theta$ between 0.25 and 0.38. Weaker lateral learning, $\gamma = 0.1$, fills every stable part from $\theta = 0.065$ up to $0.82$. Two artefacts of the floor show at the top of the range: just below the 0.8 level ($\theta \approx 0.79$) neurons stay pinned to single inputs, which are not parts there, and above $\theta \approx 0.86$ most neurons die.

Moving $\theta$ slowly is the direct test of refinement. Going down, the tree is followed exactly: start with all 16 single inputs filled at $\theta = 0.82$ and lower $\theta$ in 80 steps; at the levels 0.5, 0.25 and 0.05 the children merge into their parent within one step (at the top level, 0.8, about 0.03 late, an effect of the floor), and every stable part stays filled throughout. Going up, it is not. With $\gamma = 1$ the whole population is one cluster at low $\theta$, and at each level it falls as a body into one child: 1 of 2, 1 of 4, 1 of 8, 1 of 16. Coverage halves at every level. Weak lateral learning helps but does not fix it: with $\gamma = 0.1$ the ramp reaches 2 of 2, 3 of 4, 6 of 8 and about 10 of 16, with very unequal sizes.

Why can a cluster not split? Just past a level the parent part has lost stability, but only barely: the push towards the children grows from zero. Holding the copies together are the lateral synapses (pruned only if $\theta/\gamma$ exceeds their covariance) and the floor, which ties every neuron to the population mean. The push loses. Two tests show it: jumping $\theta$ two steps past the level, or lowering the floor tenfold, splits the cluster almost evenly (159 and 141 neurons), for $\gamma = 0.5$ and $0.1$ alike; with $\gamma = 1$ it never splits.

So $\theta$ is a resolution knob in the sense that it fixes which groups of inputs can be represented, level by level down the correlation tree. The network realises the whole level when it comes from coarser to finer only if lateral synapses are weak and the change is abrupt; coming from finer to coarser, or from a fresh start with weak lateral learning, it realises it fully. Refinement is history-dependent, not a fresh solve on each block.

Clones and allocation

Members of a cluster are exact copies, so a cluster is one function repeated, not a reservoir of different features. And how many neurons each part receives is inherited from the start: at equilibrium any split among compatible parts is a fixed point. Both, with the attempts to break them, are in the appendix, More on the covariance rule.

What the covariance rule does

  • holdsA cluster sits on a part: the Perron vector of $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ on a group of inputs, with no outside input covarying with it above $\theta$. Not, in general, an eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$.
  • holdsFor hierarchically correlated inputs the stable parts are exactly the branches of the correlation tree cut at height $\theta$; in general they recurse down the pieces of the graph $\Sigma_{ab} > \theta$.
  • amendedWhich parts are filled is decided by lateral binding: below $\theta \approx \gamma\kappa_0$ the population is one cluster. Lowering $\theta$ merges clusters exactly along the tree; raising it usually does not split them.
  • failsClusters as reservoirs of different features, and allocation set by the parameters (appendix).
