Take the note's rule literally: Hebb's covariance rule, with a constant pruning drift, on the linear resistor network of the idea chapter. The network organises itself into clusters of identical neurons, and the place each cluster sits is not an eigenvector of the input covariance. It is a part: a non-negative Perron vector of a principal submatrix of the thresholded covariance $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. This chapter derives that, watches it on three inputs, and checks it on networks of 300–400 neurons with up to 16 inputs.

The covariance rule

Every interior voltage is a fixed linear function of the input, $u_i = h_i^\top g$, where $h_i$, row $i$ of the harmonic kernel $H$, is a probability vector over the inputs (the idea chapter). So the covariance Hebb's rule needs is an inner product of embeddings, and the rule, with a regulariser that depends on the weight only, reads

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = \eta\big(h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j - \theta\big) - \lambda w_{ij}, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}.$$

A synapse onto input $b$ sees $(\Sigma_g h_i)_b$ in place of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$; lateral synapses learn with gain $\gamma$, so their threshold is $\theta/\gamma$. With $\theta > 0$ a synapse grows only where its two neurons covary by more than $\theta$, and otherwise decays to the floor $w_{\min}$. Because every row of $H$ sums to one, adding $c$ to every entry of $\Sigma_g$ adds exactly $c$ to every covariance the rule sees, so (with $\gamma = 1$) the network only ever sees

$$A = \Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top .$$

Pruning by $\theta$ and pruning by negative covariances are one mechanism: $\theta$ is measured from the level the inputs share.In the live figure: $\lambda = \eta = 1$, Euler steps $dt = 0.05$, mean-field (exact $\Sigma_g$ every step). The floor matters: every clipped synapse still conducts, so each neuron leaks towards the population mean with total conductance $\omega = N w_{\min}$. The big runs below keep $\omega = 0.009$, as in the live figure, whatever $N$.

Clusters are parts, not eigenvectors

The note expected one cluster per eigenmode of the input. What a cluster sits on is fixed by a short calculation. Inside a cluster all members share one embedding $h$, so they are at equal voltage for every stimulus and the lateral synapses among them carry no current. If the synapses to other clusters are pruned to the floor, and the floor is negligible, no lateral current reaches the cluster at all. Kirchhoff then gives $h_b \propto w_{ib}$, and the fixed point of (1) on the input synapses gives

$$\begin{gathered}A_{SS}\, h_S = c\, h_S, \qquad c > 0, \qquad h_S > 0, \\ (A h)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S,\end{gathered}$$

where $S$ is the support of $h$ and $c = (h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$ is a member's summed input conductance (with $\lambda = \eta = 1$). A cluster is a non-negative eigenvector of a principal submatrix of $A$: a part. Neither $N$, nor $\gamma$, nor the lateral weights appear; a finite floor adds small corrections. A part is stable if $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ (so $h_S$ is its Perron vector) and the off-support inequality is strict; this test is sufficient, not necessary. In words, a stable part is a local maximum of non-negative PCA of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$: a thresholded Perron vector, localised on a support whose size $\theta$ sets.

Key point

Under the covariance rule a cluster's embedding is the Perron vector of $A_{SS}$, $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, on its support $S$ (derived). It is non-negative and localised; an eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ is neither, in general.

Three inputs, worked out

Independent unit inputs. On a support of size $k$, $A_{SS} = I - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$; the uniform vector has eigenvalue $1 - k\theta$ and the others $1$. For $\theta > 0$ the uniform vector is the top one only if $k = 1$, so for $0 < \theta < 1$ only the corners are stable parts (each with $c = 1 - \theta$); for $\theta \ge 1$ there are none and the network dies. (Here $\Sigma_g = I$, so every vector is an eigenvector: this case cannot tell parts from eigenvectors.)

A correlated pair ($\rho_{12}$, unit variances, $g_3$ independent). On $\{1, 2\}$ the midpoint $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ has eigenvalue $1 + \rho_{12} - 2\theta$ and $(1, -1)$ has $1 - \rho_{12}$, so the midpoint is a stable part exactly when $\theta < \rho_{12}$. In general a corner $g_b$ is a part if and only if $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every other input $a$. With $\rho_{12} = 0.6$ and $\theta = 0.5$, one cluster sits at $g_3$ and one at the midpoint of the $g_1$–$g_2$ edge. The midpoint happens to lie along the top eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$, but the signed eigenvector $(1, -1, 0)$ has no cluster, and the number of clusters is set by $\theta$. Raise $\theta$ above $\rho_{12}$ and the midpoint stops being a part; the corners $g_1$ and $g_2$, which are not eigenvectors of this $\Sigma_g$, become parts instead.

Pruning, and binding

With independent inputs and no pruning, every lateral synapse grows, the feedback closes, and all 90 neurons end on one point near the centroid. With positively correlated inputs the statement is exact: if $\theta$ is below every entry of $\Sigma_g$, then $A$ is entrywise positive, and by Perron–Frobenius the only part is the full-support Perron vector of $A$, so there is exactly one place a cluster can sit. That covers the note's starting point (a regulariser $w_0 - \lambda w$ with $w_0 \ge 0$) for any inputs whose covariances are all positive.

Pruning is necessary but not sufficient. At $\theta = 0.2$ the three corners are the only stable parts, yet the population still becomes one blob and slides as one body to a single corner. A lateral synapse that starts near zero grows if $\gamma\,h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j > \theta$, and for independent random starting embeddings the mean of $h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j$ is $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$ ($\tfrac13$ here). So, from such a start, below $\theta \approx \gamma\kappa_0$ the population binds before any neuron has committed, and a bound population behaves like one neuron. Above it ($\theta = 0.4$) neurons choose independently and every corner is populated. This crossover is a heuristic read off the initial drift, not a derived phase boundary; it moves with the starting weights.

Twelve and sixteen inputs

With more inputs the difference between parts and eigenvectors is plain. Three covariances, each at three values of $\theta$, $N = 400$: 12 inputs in three correlated blocks; 16 in a binary hierarchy; 16 on a ring, where the eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$ are Fourier modes. The input space is now 12- or 16-dimensional, so the film shows a projection: the principal axes of $\Sigma_g^{1/2} h_i$ (before projection, distance between neurons is the RMS difference of their responses; the 2-D view approximates it).

For every cluster the derivation predicts the embedding, exactly in the limit of a negligible floor: the Perron vector of $A_{SS}$ on the cluster's support $S$ (the inputs whose synapses stay above the floor). The figure puts the prediction beside the cluster's actual mean embedding and beside the eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ that comes closest.

The part and the centroid coincide, and the eigenvector does not: on the blocks it spreads over all three blocks with signs; on the hierarchy it is a signed contrast between branches (or, at $\theta = 0.2$, the uniform vector), where the part covers one branch; on the ring it is a sine wave, where the part is a bump, a place field whose width $\theta$ sets.

Over all 30 big runs ($B = 3$ included) and all … clusters with at least 2% of the neurons: the centroid's cosine with its predicted Perron vector is at least …, every cluster passes the top-eigenvalue test, the off-support inequality holds with margin (largest $(Ah)_b$ off the support …), and the members are clones (largest member-to-centroid $L_1$ distance …). The closest eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ reaches a cosine of only …–… with a cluster at $B = 12$–$16$. Where a cluster sits is the derivation's prediction, up to the small corrections a finite floor makes, not a fitted model; what is empirical is which parts are populated.

And that is not fixed by $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$. Below $\theta \approx \kappa_0$ (0.275 for the blocks, 0.26 for the hierarchy) the population binds into one cluster on one part, as on three inputs. Above it only a subset of the stable parts is populated (8 of 12 corners for the blocks at $\theta = 0.6$, 3–6 of 16 bumps on the ring), and the subset can move with the floor (hierarchy at $\theta = 0.6$: 6 clusters at $\omega$, 4 at $\omega/3$) while the Perron fit does not.

Clones and allocation

Members of a cluster are exact copies of one another in every tested condition, so a cluster is one function repeated, not a reservoir of different features; and the number of neurons each part receives is inherited from the start, not set by the parameters (at equilibrium any split among compatible parts is a fixed point, up to the floor). Both, with the attempts to break them, are in the appendix: More on the covariance rule

What the covariance rule does, against the note

  • holdsNeurons separate into a few discrete clusters at a fixed point; $\theta$ sets how large a part's support is, and with it how many parts there are.
  • amendedClusters need pruning: if $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ is entrywise positive there is one place for a cluster; and from a fresh start a population with $\theta$ below roughly $\gamma\kappa_0$ binds into one cluster.
  • amendedClusters sit on parts, Perron vectors of $A$ on a support (derived; every populated cluster of every big run fits its part to cosine $\ge 0.9999$), not on eigenvectors of $\Sigma_g$ (best cosine 0.49–0.87 at $B = 12$–$16$).
  • failsEach cluster as a reservoir of different features: members are clones; and which parts get how many neurons comes from the start (appendix).
