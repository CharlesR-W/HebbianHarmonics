Run Hebb's covariance rule with a pruning threshold $\theta$ on the network of The basics, and the neurons gather into a few groups of nearly identical copies. This page shows where those groups sit, and why: each group sits on a non-negative piece of the input covariance, which in the runs here is not one of its eigenvectors. All inputs here are synthetic.

One caveat first. This is the one part of the story that depends on a modelling choice: the pruning threshold $\theta$ is subtracted from every synapse's growth. With the more standard multiplicative normalisation, the neurons would all go to the top eigenvector of the input covariance instead (see the last section). I kept the subtractive version because it gives the hierarchy below, which seems important.

The theory predicts where the groups go

Take three inputs $g_1, g_2, g_3$ and 60 neurons. Each neuron is a mixture $h_i$ of the inputs, a point in the triangle. We start the neurons at random mixtures, run the rule until the weights stop changing, and compare the end points with the prediction of the next section.

Why: each group sits on a peak of $R_\theta$

The engine changes each synapse as follows, with learning rate and decay set to 1 and every weight kept above a small floor $w_{\min}$:

$$\dot w_{ib} = (\Sigma h_i)_b - \theta - w_{ib}, \qquad \dot w_{ij} = \gamma\, h_i^\top\Sigma h_j - \theta - w_{ij}.$$

The first is a synapse from neuron $i$ to input $b$, the second one between two neurons; $\Sigma$ is the input covariance and $\gamma$, the lateral covariance gain, scales only the covariance term. A mixture sums to one, so the input drive is $(Ah_i)_b$ with $A = \Sigma - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ ($\mathbf 1$ the all-ones vector: every entry of $\Sigma$ lowered by $\theta$). For a mixture $h$ define

$$R_\theta(h) = \frac{h^\top A h}{h^\top h} = z^\top\Sigma z - \theta\,\|z\|_1^2, \qquad z = h/\|h\|_2 .$$

Take one group on its own and let the floor go to zero. At rest it keeps a set $S$ of inputs, and its mixture is an eigenvector of the block $A_{SS}$ whose eigenvalue $c$ is its total input weight (derivation below). The rings are the stable parts: sets $S$ whose block has a simple top eigenvalue $c > 0$ with an all-positive eigenvector, called a Perron vector (after Perron's theorem: a matrix with positive entries always has one), and every other input covarying with it by less than $\theta$. These conditions make $h$ a strict local maximum, a peak, of $R_\theta$ over mixtures. So the idealised groups are local optima of non-negative sparse PCA with an $\ell_1^2$ penalty $\theta$; we do not claim the network as a whole optimises it.

The derivation: one group, floor taken to zero

Let a group of copies have mixture $h$ and total input weight $c = \sum_b w_{ib} > 0$. Kirchhoff's law gives $c\,h_i = w_{iB} + \sum_j w_{ij}(h_j - h_i)$; copies contribute nothing to the sum, and with the floor at zero and no synapses to other groups neither does anyone else, so $w_{iB} = c\,h$. At rest each input weight is $\max(0, (Ah)_b)$. On the inputs $S$ it keeps, and off them:

$$A_{SS}\,h_S = c\,h_S,\quad h_S > 0; \qquad (Ah)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S.$$

These are exactly the first-order (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions for a maximum of $R_\theta$ over $h \ge 0$, with $c = R_\theta(h)$. They are stationarity conditions, not a stability test. A stable part adds a simple top eigenvalue on $S$ and a strict inequality outside, which together certify a strict local maximum; cases with equality outside need a separate test and are not drawn. With a positive floor the outside condition becomes $(Ah)_b \le w_{\min}$, and floor synapses to other groups move a group slightly off its part. This calculation says where one group can sit; whether several groups can coexist, and which of them the dynamics reach, are separate questions (see the ramps below).

$\theta$ cuts the correlation tree

With more inputs, the outside test has a short reading. A stable part's inputs can never be split into two sets that covary with each other by less than $\theta$: the block of $A$ between them is negative, so flipping the sign of $h$ on one set raises the Rayleigh quotient, and $h$ is not the top eigenvector. And a part built from a set whose pairs all covary by more than $\theta$ takes the whole set: a member left out would covary with it by more than $\theta$. For an exact hierarchy of unit-variance inputs, where the covariance of two inputs depends only on how high in a tree they meet, these facts settle it: away from the merge levels, the stable parts are exactly the branches of the tree above the cut at $\theta$.

Groups are parts, not eigenvectors

In networks of 400 neurons on 12 to 16 inputs, each group listens (above the floor) to only some of the inputs, and its mixture matches its predicted part. The eigenvectors of these covariances do not: after the first they take both signs, and the first spreads over every input.

Which parts get a group

A stable part is a place a group can sit; whether a group actually sits there depends on how the network gets there. On 16 hierarchically correlated inputs, we start 300 neurons fresh at each threshold and lateral gain and count the stable parts that end up holding a group.

Raising or lowering θ slowly instead of starting fresh

We ramp $\theta$ in the same network: 80 steps up from 0.03, or 69 steps down from 0.82, integrating towards equilibrium at each step.

Just past a level the parent part has only barely lost stability, and the lateral synapses that bind the copies pull them along together.

So $\theta$ sets the predicted parts for this covariance; the lateral gain, the floor and the history decide which groups the network reaches.

This depends on subtracting $\theta$

The threshold $\theta$ in (1) is subtractive: the same fixed amount is removed from every synapse's growth. That is what gives non-negative parts and the sparse-PCA reading of (2). The alternative scales all of a neuron's synapses down in proportion to hold its total fixed; Miller and MacKay (1994) call this a multiplicative constraint; under their conditions Hebbian learning then converges to the principal eigenvector of the input correlations, and Oja's unit-norm neuron (Oja 1982) is the related principal-component result. Their subtractive constraint, which subtracts whatever amount keeps the total fixed, is a related contrast rather than our rule: it typically drives weights to their bounds, and its outcome is in general not an eigenvector either. The result on this page therefore depends on this modelling choice.

Limits

Everything here is a linear network driven by the exact input covariance, with no sampling noise. The predicted parts are computed for one group with the floor at zero; the floor that keeps the network connected shifts the simulated groups slightly off them (cosine at least 0.9999 in the large runs), and the members of a group are copies only up to that shift. Which stable parts get filled, and by how many neurons, is not fixed by $\Sigma$ and $\theta$: it depends on the random start, on the lateral gain $\gamma$ and, as the ramps show, on history. The tree result needs an exact hierarchy with unit variances and fails at $\theta$ exactly equal to a merge level.
