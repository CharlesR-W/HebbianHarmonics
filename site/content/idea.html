Hebb's rule has no loss function, yet it seems to organise networks. The note behind this page proposes what it organises. Treat the network as a resistor network, and every neuron becomes a probability vector over the inputs, its harmonic embedding. Hebb's rule then becomes a closed dynamical system on those vectors, with clusters at its fixed points, and the weights can be read backwards to recover the input statistics. I built that model, simulated it, and checked each claim. The mechanism holds; the consequences mostly need amending, and one fails.

Neurons as nodes of a resistor network

Neurons are nodes with voltages; synapses are symmetric conductances $w_{ij} \ge 0$. $B$ input neurons are clamped by the rest of the brain to $g = \phi(\text{stimulus})$, a lifted stimulus; the $N$ interior neurons settle wherever Kirchhoff puts them. No current is lost at an interior node, so its voltage is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours', and solving all those averages at once gives

$$\boxed{\,u = H g, \qquad H = L_{II}^{-1} W_{IB}\,}$$

where $L_{II} = \mathrm{diag}(\deg) - W_{II}$ is the interior block of the graph Laplacian and $W_{IB}$ the interior-to-input conductances ($H$ is the Kron reduction, the discrete Poisson kernel).

Row $i$ of $H$ is neuron $i$'s harmonic embedding $h_i$: non-negative, summing to one, and a function of the weights only. It says how much of neuron $i$'s voltage comes from each input. Change one conductance and every embedding downstream of it moves:

Hebb's rule becomes a closed system

Because $u = Hg$, the covariance Hebb's rule needs is an inner product of embeddings, taken in the geometry of the input covariance $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{Cov}(g)$:

$$\boxed{\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g\, h_j\,}$$

and, for a synapse onto input $b$, $\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, g_b) = (H\Sigma_g)_{ib} = h_i^\top\Sigma_g e_b$.

The note's rule, with a regulariser that depends on the weight only, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$, then reads

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} &= w_0 - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta\, h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j \\ &= \eta\, h_i^\top\!\big(\Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\big) h_j - \lambda w_{ij}\end{aligned}}$$

where $\theta = -w_0/\eta$ is a pruning threshold in covariance units, $\lambda$ a decay, $w_{\min}$ a hard floor ($w_{ij}$ is clipped at $w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}$), and lateral (interior–interior) synapses use $\eta_{II} = \gamma\eta$. The second form uses $\mathbf 1^\top h = 1$: a constant drift is the same thing as a constant subtracted from $\Sigma_g$.

The embeddings are functions of the weights, so this is an autonomous ODE on the weights, driven only by $\Sigma_g$. Read in words: a synapse grows when its two neurons' embeddings align (in the $\Sigma_g$ geometry) by more than $\theta$, and is pruned to the floor when they do not. A synapse that survives to a fixed point stores one number about the inputs; on every surviving synapse,

$$\boxed{\,h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j = \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta}\, w_{ij}\,}$$

(with $\gamma$ on the left for lateral synapses, and "$\le$" with $w_{ij} = w_{\min}$ for pruned ones).

Read backwards, that is weight-space interpretability: take the weights, compute $H$, and solve the linear equations (4) for an unknown covariance $C$. The note writes it as $HCH^\top = R$, $C = H^+ R H^{+\top}$.

From here the note conjectures. At a fixed point the embeddings should fall into discrete clusters, one per eigenmode of $g$, their number set by the homeostatic parameters. Each cluster would hold many neurons with the statistics of that mode, a reservoir of random features for downstream use. And the regulariser would act as a spectral filter: neurons allotted to a mode as a function of its energy.

What the note conjectured, and what we found

What we found

  • holdsHebb organises something with no loss. The rule on a resistor network is bounded (voltages are convex combinations of inputs, so no covariance exceeds $\max_b \Sigma_{bb}$) and self-reinforcing (coupled neurons covary more). Every mean-field run converged to a fixed point. watch it
  • holdsEmbeddings separate into discrete clusters, provided something prunes: $\theta > 0$, or negative input covariances, which are the same thing because the network only sees $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. With $\theta$ below every entry of $\Sigma_g$ there is exactly one cluster. pruning
  • amended"Each cluster is an eigenmode of $g$." Clusters sit on non-negative, localized parts: Perron vectors of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ restricted to a subset of inputs (local maxima of non-negative PCA). On block covariances they span the top eigenspace but are its non-negative rotation (best single eigenvector: cosine 0.71–0.74); on a ring they are place fields, not Fourier modes. parts vs eigenvectors
  • amended"The homeostatic parameters set the number of clusters." Two knobs with different jobs. $\theta$ decides which parts exist. The lateral gain $\gamma$ decides how many of them a densely connected population resolves: if $\theta < \gamma\kappa_0$, where $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$ is the mean covariance of two random starting neurons, lateral synapses grow first and the population lands on one part as a single blob; this repeats coarse to fine. $\lambda$ only sets the scale. the phase diagram
  • amended"A spectral filter: neurons per mode as a function of its energy." It exists, but it belongs to the transient: any allocation is a fixed point. With independent neurons, $n_k \propto \lambda_k^{\beta}$, where $\lambda_k$ is the top eigenvalue of $\Sigma_g$ on the part's support and $\beta$ falls from about 7 to 1.5 as $\theta$ rises; parts that cannot beat $\theta$ get nothing. Below the collective threshold it is winner-take-all. Not proportional. allocation
  • fails"An expansion code: many random features per mode." With dense wiring, the members of a cluster are exact clones ($L_1 < 10^{-5}$ for every $w_{\min}$, $\gamma$, $N$ and batch size tried): $n$-fold redundancy, not a reservoir. Sparse random wiring without lateral plasticity gives a few distinct functions per mode, bounded by the mode's dimension. diversity
  • amended"Solve the weights for $\mathrm{Cov}(g)$." The equations are right, but at equilibrium they only pin down the populated parts: a non-negative rank-$K$ sketch $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k h_k h_k^\top/\lVert h_k\rVert^2$, with everything below threshold lost. The readout is better mid-training, or with sparse wiring. the readout
  • open"With a rich kernel lift, the covariance gives $p(\text{stimulus})$." Tested on MNIST; results pending. real data
  • open"Hebbian learning can only learn second moments." True for the linear circuit by construction. Whether a rectified output changes what the rule finds is the subject of a separate chapter; results pending. rectified neurons
  • open"We didn't include noise, but surely one could." Noise shared by all neurons (minibatches) cannot split clones and pushes allocation towards winner-take-all. Private per-synapse jitter breaks the symmetry and makes the $\gamma = 0$ hysteresis loops close; its effect on allocation is pending. noise

Start with three inputs

The maths, for the careful reader

Why each $h_i$ is a probability vector

Kirchhoff's current law at interior node $i$ is $\sum_j w_{ij}(u_j - u_i) + \sum_b w_{ib}(g_b - u_i) = 0$, i.e. $L_{II}u = W_{IB}g$. With every potential synapse at least $w_{\min} > 0$, $L_{II}$ is a non-singular M-matrix: its inverse is entrywise non-negative, so $H = L_{II}^{-1}W_{IB} \ge 0$. If every input is held at the same voltage $c$, $u = c\mathbf 1$ solves the equations, so $H\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$. That is the discrete maximum principle: an interior voltage is a convex combination of the clamped ones, never outside their range.

Probabilistically, $h_{ib}$ is the chance that a random walk started at $i$, stepping to a neighbour with probability proportional to conductance, first hits the inputs at $b$ (harmonic measure). So $u_i = \mathbb E[g_{\text{exit}}]$, and $h_i$ depends on the weights only.

The θ-shift identity: drift and anticorrelation are one mechanism

Since $\mathbf 1^\top h_i = 1$ for every $i$ (and $e_b$ for every input),

$$h_i^\top\big(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\big)h_j = h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \theta .$$

So the constant drift $w_0 = -\eta\theta$ is indistinguishable from subtracting $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ from the input covariance; the input synapses see $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ and nothing else. With $\gamma = 1$, the pairs $(\Sigma_g, \theta)$ and $(\Sigma_g + c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \theta + c)$ give identical trajectories (lateral synapses see $\gamma\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, so with $\gamma \ne 1$ the equivalence is only approximate). Three consequences: pruning by a negative drift and pruning by anticorrelated inputs are the same mechanism, $\theta$ measured from the level the inputs share; $\theta \le 0$ gives one cluster only when $\Sigma_g$ has no entry below $\theta$; and $\theta$ cannot be read off the weights, because it is a constant added to $\Sigma_g$.

The fixed-point "part" equation and its stability

Take a cluster of neurons sharing one embedding $h$ with support $S$. Its members are at the same voltage for every stimulus, so lateral synapses inside it carry no current; synapses to other clusters sit at the floor. Up to $O(Nw_{\min})$, Kirchhoff then gives $h_b = w_{ib}/\sum_c w_{ic}$, and the fixed point of the input synapses gives $w_{ib} = \tfrac{\eta}{\lambda}(Ah)_b$ where positive. Together ($w_{\min} \to 0$):

$$A_{SS}\,h_S = c\,h_S,\qquad c > 0,\quad h_S > 0,\qquad (Ah)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S,$$

with $c = \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta}\sum_b w_{ib}$, a member's summed input conductance, equal to $(h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$. $N$, $\gamma$ and the lateral weights drop out. So a cluster sits at a non-negative eigenvector of $A$ on some support: a part.

Stability. For one neuron on $S$, with $s = \mathbf 1^\top w$ and $h = w/s$, $\dot w = \eta(\Sigma_{SS}w/s - \theta\mathbf 1) - \lambda w$; at the fixed point $s = \eta c/\lambda$, and the Jacobian is $\tfrac{\lambda}{c}A_{SS}(I - h\mathbf 1^\top) - \lambda I$. In $A_{SS}$'s eigenbasis $A_{SS} - c\,h\mathbf 1^\top$ is triangular with diagonal $(0, \mu_2, \dots)$, so the eigenvalues are $-\lambda$ and $\lambda(\mu_k/c - 1)$. Off the support, $w_b$ grows at rate $\eta(Ah)_b$. Hence a part is stable iff $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ (so $h_S$ is its Perron vector) and $(Ah)_b < 0$ strictly off $S$, equivalently iff $h$ is a strict local maximum of the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h / h^\top h$ over non-negative $h$. A whole cluster's collective mode obeys the same equation. Two corollaries: if $\theta < \min_{bc}\Sigma_{bc}$, $A$ is positive and there is exactly one part (Perron–Frobenius); for a uniform block of correlation $\rho$ inside a background $\rho_a$, the block is a stable part iff $\rho_a < \theta < \rho$, which is exact correlation clustering.

Why any allocation is a fixed point

The part equation above does not contain $n_k$, the number of neurons in cluster $k$. Its only dependence on the allocation is through the floor: every clipped synapse still conducts $w_{\min}$, and the exact cluster equation is $h_k\big(\lVert w_{kB}\rVert_1 + w_{\min}(N - n_k)\big) = w_{kB} + w_{\min}\sum_{l\ne k} n_l h_l$, a pull towards the population mean of relative size $\omega/c$ with $\omega = Nw_{\min}\lambda/\eta$. So for small $\omega$, any split of the $N$ neurons among compatible stable parts (cross-covariances below the lateral threshold, so the synapses between them stay clipped) is a fixed point. The same holds for any weight-only regulariser (then $w = f(\mathrm{cov})$, so $h \propto f(\Sigma_g h)$) and for per-neuron synaptic scaling ($h$ is invariant to scaling a neuron's weights). Which allocation you get is inherited from the transient: the initial weights, lateral gating and noise. In the sweeps, seeds converged to different allocations with residuals below $10^{-5}$ (e.g. 34/30/26 and 35/28/27 neurons on three corners).
