Hebb's rule has no loss function, yet it seems to organise networks. The note behind this page says what it organises: in a resistor network every neuron becomes a probability vector over the inputs, its harmonic embedding, and Hebb's rule becomes a closed dynamical system on those vectors, with clusters at its fixed points and weights that can be read backwards for the input statistics. I built that model and checked each claim. The mechanism holds; the consequences mostly need amending, and one fails.

Neurons as nodes of a resistor network

Neurons are nodes with voltages; synapses are symmetric conductances $w_{ij} \ge 0$. $B$ input neurons are clamped by the rest of the brain to $g = \phi(\text{stimulus})$, a lifted stimulus; the $N$ interior neurons settle wherever Kirchhoff puts them. No current is lost at an interior node, so its voltage is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours', and solving all those averages at once gives

$$\boxed{\,u = H g, \qquad H = L_{II}^{-1} W_{IB}\,}$$

where $L_{II} = \mathrm{diag}(\deg) - W_{II}$ is the interior block of the graph Laplacian and $W_{IB}$ the interior-to-input conductances ($H$ is the Kron reduction, the discrete Poisson kernel).

Row $i$ of $H$ is neuron $i$'s harmonic embedding $h_i$: non-negative, summing to one, and a function of the weights only. It says how much of neuron $i$'s voltage comes from each input. Change one conductance and every embedding downstream of it moves:

Hebb's rule becomes a closed system

Because $u = Hg$, the covariance Hebb's rule needs is an inner product of embeddings, taken in the geometry of the input covariance $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{Cov}(g)$:

$$\boxed{\,\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g\, h_j\,}$$

and, for a synapse onto input $b$, $\mathrm{Cov}(u_i, g_b) = (H\Sigma_g)_{ib} = h_i^\top\Sigma_g e_b$.

The note's rule, with a regulariser that depends on the weight only, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$, then reads

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} &= w_0 - \lambda w_{ij} + \eta\, h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j \\ &= \eta\, h_i^\top\!\big(\Sigma_g - \theta\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\big) h_j - \lambda w_{ij}\end{aligned}}$$

where $\theta = -w_0/\eta$ is a pruning threshold in covariance units, $\lambda$ a decay, $w_{\min}$ a hard floor ($w_{ij}$ is clipped at $w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}$), and lateral (interior–interior) synapses use $\eta_{II} = \gamma\eta$. The second form uses $\mathbf 1^\top h = 1$: a constant drift is the same thing as a constant subtracted from $\Sigma_g$.

The embeddings are functions of the weights, so this is an autonomous ODE on the weights, driven only by $\Sigma_g$. A synapse is pulled towards $\tfrac{\eta}{\lambda}(h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \theta)$ when its two neurons' embeddings align by more than $\theta$, and pruned to the floor when they do not; the target moves, because every change of weight moves the embeddings. At a fixed point every surviving synapse stores one number about the inputs:

$$\boxed{\,h_i^\top \Sigma_g h_j = \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta}\, w_{ij}\,}$$

(with $\gamma$ on the left for lateral synapses, and "$\le$" with $w_{ij} = w_{\min}$ for pruned ones).

Read backwards, that is weight-space interpretability: take the weights, compute $H$, and solve the linear equations (4) for an unknown covariance $C$. The note writes it as $HCH^\top = R$, $C = H^+ R H^{+\top}$.

Try it: one synapse's drift, from embeddings to weight change

From here the note conjectures. At a fixed point the embeddings should fall into discrete clusters, one per eigenmode of $g$, their number set by the homeostatic parameters. Each cluster would hold many neurons with the statistics of that mode, a reservoir of random features for downstream use. And the regulariser would act as a spectral filter: neurons allotted to a mode as a function of its energy.

Samples drawn from the read-back covariance are the mean digit plus blobs, not recognisable digits. real data
The verdicts in more detail
  • Pruning. Voltages are convex combinations of the inputs, so no covariance exceeds $\max_b \Sigma_{bb}$. A positive $\theta$ and negative covariances are the same mechanism because the network only sees $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. Parts are Perron vectors of that matrix on a subset of inputs.
  • Thresholds. With $\theta$ below every entry of $\Sigma_g$ there is exactly one part, hence one cluster. $\kappa_0 = \mathbf 1^\top\Sigma_g\mathbf 1/B^2$ is the mean covariance of two random starting neurons, so $\gamma\kappa_0$ is where the expected initial lateral drift changes sign. It predicted every tested transition within 0.03–0.08, coarse to fine, but it is not a derived phase boundary. $\lambda$ only sets the scale.
  • Allocation without noise. Any split among compatible parts is a fixed point (zero-floor limit; a finite floor adds a weak dependence), so the transient decides; it is never proportional. $\lambda_k$ is the top eigenvalue of $\Sigma_g$ on the part's support; $\beta$ falls as $\theta$ rises (independent neurons, fits on the tested families). Parts that cannot beat $\theta$ get nothing; a bound population is winner-take-all.
  • Private noise. It gives jitter, not diversity. Multiplicative noise with a small floor never lets pruned synapses regrow, so there the allocation stays inherited. With lateral plasticity, noise ends in one bound cluster on the loudest part.
  • Clones. Swapping symmetry only keeps identical neurons identical; that members become identical is empirical (spread $< 10^{-5}$ in every tested condition). Sparse random wiring without lateral plasticity gives a few distinct functions per mode; private noise only jitters them.
  • Readout. The parts-sum $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + \sum_k c_k h_k h_k^\top/\lVert h_k\rVert^2$ satisfies the active-edge equations for disjoint zero-floor parts. Reading the weights mid-training does better than at equilibrium.
  • Rectified output. A Gaussian control with the digits' covariance removes the difference, so it comes from moments beyond the second; for Gaussian inputs rectification changes the fixed points but adds no information. Stacking layers was not run: on digits the rectified population is one effective function, though on a synthetic ring it keeps 7–9 parts populated where the linear rule keeps 1, so depth is still an open question.

Start with three inputs

The maths, for the careful reader

Why each $h_i$ is a probability vector

Kirchhoff's current law at interior node $i$ is $\sum_j w_{ij}(u_j - u_i) + \sum_b w_{ib}(g_b - u_i) = 0$, i.e. $L_{II}u = W_{IB}g$. With every potential synapse at least $w_{\min} > 0$, $L_{II}$ is a non-singular M-matrix: its inverse is entrywise non-negative, so $H = L_{II}^{-1}W_{IB} \ge 0$. If every input is held at the same voltage $c$, $u = c\mathbf 1$ solves the equations, so $H\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$. That is the discrete maximum principle: an interior voltage is a convex combination of the clamped ones, never outside their range.

Probabilistically, $h_{ib}$ is the chance that a random walk started at $i$, stepping to a neighbour with probability proportional to conductance, first hits the inputs at $b$ (harmonic measure). So $u_i = \mathbb E[g_{\text{exit}}]$, and $h_i$ depends on the weights only.

The θ-shift identity: drift and anticorrelation are one mechanism

Since $\mathbf 1^\top h_i = 1$ for every $i$ (and $e_b$ for every input),

$$h_i^\top\big(\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\big)h_j = h_i^\top\Sigma_g h_j - \theta .$$

So the constant drift $w_0 = -\eta\theta$ is indistinguishable from subtracting $\theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ from the input covariance; the input synapses see $A = \Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$ and nothing else. With $\gamma = 1$, the pairs $(\Sigma_g, \theta)$ and $(\Sigma_g + c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top, \theta + c)$ give identical trajectories (lateral synapses see $\gamma\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, so with $\gamma \ne 1$ the equivalence is only approximate). Three consequences: pruning by a negative drift and pruning by anticorrelated inputs are the same mechanism, $\theta$ measured from the level the inputs share; $\theta \le 0$ guarantees one cluster only when $\Sigma_g$ has no entry below $\theta$; and $\theta$ cannot be read off the weights, because it is a constant added to $\Sigma_g$.

The fixed-point "part" equation and its stability

Take a cluster of neurons sharing one embedding $h$ with support $S$. Its members are at the same voltage for every stimulus, so lateral synapses inside it carry no current; synapses to other clusters sit at the floor. Up to $O(Nw_{\min})$, Kirchhoff then gives $h_b = w_{ib}/\sum_c w_{ic}$, and the fixed point of the input synapses gives $w_{ib} = \tfrac{\eta}{\lambda}(Ah)_b$ where positive. Together ($w_{\min} \to 0$):

$$A_{SS}\,h_S = c\,h_S,\qquad c > 0,\quad h_S > 0,\qquad (Ah)_b \le 0 \ \text{ for } b \notin S,$$

with $c = \tfrac{\lambda}{\eta}\sum_b w_{ib}$, a member's summed input conductance, equal to $(h^\top\Sigma_g h - \theta)/\lVert h\rVert^2$. $N$, $\gamma$ and the lateral weights drop out. So a cluster sits at a non-negative eigenvector of $A$ on some support: a part.

Stability. For one neuron on $S$, with $s = \mathbf 1^\top w$ and $h = w/s$, $\dot w = \eta(\Sigma_{SS}w/s - \theta\mathbf 1) - \lambda w$; at the fixed point $s = \eta c/\lambda$, and the Jacobian is $\tfrac{\lambda}{c}A_{SS}(I - h\mathbf 1^\top) - \lambda I$. In $A_{SS}$'s eigenbasis $A_{SS} - c\,h\mathbf 1^\top$ is triangular with diagonal $(0, \mu_2, \dots)$, so the eigenvalues are $-\lambda$ and $\lambda(\mu_k/c - 1)$. Off the support, $w_b$ grows at rate $\eta(Ah)_b$. Hence a part is stable if $c$ is strictly the top eigenvalue of $A_{SS}$ (so $h_S$ is its Perron vector) and $(Ah)_b < 0$ strictly off $S$; $h$ is then a strict local maximum of the Rayleigh quotient $h^\top A h / h^\top h$ over non-negative $h$. This test is sufficient (the nondegenerate case), not necessary: with $\Sigma_g = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1)$ and $\theta = 0$ the dominant vertex is stable although its off-support margin is zero. A whole cluster's collective mode obeys the same equation. Corollaries: if $\theta < \min_{bc}\Sigma_{bc}$, $A$ is positive and there is exactly one part (Perron–Frobenius); a single input $b$ is a part iff $\Sigma_{bb} > \theta$ and $\Sigma_{ab} \le \theta$ for every $a \ne b$; for a uniform block of correlation $\rho$ inside a background $\rho_a$, the block is a stable part iff $\rho_a < \theta < \rho$, which is exact correlation clustering.

Why any split among compatible parts is a fixed point

The part equation above does not contain $n_k$, the number of neurons in cluster $k$. Its only dependence on the allocation is through the floor: every clipped synapse still conducts $w_{\min}$, and the exact cluster equation is $h_k\big(\lVert w_{kB}\rVert_1 + w_{\min}(N - n_k)\big) = w_{kB} + w_{\min}\sum_{l\ne k} n_l h_l$, a pull towards the population mean of relative size $\omega/c$ with $\omega = Nw_{\min}\lambda/\eta$. So in the zero-floor limit, any split of the $N$ neurons among mutually compatible stable parts (cross-covariances below the lateral threshold, so the synapses between them stay clipped and the clusters are disconnected) is a fixed point. A finite floor couples the clusters, so the equilibria depend weakly, at $O(\omega/c)$, on the allocation. The same holds for any weight-only regulariser (then $w = f(\mathrm{cov})$, so $h \propto f(\Sigma_g h)$) and for per-neuron synaptic scaling ($h$ is invariant to scaling a neuron's weights). Without private noise, which allocation you get is inherited from the transient: the initial weights, lateral gating and shared noise. In the sweeps, seeds converged to different allocations with residuals below $10^{-5}$ (e.g. 34/30/26 and 35/28/27 neurons on three corners).
