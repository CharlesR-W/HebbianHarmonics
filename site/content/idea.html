Hebbian learning, "neurons that fire together wire together", changes each synapse using only what the two neurons it joins are doing. No global loss function is in sight. The note this page grew from asks how that can work, and what the network is then doing. Its answer starts from one change of variables, the harmonic embedding: in a linear resistor network every neuron is a fixed mixture of the inputs, and Hebb's rule acts on those mixtures. This chapter sets up the model, the embedding and the two learning rules the rest of the page uses, and ends with what we found.

The question

The note begins: "It's remarkable that this works because there is no global loss function. How then, or what, is the brain doing?" Its proposal is a resistor network. Neurons are nodes with voltages, synapses are conductances, and a set of input neurons is clamped to the activity of the rest of the brain. The Hebb rule, with a regulariser on each weight, should then sort the other neurons into discrete clusters, each tied to an eigenmode of the input, and one should be able to read the input statistics back out of the weights. I take the cleanest case first, linear neurons, and add nonlinear neurons at the end of the main path.

The linear resistor network

$B$ input neurons are clamped to a stimulus $g \in \mathbb R^B$, drawn from a distribution with zero mean and covariance $\Sigma_g$. $N$ interior neurons have voltages $u_i$. Interior neurons are joined to one another by symmetric conductances $w_{ij} = w_{ji} \ge 0$, and to the inputs by conductances $a_{ib} \ge 0$. The network's energy, counting each pair once, is

$$E_W(u; g) = \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\,(u_i - u_j)^2 + \tfrac12 \sum_{i,b} a_{ib}\,(u_i - g_b)^2 .$$

For a given stimulus the voltages relax quickly, $\tau\,\dot u_i = -\partial E_W/\partial u_i$, to the unique minimum of $E_W$ (unique once every neuron is connected to some input). There each neuron sits at the conductance-weighted mean of its neighbours (Kirchhoff's current law),

$$d_i\,u_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\,u_j + \sum_b a_{ib}\,g_b, \qquad d_i = \sum_j w_{ij} + \sum_b a_{ib}.$$

The harmonic embedding

The node equations are linear, so the voltages are a linear function of the inputs,

$$u = H g, \qquad H = \big(\mathrm{diag}(d) - W\big)^{-1} A ,$$

with $A = (a_{ib})$. $H$ is the discrete Poisson kernel of the graph (the Kron reduction of its Laplacian onto the inputs). Each row $h_i$ is non-negative and sums to one, since constant inputs give constant voltages. So $h_i$ is a probability vector over the inputs, the neuron's harmonic embedding: a point in the simplex whose corners are the inputs. It depends on the weights alone, and it is all there is to know about what neuron $i$ computes: $u_i = h_i \cdot g$.

Two pictures of the same network are therefore worth keeping apart. In neuron space a neuron is a node in a wiring diagram; on this page those diagrams put nodes at random (seeded) positions, because where a neuron is says nothing about what it does. In input space a neuron is the point $h_i$. The figure shows both, for the same eight neurons; hover one and it is ringed in the other panel.

At $W = 0$ each neuron's embedding is just its attachment shares (the hollow rings). Take the strong-fusion tour: synapses grow between neurons that respond alike, each synapse pulls its two neurons' embeddings towards each other, and the points in the triangle travel to one spot while the wiring diagram, by construction, stays where it was. Learning is something that happens in input space. (The default settings make weaker synapses, so the embeddings move only a little.)

The embedding turns statistics of the voltages into geometry of the embeddings. The covariance of two neurons is an inner product of their embeddings, weighted by the input covariance:

$$\Sigma_u = H\,\Sigma_g\,H^\top, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = h_i^\top \Sigma_g\, h_j = \langle h_i, h_j\rangle_{\Sigma_g}.$$

So a Hebbian rule, which only sees coactivity, is a rule on embeddings.

Two Hebbian rules

The note's covariance rule

The note's rule grows a synapse with the covariance of the two voltages and adds a regulariser that depends on the weight only, $\rho(w) = w_0 - \lambda w$:

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = w_0 - \lambda\,w_{ij} + \eta\,\langle h_i, h_j\rangle_{\Sigma_g}, \qquad w_{ij} \ge w_{\min}.$$

Here every synapse is plastic, the ones onto the inputs too (a synapse onto input $b$ sees $(H\Sigma_g)_{ib}$). Writing $w_0 = -\eta\theta$, a synapse grows only where its two neurons covary by more than a threshold $\theta$, and otherwise decays to a small floor. In the note's words, "we cut the connection between two neurons to the extent that their harmonic embeddings misalign". The note expected clusters, one per eigenmode of the input. Clusters do form, but they sit on something else: non-negative Perron vectors of principal submatrices of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, which Perron parts derives and tests.

The distance rule: a local rule that descends a global objective

The second rule answers the note's opening puzzle directly. Keep the input conductances fixed (they are the attachments $a_{ib}$ from now on), and let the lateral synapses descend

$$J(W) = \mathbb E_g\Big[\min_u E_W(u; g)\Big] + \sum_{i<j}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\,w_{ij}^2 - b\,w_{ij}\Big),$$

the energy the network settles to, averaged over stimuli, plus a cost per synapse (a decay $\alpha$ and a constant drive $b$). The derivative of a minimum with respect to a parameter is the partial derivative at the minimiser: the minimiser moves too, but the gradient in $u$ vanishes there, so its motion contributes nothing to first order (the envelope theorem). $E_W$ depends on $w_{ij}$ only through $\tfrac12 w_{ij}(u_i - u_j)^2$, so gradient descent on $J$ is

$$\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = b - \alpha\,w_{ij} - \tfrac12 D_{ij}^2, \qquad D_{ij}^2 = \mathbb E_g\big[(u_i - u_j)^2\big] = \lVert h_i - h_j\rVert^2_{\Sigma_g},$$

projected so that $w_{ij}$ stays non-negative. Panel (c) of the figure checks the envelope step at every $W$, not only at fixed points.

Key point

The rule is local: a synapse needs only the mean squared voltage difference across itself, which it can sense. Yet the network as a whole descends one objective, $J$. There is a loss function after all; no part of the network computes it, because it is the network's own equilibrium energy.

A close cousin of the covariance rule. Expanding the square,

$$-\tfrac12 D_{ij}^2 = \underbrace{\langle h_i, h_j\rangle_{\Sigma_g}}_{\text{coactivity}} - \tfrac12\,\langle h_i, h_i\rangle_{\Sigma_g} - \tfrac12\,\langle h_j, h_j\rangle_{\Sigma_g}.$$

The first term is the covariance rule's Hebbian term. The other two subtract each neuron's activity power, so a pair is drawn together by being alike, not by being loud.

The fixed point. Setting (7) to zero, with the projection,

$$w_{ij}^\ast = \frac{\big(b - D_{ij}^2/2\big)_+}{\alpha}.$$

An edge lives exactly when $D_{ij}^2 < 2b$, and its weight is then a known linear function of a distance between embeddings. Pruning is built in; $b$ sets the range of fusion and $\alpha$ its strength. This is not a formula for $W$, since $D_{ij}$ itself depends on $W$, and several fixed points can coexist (the fusion appendix works this out). But it is what makes the weights readable: every live synapse is one linear equation on the input second moment, and Reading the weights, which works with this rule, solves those equations. Which rule where, and what differs

The covariance rule (5) is the note's, and it is the rule of Perron parts and of the appendix More on the covariance rule; there every synapse, input synapses included, is plastic, and the floor $w_{\min}$ matters. The distance rule (7) is the rule of Reading the weights, Nonlinear neurons and the appendices Fusion, Many inputs, Diversity and Allocation; there only lateral synapses learn and the attachments are fixed. The distance rule uses the uncentred second moment $C = \mathbb E[gg^\top]$, which equals $\Sigma_g$ for zero-mean inputs; in simulations the expectation is the mean over a fixed training bank of stimuli, so the identities above hold exactly for that bank's moment $\hat C$.

What we found

What we found

  • holdsA local rule with a global objective. The distance rule is exactly gradient descent on the network's averaged equilibrium energy plus a weight cost, and it contains Hebb's coactivity term. Checked by finite differences in the figure above.
  • amendedClusters, but not eigenmodes: Perron parts. Under the covariance rule each cluster sits on a non-negative Perron vector of a principal submatrix of $\Sigma_g - \theta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$, with $\theta$ setting the support size. In precomputed runs with $N = 300$–$400$ neurons and up to 16 inputs, every cluster holding at least 2% of the neurons matched its predicted part to cosine $\ge 0.9999$, while with 12–16 correlated inputs the best eigenvector of $\Sigma_g$ reached only 0.49–0.87 (with 3 independent inputs $\Sigma_g = I$, and every vector is an eigenvector). Members of a cluster are copies of each other. Which of the possible parts get populated is not fixed by $\Sigma_g$ and $\theta$: it moved with the starting state and with the floor. Perron parts
  • holdsReading the input statistics from the weights, for linear neurons under the distance rule, with a precise scope: for stationary noiseless weights, known attachments and rule parameters, and a full-rank, well-conditioned live-edge operator, the weights identify the training bank's contrast second moment $P\hat C P$ exactly. More neurons than inputs is what makes the operator full rank: with 12 inputs, full rank arrived at $N = 40$, $80$ or $320$ for $b = 0.1$, $0.036$ or $0.01$. Only contrasts are visible, the moment is the bank's, and a second moment is not the input distribution. Reading the weights
  • amendedNonlinear neurons keep the geometry: the rule and its fixed point carry over to response functions, live synapses still state exact response distances, and dead pairs give lower bounds. They add a little diversity within a cluster (about 1–2% of member variance in compact clusters with spread thresholds, against 0.1–0.3% for linear neurons), not the expansion code the note hoped for, and fusion makes such neurons more linear. Nonlinear neurons
  • failsAn allocation law $n(\lambda)$, neurons per input mode set by its energy, did not emerge in the basic model or with plastic attachments. Allocation

So the model explains the first half of the note: a local, Hebbian rule that groups neurons by their harmonic embeddings, and weights from which the contrast part of the input statistics can be read back. The second half, an expansion code with a spectral allocation law, needs ingredients the basic model does not have.
